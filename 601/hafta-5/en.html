Last week: the oscillator ladder and Dirac notation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceThe oscillator energies are evenly spaced, \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)\), and the ground state is a Gaussian with the smallest possible uncertainty. In Dirac notation a state is a ket, the bra is its dagger, and the inner product is a complex number that gets conjugated when you swap its sides.

The lecture opened with a two board summary of week 4. The first item is the eigenvalue problem of the oscillator.

\[ E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\tfrac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots \]

The gap between two neighbouring levels is always \(\hbar\omega\). The board calls them “evenly separated levels”. The lowest level is not zero but \(\hbar\omega/2\), the zero point energy. The energy eigenstates are

\[ u_n(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n\,n!\,\sqrt\pi\,x_0}}\;H_n\!\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right)e^{-x^2/2x_0^2},\qquad x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}u_n(x)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n\,n!\,\sqrt\pi\,x_0}}\;H_n\!\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right)e^{-x^2/2x_0^2}\\[2pt]x_0&=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}\end{aligned} \]

\(x_0\) is the natural length scale of the oscillator. \(H_n\) is the Hermite polynomial of degree n and it gives the wave function n nodes. The factor under the square root is the normalisation, \(\int|u_n|^2dx=1\). The ground state with n = 0 is a pure Gaussian and it meets the uncertainty relation with equality, \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\). That is what the line “For the ground state” means. No state is less uncertain than this one.

The second item is Dirac notation. A state is a vector in Hilbert space, the ket \(|\psi\rangle\). A Hilbert space is a complex vector space with an inner product that is also complete. The bra is the dagger of the ket. You turn the column into a row and conjugate every entry. A bra next to a ket gives the inner product.

\[ \langle\psi|=\big(|\psi\rangle\big)^\dagger,\qquad\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\in\mathbb C,\qquad\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^* \]

The last identity is the “conjugate or hermitian sym. prop.” on the board. If you swap the order you take the complex conjugate. One consequence follows at once. \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^*\), so the inner product of a ket with itself is real and never negative. You can read it as the squared length.

Board note

The Hermite polynomial in the formula for \(u_n\) is written as \(H_0\). It should be \(H_n\). With \(H_0=1\) every n would give the same Gaussian with no nodes, so the states would not differ at all. The normalisation would fail too. For n = 1 with \(H_0\) you get \(\int|u_1|^2dx=1/2\). For reference \(H_0=1\), \(H_1(\xi)=2\xi\) and \(H_2(\xi)=4\xi^2-2\).

High school example

Picture a building whose floors are all the same height, ħω apart. Even the ground floor sits a little above the street, at ħω/2. The inner product is also familiar. It is the dot product of two vectors. The one difference is that complex numbers need the conjugate. Without it the vector (1, i) would have squared length \(1^2+i^2=0\), which is absurd for a non zero vector. With the conjugate you get \(|1|^2+|i|^2=2\).

Explain it in your own words, then open and compare. Why does the order matter in an inner product?

Because the bra is the conjugated ket. \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\sum_n d_n^*c_n\). Swapping the sides gives \(\sum_n c_n^*d_n\), which is the complex conjugate of the first. With real vectors you see no difference. With complex vectors the sign of the imaginary part flips. When you pair a ket with itself the order cannot matter, so the result is real.

Complete orthonormal basis, expansion and projection

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceIn a complete orthonormal basis the identity operator can be written as \(\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|\). Putting it in front of a ket produces the coefficients \(c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle\). \(|n\rangle\langle n|\) is a projection operator, and an observable is a sum of projectors weighted by its eigenvalues.

Orthonormal means \(\langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\). The word “discrete” on the board says the basis is countable and δ is the Kronecker delta. Continuous bases bring the Dirac delta in week 6. Complete means every ket in the space can be expanded in this basis. The operator form of completeness is the resolution of the identity.

\[ \sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat I \]

An expansion is nothing more than putting this identity in front of a ket. The bracket on the right of the board shows the three steps.

\[ |\psi\rangle=\hat I|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle,\qquad c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle \]

For a normalised ket the squared moduli of the coefficients add up to 1.

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\langle m|n\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2=1 \]

This is the “req. probabilistic description” on the board. \(|c_n|^2\) is the probability of outcome n and the probabilities have to add up to 1.

The projection operator \(\hat P_n=|n\rangle\langle n|\) keeps only the part of a ket that lies along \(|n\rangle\), so \(\hat P_n|\psi\rangle=c_n|n\rangle\). It has two properties.

\[ \hat P_n^2=|n\rangle\langle n|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat P_n,\qquad\hat P_n^\dagger=\big(|n\rangle\langle n|\big)^\dagger=|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat P_n \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat P_n^2&=|n\rangle\langle n|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat P_n\\[2pt]\hat P_n^\dagger&=\big(|n\rangle\langle n|\big)^\dagger=|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat P_n\end{aligned} \]

The first says projecting a second time changes nothing, because \(\langle n|n\rangle=1\) sits in the middle. The second says a projector is Hermitian. The last line on the board leads into this week. An observable is a weighted sum of projectors in its own eigenbasis.

\[ \hat A=\sum_na_n\hat P_n=\sum_na_n|n\rangle\langle n| \]

The weights \(a_n\) are the possible measurement results, the eigenvalues. On Board 9 we will see that the matrix of this expression is diagonal.

High school example

Think of the shadows a pencil on the table casts on the floor and on the wall. The shadow of a shadow is the same shadow, which is \(\hat P^2=\hat P\). Add up the shadows along all mutually perpendicular directions and you get the pencil back. The resolution of the identity says the sum of all projections is the object itself.

Exam pattern. “Show that \(\sum_n|c_n|^2=1\) for a normalised ket”

1. Write the expansions of the ket and the bra, \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle\) and \(\langle\psi|=\sum_mc_m^*\langle m|\). Do not forget the conjugate in the bra.

2. Multiply them, \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\langle m|n\rangle\).

3. Orthonormality \(\langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\) collapses the double sum to \(\sum_n|c_n|^2\).

4. For a normalised ket the left side is 1. A common mistake is writing \(c_n^2\). The square of a complex number is not a probability.

6.1 Linear operators, from operator to matrix

Once a basis is chosen everything becomes numbers. A ket becomes a column, a bra a row and an operator a square table. Four boards build this step by step and end by turning an abstract equation into a matrix product.

Board 3174744

6.1 A linear operator and inserting two identities

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceA linear operator respects superposition, so knowing what it does to the basis kets is enough to know what it does to every ket. Inserting the identity on both sides of \(\hat A\) gives \(\hat A=\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle A_{mn}\langle n|\), and \(A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle\) are the entries of a square matrix.

Chapter 6 is titled “Operators, Matrices, and Changes of Basis”. The first definition is linearity.

\[ \hat A\big(\alpha|\psi\rangle+\beta|\phi\rangle\big)=\alpha\hat A|\psi\rangle+\beta\hat A|\phi\rangle,\qquad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C \]

Linear operators fit the superposition principle. Acting on the sum of two states is the same as acting on each one and then adding. A non linear example is \(\psi\mapsto|\psi|^2\psi\). The basic operators of quantum mechanics, position, momentum and the Hamiltonian, are not like that.

Once a basis is chosen every linear operator turns into a matrix. The trick is to use the identity twice. The board writes \(\hat A=\hat I\hat A\hat I\).

\[ \hat A=\hat I\hat A\hat I=\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|\;\hat A\;\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|=\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle\underbrace{\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle}_{A_{mn}}\langle n| \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat A&=\hat I\hat A\hat I=\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|\;\hat A\;\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|\\&=\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle\underbrace{\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle}_{A_{mn}}\langle n|\end{aligned} \]

The middle piece \(\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle\) is a number, the inner product of the bra \(\langle m|\) with the ket \(\hat A|n\rangle\). We call it \(A_{mn}\), with m the row and n the column. In an N dimensional space there are N² of them, an N×N square matrix. The operator itself is built from these numbers and the elementary tables \(|m\rangle\langle n|\).

How to read it. \(A_{mn}\) is the m-th component of the ket \(\hat A|n\rangle\). So the n-th column of the matrix lists the coordinates of where \(\hat A\) sends the n-th basis ket.

\[ \hat A|n\rangle=\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle=\sum_mA_{mn}|m\rangle \]

An example in two dimensions is \(\hat X|1\rangle=|2\rangle\) and \(\hat X|2\rangle=|1\rangle\), the NOT gate of a quantum computer. The first column is where \(|1\rangle\) goes, (0, 1). The second column is where \(|2\rangle\) goes, (1, 0).

\[ X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \]
High school example

If you know the price of each item in a shop you can work out the total of any basket. Two loaves and three milks cost twice the bread price plus three times the milk price. Prices are linear. An operator works the same way. Knowing what it does to the basis kets, the columns of its matrix, is enough to know what it does to any ket.

Exam pattern. “Write \(\hat A\) as a matrix in the basis \(\{|n\rangle\}\)”

1. Apply \(\hat A\) to each basis ket.

2. Expand the result in the same basis, \(\hat A|n\rangle=\sum_mA_{mn}|m\rangle\).

3. Write the coefficients into the n-th column. In short \(A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle\).

If they ask for the general expression, write \(\hat A=\hat I\hat A\hat I\) and expand both identities. A common slip is mixing up rows and columns. The components of \(\hat A|n\rangle\) go into a column, not a row.

Board 4

Matrix elements and the ket as a column vector

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceIn the same basis a ket becomes a column vector, \(|\psi\rangle\to\vec c=(c_1,\dots,c_N)^T\), and a bra becomes a conjugated row, \(\langle\psi|\to\vec c^{\,\dagger}\). The components of \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\) are then row times column, \(\sum_nA_{mn}c_n\).

\(A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle\) is the matrix representation of the operator. In the same basis the ket expands as \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle\). The set of \(c_n\) describes the ket completely, which is the “describes / represents” on the board. Stack them in a column.

\[ |\psi\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\vec c=\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\\\vdots\\c_N\end{pmatrix},\qquad c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle \]

The bra is \(\langle\psi|=\sum_nc_n^*\langle n|\), the row vector \(\vec c^{\,\dagger}=(c_1^*,\dots,c_N^*)\). The inner product of two kets is row times column.

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\vec d^{\,\dagger}\vec c=\sum_nd_n^*c_n \]

Now take \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\). Apply the expansion from Board 3 to the ket. The factor \(\langle n|\psi\rangle\) on the right is exactly \(c_n\).

\[ \hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle A_{mn}\langle n|\psi\rangle=\sum_m\Big(\sum_nA_{mn}c_n\Big)|m\rangle \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat A|\psi\rangle&=\sum_{m,n}|m\rangle A_{mn}\langle n|\psi\rangle\\&=\sum_m\Big(\sum_nA_{mn}c_n\Big)|m\rangle\end{aligned} \]

The bracket is the m-th row of A times the column \(\vec c\), ordinary matrix multiplication from school. The next two boards give this result a name.

One warning. These numbers depend on the basis. The same ket is a different column in a different basis, and the same operator is a different matrix. Section 6.5 on Boards 12 to 14 connects the two descriptions.

Board note

The last entry of the column is written \(c_n\). Here n is the summation index, so the last entry should be \(c_N\) with N the dimension of the space. The next whiteboard makes this explicit with “let n = 1, 2, …, N”.

High school example

Describing a building as “2 km east and 1 km north of the main square”. The pair (2, 1) is not the building itself, only its description relative to the axes you chose. Change the axes and the numbers change while the building stays put. The ket is the building and \(\vec c\) is the description.

Explain it in your own words, then open and compare. Why is a bra a row, and why is it conjugated?

It is a row because a bra has to combine with a ket into a number, and a row times a column is a number. It is conjugated because \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\) has to behave like a squared length, real and non negative, \(\sum_nc_n^*c_n=\sum_n|c_n|^2\). Without the conjugate you would get \(\sum c_n^2\), which can be complex or even zero.

Board 5174744

Three languages, one physics: waves, Dirac, matrices

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceThe action of an operator on a state can be written in three languages. There is a differential operator acting on a wave function in Schrödinger's language, the abstract \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\) in Dirac's, and an N×N matrix times an N×1 column in Heisenberg's matrix mechanics. All three describe the same calculation.

The top of the board names the result of the previous board.

\[ \hat A|\psi\rangle\;\longleftrightarrow\;\sum_nA_{mn}c_n\;\longleftrightarrow\;\overleftrightarrow{A}\,\vec c,\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N \]

The double arrow \(\overleftrightarrow{A}\) is the lecturer's notation for a matrix and the single arrow \(\vec c\) marks a column vector. The sizes are (N×N)(N×1) = N×1. The result is again a ket and again a column.

The summary at the bottom sets the three languages side by side.

LanguageStateOperatorExample, momentum
Schrödinger, wave mechanics, 1926\(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\)differential operator\(-i\hbar\,\dfrac{d\psi}{dx}\)
Dirac, bra-ket\(|\psi\rangle\)\(\hat A\)\(\hat p|\psi\rangle\)
Heisenberg, matrix mechanics, 1925\(\vec c\)N×N matrix\(P\vec c\)

Some history. Heisenberg started with matrices in 1925 and Schrödinger with his wave equation in 1926. Later that year Schrödinger showed the two are equivalent. Dirac notation is the abstract language that contains both. \(\psi(x)\) is the same ket written in coordinates of the position basis, with one component for every x, a continuous “column”. \(\vec c\) holds the coordinates in a discrete basis. Week 6 builds this link in detail with the position and momentum bases.

The lecturer tied it together in class. In every version we do the same thing. We describe a microscopic system by a state, and each quantity we measure has an operator. Which language you write in depends on where the calculation is easiest.

Board note

The board sums up an operator acting on a state as “observing some feature of the quantum state”. Taken literally that is not right. \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\) is just a new ket. It is often not even normalised and it is not the state after a measurement. A measurement returns one eigenvalue \(a_n\) of \(\hat A\) with probability \(|\langle a_n|\psi\rangle|^2\), and the state becomes \(|a_n\rangle\) afterwards. For example \(\hat X|1\rangle=|2\rangle\), yet measuring X in the state \(|1\rangle\) gives +1 half the time and −1 half the time. There is no measurement outcome called “|2⟩”, and Board 14 works this out. The operator is the observable. Writing \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\) is not measuring.

“Heisenberg's way” here means matrix mechanics. A separate idea about time evolution comes later, the Heisenberg picture, which moves the time dependence from the state to the operators. Do not mix the two up.

High school example

The same song in three forms. There is the sheet music, the sound wave coming out of the speaker and the list of numbers in a MIDI file. All three are the same song. A guitarist reads the sheet, a sound engineer looks at the wave and a computer uses the numbers. You pick whichever makes your job easier.

Board 7983466

6.2 A Hermitian operator is a mirrored, conjugated table

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceThe matrix form of \(\hat A^\dagger=\hat A\) is \(A_{mn}=A_{nm}^*\). Hold a mirror to the diagonal and you see the conjugate of the opposite entry. The derivation rests on one rule, \(\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle={}\)\(\langle n|\hat A^\dagger|m\rangle^*\).

The Hermitian conjugate or adjoint \(\hat A^\dagger\) is defined by this identity for all \(|\phi\rangle\) and \(|\psi\rangle\).

\[ \langle\phi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger|\phi\rangle^* \]

Its origin is the first line of Board 11. The bra of \(\hat A|\phi\rangle\) is \(\langle\phi|\hat A^\dagger\). Combine that with the conjugate symmetry of the inner product, \(\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger|\phi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\hat A\psi|\phi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\phi|\hat A\psi\rangle^*\). Conjugating both sides gives the identity above.

In matrix language \((A^\dagger)_{mn}={}\)\(\langle m|\hat A^\dagger|n\rangle={}\)\(\langle n|\hat A|m\rangle^*={}A_{nm}^*\). The dagger is the transpose plus the complex conjugate, the conjugate transpose.

A Hermitian operator has \(\hat A^\dagger=\hat A\). The derivation on the board is

\[ A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle=\langle n|\hat A^\dagger|m\rangle^*=\langle n|\hat A|m\rangle^*=A_{nm}^* \]
\[ \begin{aligned}A_{mn}&=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle=\langle n|\hat A^\dagger|m\rangle^*\\&=\langle n|\hat A|m\rangle^*=A_{nm}^*\end{aligned} \]

The first equality is conjugate symmetry, the definition above with φ = m and ψ = n. The second uses \(\hat A^\dagger=\hat A\). The result in the red box is that a Hermitian matrix equals its own conjugate transpose.

Immediate consequences. The diagonal entries are real, since \(A_{nn}=A_{nn}^*\). Every entry below the diagonal is the conjugate of the one above. An N×N Hermitian matrix has N real diagonal entries and N(N−1)/2 complex off diagonal entries, N² real parameters in total.

\[ \begin{pmatrix}2&1-i\\1+i&-1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{Hermitian},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{not, symmetric but }A_{21}\neq A_{12}^* \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\begin{pmatrix}2&1-i\\1+i&-1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{Hermitian}\\[4pt]&\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{not Hermitian}\end{aligned} \]

For real matrices Hermitian and symmetric mean the same thing. For complex matrices being symmetric is not enough. The conjugate is required.

Board note

The bottom left of the board reads \(\langle\phi|\hat A|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger|\phi\rangle\). The star on the right is missing. It should be \(\langle\phi|\hat A|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger|\phi\rangle^*\). The main line \(\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle={}\)\(\langle n|\hat A^\dagger|m\rangle^*\) is correct, so compare the two. Without the star the identity is false in general. Try it with a random complex matrix and it fails.

The middle line \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^\dagger={}\)\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle^*\) is confusing too. The dagger of a number is its conjugate, so \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^\dagger={}\)\(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^*={}\)\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\). What is meant is the line below it, \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^*\).

High school example

A table of distances between cities. Ankara to Istanbul equals Istanbul to Ankara, so the table is symmetric about its diagonal. A Hermitian matrix is the complex version. In the mirror each number turns into its conjugate, a + bi into a − bi. The conjugate does nothing to real numbers, so every real symmetric table is already Hermitian.

In one sentenceA Hermitian operator has real eigenvalues, its eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and its eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis of the space. Its expectation value in any state is real too. Measuring devices show real numbers, which is why observables are chosen to be Hermitian.

The proof on the board is for the expectation value.

\[ \langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger|\psi\rangle^*=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle^*\;\Longrightarrow\;\langle A\rangle=\langle A\rangle^*\in\mathbb R \]

The first equality is the rule from Board 7 with φ = ψ and the second is \(\hat A^\dagger=\hat A\). A number equal to its own conjugate is real.

Real eigenvalues. If \(\hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle\) then \(\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle={}\)\(a\langle a|a\rangle\). The left side is real by the argument above and \(\langle a|a\rangle\) is a positive real number. Dividing shows that a is real.

Orthogonal eigenvectors. Take \(\hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle\) and \(\hat A|b\rangle=b|b\rangle\) with a ≠ b. Compute \(\langle b|\hat A|a\rangle\) in two ways. Acting on the ket to the right gives \(a\langle b|a\rangle\). Acting on the bra to the left uses \(\langle b|\hat A={}\)\((\hat A^\dagger|b\rangle)^\dagger={}\)\((\hat A|b\rangle)^\dagger={}b^*\langle b|={}\)\(b\langle b|\) and gives \(b\langle b|a\rangle\).

\[ \langle b|\hat A|a\rangle=a\langle b|a\rangle=b\langle b|a\rangle\;\Longrightarrow\;(a-b)\langle b|a\rangle=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\langle b|a\rangle=0 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\langle b|\hat A|a\rangle=a\langle b|a\rangle=b\langle b|a\rangle\\&\Longrightarrow(a-b)\langle b|a\rangle=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\langle b|a\rangle=0\end{aligned} \]

When an eigenvalue is degenerate, with several eigenvectors, you choose orthonormal vectors inside that subspace with Gram–Schmidt. In finite dimensions the eigenvectors always form a complete basis. That is the spectral theorem of Board 12. The note “Orthonormal basis for the expansions in V, the Hilbert space corresponding to the physical system” says the same. We can expand every state in this basis and the \(|c_n|^2\) become probabilities.

The last line says that when the basis is the eigenbasis of \(\hat A\) the spectral decomposition is \(\hat A=\sum_na_n|n\rangle\langle n|\). The board says “the basis for the Hermitian operator”, meaning the basis made of the eigenvectors of \(\hat A\).

An operator is diagonal in its own eigenbasis

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceIf you pick the eigenvectors of an operator as the basis, its matrix becomes diagonal, \(A_{mn}=a_n\delta_{mn}\), so \(A=\mathrm{diag}(a_1,\dots,a_N)\). In this basis the expectation value of a state is the average of the eigenvalues weighted by their probabilities, \(\sum_na_n|c_n|^2\).

The spectral decomposition \(\hat A={}\sum_na_n|n\rangle\langle n|={}\)\(\sum_na_n\hat P_n\) is the same form we wrote with projectors on Board 2. Compute its matrix elements.

\[ A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle=a_n\langle m|n\rangle=a_n\delta_{mn}\quad\Rightarrow\quad A_{mn}=0\ (m\neq n),\qquad A_{nn}=a_n \]
\[ \begin{aligned}A_{mn}&=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle=a_n\langle m|n\rangle=a_n\delta_{mn}\\[2pt]&\Rightarrow\;A_{mn}=0\ (m\neq n),\quad A_{nn}=a_n\end{aligned} \]

The first equality uses the eigenvalue problem \(\hat A|n\rangle=a_n|n\rangle\). In matrix language it reads \(A\vec a_n=a_n\vec a_n\). In its own basis \(\vec a_n\) is the n-th unit column, with 1 in place n and 0 everywhere else. The matrix is

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}a_1&&&0\\&a_2&&\\&&\ddots&\\0&&&a_N\end{pmatrix}=\mathrm{diag}(a_n) \]

Why do we want this? The lecturer said it in class. We want to write the operator in a basis where its matrix is diagonal. A diagonal matrix does not mix the axes. It just scales each axis by its own eigenvalue. Calculations get easy, \(A^k=\mathrm{diag}(a_n^k)\) and \(f(A)=\mathrm{diag}(f(a_n))\). Week 6 computes functions of operators exactly this way.

The expectation value can be read off directly in this basis, a point the lecturer stressed near the end of class.

\[ \langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_na_n\langle\psi|n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle=\sum_na_n|c_n|^2 \]

Each possible result \(a_n\) is multiplied by its probability \(|c_n|^2\) and the products are added. It is a weighted average.

Board note

The board says the decomposition holds for a “discrete and nondegenerate spectrum”. That is the case treated in class. The formula also works when the spectrum is degenerate. The repeated eigenvalue appears several times on the diagonal, its eigenvectors are chosen orthonormal, and \(\hat P_a=\sum_{a_n=a}|n\rangle\langle n|\) projects onto the whole subspace. For a continuous spectrum such as position or momentum the sum becomes an integral, which is week 6.

High school example

A single measurement always gives an eigenvalue, while the average can be any number in between. For the diagonal matrix think of stretching a photo 2 times sideways and 3 times upwards. The axes do not mix and each is scaled by its own factor.

Board 10

6.3 A unitary operator is a rotation that keeps lengths and angles

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceA unitary operator satisfies \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U={}\)\(\hat U\hat U^\dagger={}\hat I\), so its inverse is its dagger. It preserves inner products, which means lengths, angles and transition probabilities do not change. That is why time evolution and change of basis are unitary.

Recall that a unitary operator satisfies \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\). The board lists two helper rules, \(\hat I^\dagger=\hat I\) and the dagger reversing the order of a product, \((\hat A\hat B)^\dagger={}\)\(\hat B^\dagger\hat A^\dagger\).

With these rules the lecturer takes the dagger of \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\), arrives at \(\hat U\hat U^\dagger=\hat I\), and writes that a unitary operator commutes with its own dagger, \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat U\hat U^\dagger\). The conclusion is right but the reasoning is not, as the board note below explains. Operators that commute with their own dagger are called normal. Hermitian and unitary operators are both normal, and the spectral theorem on Board 12 holds for normal operators.

The key line of the board is that inner products are preserved.

\[ \langle\hat U\phi|\hat U\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\hat U|\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \]

In the first step the bra of \(|\hat U\phi\rangle\) is \(\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\). The first line of Board 11 explains why. The \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U\) in the middle cancels to 1. Setting φ = ψ shows that length is preserved. A normalised state stays normalised and the total probability stays 1. The transition probability \(|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2\) does not change either, the “norms, transition probabilities” on the board.

In matrix language the columns of U, and its rows, are orthonormal and \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\). Its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle. If \(\hat U|u\rangle=\lambda|u\rangle\) then \(\langle u|u\rangle={}\)\(\langle\hat Uu|\hat Uu\rangle={}\)\(|\lambda|^2\langle u|u\rangle\), so \(|\lambda|=1\). The real counterparts are rotation and reflection matrices with \(R^TR=I\).

Where they show up. Time evolution \(\hat U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\), every gate in a quantum computer, and the change of basis matrix of 6.5.

Board note

Taking the dagger of \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\) gives nothing new. \((\hat U^\dagger\hat U)^\dagger={}\)\(\hat U^\dagger(\hat U^\dagger)^\dagger={}\)\(\hat U^\dagger\hat U\), so the same equation comes back. The correct reason for \(\hat U\hat U^\dagger=\hat I\) is this. In finite dimensions \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\) says \(\hat U^\dagger\) is a left inverse of U. For a square matrix a left inverse is also a right inverse because det U ≠ 0, hence \(\hat U\hat U^\dagger=\hat I\).

In infinite dimensions this is not automatic. The shift operator \(\hat S|n\rangle=|n+1\rangle\) for n = 0, 1, 2, … satisfies \(\hat S^\dagger\hat S=\hat I\) but \(\hat S\hat S^\dagger|0\rangle=0\), so \(\hat S\hat S^\dagger\neq\hat I\). That is why the general definition asks for both conditions, \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U={}\)\(\hat U\hat U^\dagger={}\hat I\).

The dagger when moving to the bra, and the eigenvalue problem

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceThe bra of \(\hat A|\psi\rangle\) is \(\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger\). The operator gets a dagger and moves to the right. The eigenvalue problem \(A\vec a_n=a_n\vec a_n\) asks for a non zero solution, which exists only if \(\det(A-a_nI)=0\). You get the eigenvalues from this equation first and then an eigenvector for each.

The first line of the board justifies the first equality on Board 10. \(\hat A|\psi\rangle=|\phi\rangle\) is sometimes written \(|\hat A\psi\rangle\). Its bra is

\[ \langle\phi|=\big(|\phi\rangle\big)^\dagger=\big(|\hat A\psi\rangle\big)^\dagger=\langle\hat A\psi|=\langle\psi|\hat A^\dagger \]

In matrix language this is \((A\vec c)^\dagger={}\)\(\vec c^{\,\dagger}A^\dagger\). The transpose of a product reverses the order, and the conjugate comes along. For a unitary operator \(\langle\hat U\phi|={}\)\(\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\) is a special case.

Now the eigenvalue problem \(\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n|a_n\rangle\). In matrix language

\[ A\vec a_n=a_n\vec a_n\;\Longrightarrow\;(A-a_nI)\,\vec a_n=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\det(A-a_nI)=0 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&A\vec a_n=a_n\vec a_n\;\Longrightarrow\;(A-a_nI)\,\vec a_n=0\\&\Longrightarrow\;\det(A-a_nI)=0\end{aligned} \]

Why the determinant? If \(A-a_nI\) were invertible you could multiply both sides by its inverse and get \(\vec a_n=0\). A non zero eigenvector, the “we have nonzero \(\vec a_n\) vectors iff” on the board, exists only when the matrix is singular, and singular means zero determinant.

The recipe has three steps. First expand the characteristic determinant into a polynomial of degree N whose roots are the eigenvalues. Then put each eigenvalue into \((A-a_nI)\vec a_n=0\) and solve for the eigenvector. Finally normalise. For 2×2 there is a shortcut.

\[ \det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-(\mathrm{tr}A)\,\lambda+\det A=0 \]

The eigenvalues add up to the trace and multiply to the determinant. A worked example with the Hermitian \(A={}\)\(\begin{pmatrix}1&1-i\\1+i&2\end{pmatrix}\). The trace is 3 and the determinant is \(2-(1-i)(1+i)=2-2=0\), so \(\lambda^2-3\lambda=0\) and λ = 0 or 3.

  • λ = 3. \((A-3I)\vec a={}\)\(\begin{pmatrix}-2&1-i\\1+i&-1\end{pmatrix}\vec a={}\)\(0\). The second row says \((1+i)x=y\). Choosing x = 1 − i gives y = 2. Normalised, \(\vec a_{(3)}=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1-i,\ 2)^T\).
  • λ = 0. The first row says \(x+(1-i)y=0\). Choosing y = −1 gives x = 1 − i. Normalised, \(\vec a_{(0)}=\frac1{\sqrt3}(1-i,\ -1)^T\).
  • Check. \(\vec a_{(0)}^{\,\dagger}\vec a_{(3)}={}\)\(\frac{(1+i)(1-i)+(-1)(2)}{\sqrt{18}}={}\)\(\frac{2-2}{\sqrt{18}}={}0\). They are orthogonal, as Board 8 promised.

Board 12

Spectral theorem and the change of basis matrix

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of .

In one sentenceThe matrix U whose columns are the eigenvectors is unitary and diagonalises a Hermitian matrix, \(U^\dagger AU=\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\), or equivalently \(A=U\,\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\,U^\dagger\). Moving between two orthonormal bases is also done by a unitary matrix, \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\).

Let the n-th column of U be the n-th normalised eigenvector, \(U=(\vec a_1\ \vec a_2\ \cdots\ \vec a_N)\). The n-th column of AU is \(A\vec a_n=a_n\vec a_n\), so \(AU=U\,\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\). The eigenvectors are orthonormal, so U is unitary, \((U^\dagger U)_{mn}={}\)\(\vec a_m^{\,\dagger}\vec a_n={}\delta_{mn}\). Multiply by \(U^\dagger\) from the left.

\[ U^\dagger AU=\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A=U\,\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\,U^\dagger \]

The “spectral theorem” in quotes on the board says every Hermitian matrix, and more generally every normal matrix, can be diagonalised by a unitary transformation. The lecturer summed it up like this. You wrote A in some basis where it is not diagonal. You solved the eigenvalue problem. With the unitary transformation built from the eigenvectors you moved to the eigenbasis. What you did is a change of basis, which is why the board continues with 6.5.

Example 1. The eigenvectors of X are \((1,1)^T/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)^T/\sqrt2\). U is the Hadamard matrix H and \(H^\dagger=H\).

\[ H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H^\dagger XH=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=Z \]

Example 2. For the matrix of Board 11, \(U=\big(\vec a_{(0)}\ \ \vec a_{(3)}\big)\) and \(U^\dagger AU=\mathrm{diag}(0,\,3)\).

6.5 Change of basis. \(\{|e_n\rangle\}\) and \(\{|f_m\rangle\}\) are two complete orthonormal bases of an N dimensional Hilbert space. Both have N vectors. The dimension does not depend on the choice of basis, so the matrix is square. The elements of the change of basis matrix are

\[ U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle,\qquad m,n=1,\dots,N \]

The n-th column of U lists the coordinates of the old basis ket \(|e_n\rangle\) in the new basis. Is U unitary? Insert the identity of the f basis.

\[ (U^\dagger U)_{nk}=\sum_mU_{mn}^*U_{mk}=\sum_m\langle e_n|f_m\rangle\langle f_m|e_k\rangle=\langle e_n|e_k\rangle=\delta_{nk} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}(U^\dagger U)_{nk}&=\sum_mU_{mn}^*U_{mk}=\sum_m\langle e_n|f_m\rangle\langle f_m|e_k\rangle\\&=\langle e_n|e_k\rangle=\delta_{nk}\end{aligned} \]
Careful, two different U's

The lecturer uses the letter U for two jobs. The U of the spectral theorem has the eigenvectors in its columns, the kets of the new basis written in the old one, and the formula is \(U^\dagger AU\). The change of basis matrix \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\) has the bras of the new basis in its rows, and the formula on Board 13 is \(UAU^\dagger\). When the new basis is the eigenbasis the two are each other's dagger, \(U_{\text{basis}}=U_{\text{spectral}}^\dagger\). Both lead to the same diagonal matrix.

In the X example you cannot see the difference because \(H^\dagger=H\). With Pauli Y you can. For \(U={}\)\(\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix}\) you get \(U^\dagger YU=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)\), but \(UYU^\dagger=X\), which is not diagonal. A rule to remember. Kets in the columns go with \(U^\dagger AU\), bras in the rows go with \(UAU^\dagger\).

Coordinates transform with U and matrices with U A U†, the physics stays put

In one sentenceThe coordinates of one state in two bases are related by \(\vec c_f=U\vec c_e\) and the two matrices of one operator by \(A_f=UA_eU^\dagger\). The state and the operator do not change physically, only their descriptions do, so physical numbers such as the expectation value come out the same in both bases.

Coordinates. \(c_{m,f}=\langle f_m|\psi\rangle\). Insert the identity of the e basis.

Operator. In class the lecturer put the identity of the e basis on both sides of the matrix element in the f basis.

\[ (A_f)_{mk}=\langle f_m|\hat A|f_k\rangle=\sum_{n,l}\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\hat A|e_l\rangle\langle e_l|f_k\rangle=\sum_{n,l}U_{mn}\,(A_e)_{nl}\,U_{kl}^* \]
\[ \begin{aligned}(A_f)_{mk}&=\langle f_m|\hat A|f_k\rangle\\&=\sum_{n,l}\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\hat A|e_l\rangle\langle e_l|f_k\rangle\\&=\sum_{n,l}U_{mn}\,(A_e)_{nl}\,U_{kl}^*\end{aligned} \]

\[ \vec c_f^{\,\dagger}A_f\vec c_f=\vec c_e^{\,\dagger}\underbrace{U^\dagger U}_{I}A_e\underbrace{U^\dagger U}_{I}\vec c_e=\vec c_e^{\,\dagger}A_e\vec c_e=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle \]

In the same way inner products \(\vec d_f^{\,\dagger}\vec c_f={}\)\(\vec d_e^{\,\dagger}\vec c_e\), eigenvalues, the trace and the determinant all stay the same. What changes are the numbers themselves, the coordinates and the matrix entries.

The lecturer connected this to physics being independent of the observer. We build the mathematics so that the choice of coordinates cannot change the physics. In classical mechanics you also pick a reference frame and write coordinates relative to it. Rotating the frame changes the coordinates, not the motion of the body. If a representation changed a physical result, it would not be a good description of nature.

Exam practice

Seven solved problems

Homework style problems. Solve each one yourself first, then open the solution. Each repeats one board derivation in exam language.

1. \(\hat A|1\rangle=2|1\rangle+i|2\rangle\) and \(\hat A|2\rangle=-i|1\rangle+3|2\rangle\). Write the matrix, say whether it is Hermitian, and find the eigenvalues.

The columns are the images of the basis kets, \(A={}\)\(\begin{pmatrix}2&-i\\i&3\end{pmatrix}\).

It is Hermitian. The diagonal is real and \(A_{21}=i=(-i)^*=A_{12}^*\).

\(\lambda^2-5\lambda+(6-1)={}0\Rightarrow{}\)\(\lambda={}\)\(\frac{5\pm\sqrt5}{2}\approx3.618,\ 1.382\). They are real, add up to the trace 5 and multiply to the determinant 5.

2. Prove that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real and that eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Let \(\hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle\). Then \(\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle={}\)\(a\langle a|a\rangle\). The left side satisfies \(\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle={}\)\(\langle a|\hat A^\dagger|a\rangle^*={}\)\(\langle a|\hat A|a\rangle^*\), so it is real. Since \(\langle a|a\rangle>0\), a is real.

Let \(\hat A|b\rangle=b|b\rangle\) with a ≠ b. \(\langle b|\hat A={}\)\((\hat A|b\rangle)^\dagger={}b^*\langle b|={}\)\(b\langle b|\). Hence \(\langle b|\hat A|a\rangle={}\)\(a\langle b|a\rangle={}\)\(b\langle b|a\rangle\Rightarrow{}\)\((a-b)\langle b|a\rangle={}0\Rightarrow{}\)\(\langle b|a\rangle={}0\).

3. (a) Show that a unitary operator preserves inner products. (b) In finite dimensions derive \(\hat U\hat U^\dagger=\hat I\) from \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\) and explain why taking the dagger of the equation is not enough. (c) Show that its eigenvalues have modulus 1.

(a) \(\langle\hat U\phi|={}\)\(\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\) ⇒ \(\langle\hat U\phi|\hat U\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\hat U|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\).

(b) \((\hat U^\dagger\hat U)^\dagger={}\)\(\hat U^\dagger\hat U\), the same equation. In finite dimensions U is a square matrix and \(U^\dagger U=I\) gives \(\det U^\dagger\det U=1\). So U is invertible with \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\), and \(UU^\dagger=UU^{-1}=I\). In infinite dimensions the shift \(\hat S|n\rangle=|n+1\rangle\) is a counterexample.

(c) \(\hat U|u\rangle=\lambda|u\rangle\) ⇒ \(\langle u|u\rangle={}\)\(\langle\hat Uu|\hat Uu\rangle={}\)\(|\lambda|^2\langle u|u\rangle\) ⇒ \(|\lambda|=1\).

4. Diagonalise \(A={}\)\(\begin{pmatrix}1&1-i\\1+i&2\end{pmatrix}\). Give the eigenvalues, the normalised eigenvectors, U and \(U^\dagger AU\).

Trace 3 and determinant 0 give λ = 0 and 3.

\(\vec a_{(0)}=\frac1{\sqrt3}(1-i,\,-1)^T\) and \(\vec a_{(3)}=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1-i,\,2)^T\), with \(\vec a_{(0)}^{\,\dagger}\vec a_{(3)}=0\).

\[ U=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1-i}{\sqrt3}&\frac{1-i}{\sqrt6}\\-\frac1{\sqrt3}&\frac2{\sqrt6}\end{pmatrix},\qquad U^\dagger AU=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix} \]

Check \(U^\dagger U=I\). The squared column lengths are \(\frac{2+1}{3}=1\) and \(\frac{2+4}{6}=1\).

5. For two complete orthonormal bases, show that \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\) is unitary and that \(\vec c_f=U\vec c_e\) and \(A_f=UA_eU^\dagger\).

\((U^\dagger U)_{nk}={}\)\(\sum_m\langle e_n|f_m\rangle\langle f_m|e_k\rangle={}\)\(\langle e_n|\hat I_f|e_k\rangle={}\)\(\delta_{nk}\).

\(c_{m,f}={}\)\(\langle f_m|\hat I_e|\psi\rangle={}\)\(\sum_nU_{mn}c_{n,e}\).

\((A_f)_{mk}={}\)\(\langle f_m|\hat I_e\hat A\hat I_e|f_k\rangle={}\)\(\sum_{n,l}U_{mn}(A_e)_{nl}U_{kl}^*={}\)\((UA_eU^\dagger)_{mk}\).

6. In the state \(\vec c_e=(3/5,\ 4i/5)^T\), compute ⟨Y⟩ in the e basis and in the eigenbasis of Y. Give the possible results of measuring Y and their probabilities.

In the e basis \(\vec c_e^{\,\dagger}Y\vec c_e=\tfrac{24}{25}\), as on Board 14.

In the eigenbasis \(\vec c_f=\frac{\sqrt2}{10}(7,\,-1)^T\), \(A_f=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)\) and \(\langle Y\rangle=0.98-0.02=0.96\).

The results are +1 with probability 0.98 and −1 with probability 0.02. Both routes give the same number. A unitary change of basis does not change the physics.

7. What can the eigenvalues of an operator that is both Hermitian and unitary be? Find \(\hat A^2\) and give an example.

Hermitian ⇒ the eigenvalues are real. Unitary ⇒ \(|\lambda|=1\). Both together ⇒ λ = ±1.

\(\hat A^2={}\hat A\hat A={}\)\(\hat A\hat A^\dagger={}\hat I\). Examples are Pauli X, Y, Z and the Hadamard H. They are observables whose only outcomes are ±1 and at the same time valid quantum gates.
