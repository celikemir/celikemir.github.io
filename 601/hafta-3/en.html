From stationary states to wells

The one idea of this week: once you find the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, time evolution comes for free, since each component just picks up a phase rotating at a rate proportional to its own energy. Then we use this idea on real potentials: the infinite well, the finite well and the beginning of the harmonic oscillator. Along the way come light between mirrors and degeneracy in three dimensions.

Part A · Boards 1–2

Stationary states

We start from last week's Schrödinger equation. If the potential does not depend on time, the equation splits in two and special solutions called “stationary states” appear. The whole week is built on them.

Board 1597927

Separation of variables and the stationary state

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIf the potential does not depend on time we can split the wave function into “time part × position part”; the time part comes out as just a rotating phase, so \(|\psi|^2\) never changes, and these solutions are called stationary states.

We start with last week's equation. In abstract form \(i\hbar\,\partial_t|\psi\rangle=\hat H|\psi\rangle\): the Hamiltonian, the total energy operator, determines how the state changes in time. In the position representation the same equation becomes a partial differential equation:

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(t,x)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\psi(t,x)+V(x)\,\psi(t,x) \]

The first term on the right is the kinetic energy, the second the potential energy. The key phrase on the board is “time-independent potential”: if V depends only on x, we can split the equation in two. The board skips this step; it is the part that can be asked in the exam, so here it is in full:

  1. Try the solution as a product: \(\psi(t,x)=T(t)\,u(x)\).
  2. Substitute. The time derivative touches only T and the position derivative only u: \(i\hbar\,u\,\dot T=T\big(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u''+Vu\big)\).
  3. Divide both sides by \(T\,u\). The left side depends only on t and the right side only on x.
  4. If they are equal for every t and every x, both must equal the same constant. The unit of this constant is energy; its name is \(E_n\).
\[ i\hbar\,\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{1}{u}\Big(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u''+V u\Big)=E_n \]

So one equation splits into two; these are the “Temporal eqn.” and “Spatial eqn.” on the board:

\[ i\hbar\frac{dT}{dt}=E_n\,T\;\Rightarrow\;T(t)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},\qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_n}{dx^2}+V(x)\,u_n=E_n\,u_n \]

The second is the time-independent Schrödinger equation. It is really an eigenvalue equation, \(\hat H u_n=E_n u_n\): solutions satisfying the boundary conditions exist only for certain values of E, which is why the subscript n appears. The time equation is the same for every potential and easy to check: the derivative of \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\) is \(-iE_n/\hbar\) times itself, and multiplying by iħ gives \(E_n\) times itself.

Together they give the stationary state: \(\psi_n(t,x)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}u_n(x)\). The note “for each t the same probability” on the left edge of the board says why it is stationary:

\[ |\psi_n(t,x)|^2=\big|e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\big|^2\,|u_n(x)|^2=|u_n(x)|^2 \]

\(e^{-i\theta}\) is an arrow of length 1 in the complex plane; it rotates in time but its length does not change. Since the probability density looks only at the square of the length, it is the same at every t. “Stationary” does not mean the particle is standing still. It means the probability distribution and every expectation value computed from it do not change in time.

Look at the units in the exponent too: \(E_nt/\hbar\) is joule × second divided by joule·second, so it is dimensionless. Nothing with a unit can go inside an exponential; this check is the fastest way to tell in an exam whether you wrote a formula correctly.

High school example

The hand of a clock. As the hand turns, the direction of its tip keeps changing but its length stays the same. \(e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta\) is such a hand, and the square of its length is \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\). In a stationary state the wave function at every point is multiplied by a hand rotating at the same speed, and a measurement looks only at lengths. Although the hands turn, no length changes, so the measured distribution does not change.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why do both sides have to equal the same constant?

Hold x fixed and change only t: the right side does not change at all, so the left side cannot change with t, so it is a constant. The same logic holds for x. If an expression depending only on t equals an expression depending only on x everywhere, both must be constant. We see from the units that the constant is an energy: the unit of \(i\hbar\,\dot T/T\) is J·s × 1/s = J.

Board 2

Energy eigenstates form a basis

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceStationary states form a complete basis: if you write the initial state as a sum of them, time evolution is just multiplying each term by a phase rotating at the rate of its own energy.

We write the spatial equation of Board 1 in Dirac language. The abstract ket \(|E_n\rangle\) is “the state with energy \(E_n\)”, and \(u_n(x)\) is its form in the position representation:

\[ \hat H|E_n\rangle=E_n|E_n\rangle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\hat H\,u_n(x)=E_n\,u_n(x),\qquad u_n(x)=\langle x|E_n\rangle \]

The note at the bottom right of the board matters: the eigenvalue gives the state a label (“eigenvalue provides a label for the quantum state”). When we write \(|E_n\rangle\) we say which state we mean by its energy.

The real claim is this: the \(u_n\) form a complete basis. So any initial state can be written as a sum of them:

\[ \psi(x)=\sum_n c_n\,u_n(x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad|\psi(0)\rangle=\sum_n c_n|E_n\rangle \]

To see why this helps, remember that the Schrödinger equation is linear: a sum of two solutions with constant coefficients is again a solution. Since each \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|E_n\rangle\) is a solution on its own, their sum is a solution too:

\[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,|E_n\rangle \]

This is the “a phase introduced with time evolution” at the bottom of the board: time adds to each component its own phase and does nothing else. The most used recipe of the week comes from here:

  1. Solve the eigenvalue problem: \(\hat Hu_n=E_nu_n\). All the work in Part C is this.
  2. Expand the initial state: \(c_n=\langle E_n|\psi(0)\rangle\). Board 6.
  3. Add \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\) to each term. Done.

The measurement side comes from here too. If you measure energy, the probability of finding \(E_n\) is \(|c_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|^2=|c_n|^2\), which does not change in time. The expectation value of the energy is \(\langle E\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\). A superposition is not stationary (we will see this on Board 3), but its energy distribution is still constant: energy is conserved.

Board note

On the board the exponent is written as \(-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_n(t)\). \(E_n\) is not a function of time but a constant number; the correct form is \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), that is, \(E_n\) times t. The parenthesized form reads like a time-dependent energy, so do not write it that way in the exam.

High school example

A piano chord. In a chord each note vibrates at its own frequency and the notes do not mix. If you split a chord into its notes (C, E, G) you know separately what each note will do a second later, and you know their sum too. In quantum mechanics the notes are the energy eigenstates and the frequencies are \(E_n/\hbar\). The hard part is splitting the chord into notes; after that, time evolution is easy.

Part B · Boards 3–6

The time evolution operator

We write the same recipe in operator language: a single operator Û(t) does the time evolution. In these four boards we see which phase is visible, why Û conserves probability, and why the energy basis is orthogonal and complete.

Board 3597927

Global phase is invisible, relative phase is visible

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceA common phase multiplying the whole state (global phase) changes no measurement; but the phase difference between components (relative phase) drifts in time and moves the probability distribution through the interference term.

Let us pull the phase of any one \(E_m\) out of the sum on Board 2:

\[ |\psi(t)\rangle=\underbrace{e^{-iE_mt/\hbar}}_{\text{global phase}}\;\sum_n c_n\,\underbrace{e^{-i(E_n-E_m)t/\hbar}}_{\text{relative phases}}\,|E_n\rangle \]

The factor in front is the same for every component; it is called the global (overall) phase. Since \(|e^{i\alpha}\psi|^2=|\psi|^2\), it changes no probability and is physically invisible. The factors inside differ from component to component and depend only on energy differences. These are what you can see.

Let us see why in the simplest example: an equal mixture of two eigenstates, \(\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(u_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+u_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\big)\). Let the u be real (as in the infinite well). When taking the absolute square the cross terms remain:

\[ |\psi(x,t)|^2=\tfrac12\Big[u_1^2+u_2^2+2\,u_1u_2\cos\Big(\frac{(E_2-E_1)\,t}{\hbar}\Big)\Big] \]

The first two terms are constant. The third is the interference term, and it oscillates at angular frequency \(\omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar\). The note on the right of the board, “over interference terms change the measurable probability distributions”, is exactly this: the probability swings from one side of the well to the other.

Predict first: at t = 0 the two waves have the same sign in the left half of the well and opposite signs in the right half. After half a period, in which half does the probability gather? Then drag the slider.

The blue curve is \(|\psi(x,t)|^2\), the dashed gray curve is the constant part \(\tfrac12(u_1^2+u_2^2)\) left without the interference term, and the red line is ⟨x⟩. T = h/(E₂ − E₁) is the period of the interference. Multiplying the whole state by a global phase would change nothing in this picture.

An exam-worthy consequence of this distinction: shifting the zero of the potential (adding the same constant everywhere) shifts all the \(E_n\) by the same amount. This changes only the global phase; since energy differences stay the same, no measurement changes. Physics measures energy differences, not absolute energy.

The bottom half of the board opens section 2.3: the time evolution operator. We want to write our recipe with a single operator, \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle\). For a time-independent Ĥ the answer is:

\[ \hat U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} \]

Take the derivative to check:

\[ i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle=i\hbar\Big(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H\Big)e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle=\hat H|\psi(t)\rangle \]

The rule \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{at}=ae^{at}\) for numbers works just the same, because Ĥ commutes with itself. What does the exponential of an operator mean? In the energy basis it is very simple: Û applied to \(|E_n\rangle\) makes it \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|E_n\rangle\). Apply it to \(\sum_n c_n|E_n\rangle\) and the formula of Board 2 comes out.

Board note

At the bottom right of the board it says \(e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H(t)}\). The correct form is \(e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\): Ĥ times t. \(\hat H(t)\) would mean a time-dependent Hamiltonian, and in that case Û is not a simple exponential; that is exactly the case we are leaving out here. It is written correctly on Board 5.

High school example

Beats. Two tuning forks play at 440 Hz and 442 Hz. Each alone gives a sound of constant intensity. Played together, the sound swells and fades twice per second; the beat frequency is \(|f_2-f_1|\). The waves sometimes overlap in phase and sometimes cancel in opposite phase. The interference term in quantum mechanics is the same thing: the oscillation frequency is not the energies themselves but their difference, \((E_2-E_1)/h\). If you delayed the sound of both forks by the same half turn (a global phase), the beat would not change at all.

Board 4

Hermitian Ĥ, unitary Û

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceSince Ĥ is Hermitian, \(\hat U=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\) is unitary: the total probability stays 1 at every moment and the inner product of two states does not change in time. In the energy basis Ĥ is also diagonal.

Hermitian means \(\hat H^\dagger=\hat H\). The operator of every measurable quantity is like this, because the measurement results, the eigenvalues, must be real (proof on Board 5). From here let us compute the adjoint of Û: \(\hat U^\dagger=e^{+i\hat H^\dagger t/\hbar}=e^{+i\hat Ht/\hbar}\). Then:

\[ \hat U^\dagger\hat U=e^{+i\hat Ht/\hbar}\,e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}=\hat I \]

Since the two exponentials contain the same operator, they combine as they do for numbers: \(e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta}=1\). Such an operator is called unitary. The underlined result on the board: “normalizations and inner products are preserved”.

\[ \langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(0)|\hat U^\dagger\hat U|\psi(0)\rangle=\langle\psi(0)|\psi(0)\rangle=1 \]

Why is this vital? The Born rule reads \(|\psi|^2\) as a probability, and the total probability must be 1 at every moment. Unitarity guarantees this. Think of the opposite: if an eigenvalue of Ĥ were complex, \(E=E_0-i\Gamma/2\), then instead of a phase there would be a decaying factor like \(|e^{-iEt/\hbar}|^2=e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}\) and the particle would “disappear”. This is a deliberate trick for modeling decaying states; in the fundamental theory Ĥ is Hermitian.

Conservation of the inner product also says this: however similar two states are at the start (\(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\)), they stay exactly that similar. Time evolution is like a rotation in state space; it preserves lengths and the angles between states.

The second half of the board: Ĥ is diagonal in the energy basis, because the basis is made of the eigenstates of Ĥ:

\[ H_{mn}=\langle E_m|\hat H|E_n\rangle=E_n\langle E_m|E_n\rangle=E_n\,\delta_{mn}\;\Longrightarrow\;\hat H=\begin{pmatrix}E_1&0&\cdots\\0&E_2&\cdots\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix} \]

The note “even n may go ∞”: the matrix can be infinite dimensional. The infinite well and the harmonic oscillator have infinitely many energy levels; each is finite but their number is infinite. The benefit of being diagonal: in this basis the Schrödinger equation splits into independent equations, \(i\hbar\,\dot c_n=E_nc_n\), with solution \(c_n(t)=c_n(0)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\). The components do not mix.

High school example

A rotation. The matrix that rotates a vector in the plane by θ is \(R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\). A rotation changes neither the length of an arrow nor the angle between two arrows; in mathematics, \(R^{T}R=I\). A unitary matrix is the complex version of this: \(U^\dagger U=I\). Time evolution rotates the state but never stretches or shrinks it.

Board 5597927

Û is diagonal in the energy basis, eigenstates are orthogonal

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIn the energy basis Û is diagonal with only phases on its diagonal; this basis is so useful because eigenstates with different energies are orthogonal to each other.

On Board 4 Ĥ was diagonal. A function of a diagonal matrix is the function of the numbers on its diagonal, so Û is diagonal too:

\[ \hat U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}&0&\cdots\\0&e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}&\cdots\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},\qquad U_{mn}=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,\delta_{mn} \]

Applying this matrix to the column \(\vec c=(c_1,c_2,\dots)\) means multiplying each component by its own phase: again the recipe of Board 2, this time in matrix language.

“Energybasis will be useful in numerical discussions”: in a numerical calculation, once you diagonalize Ĥ you can jump to any t with a single multiplication instead of stepping forward. The MATLAB examples later in the course are built on exactly this: find the eigenvectors and energies with [V,D] = eig(H), then \(\vec c(t)=V\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\big)\,V^\dagger\,\vec c(0)\).

Section 2.4: orthogonality and completeness of the energy basis. The claim: eigenstates of a Hermitian Ĥ with different eigenvalues are orthogonal. The proof is short and a classic exam question:

  1. \(\hat H|E_n\rangle=E_n|E_n\rangle\) and \(\hat H|E_m\rangle=E_m|E_m\rangle\).
  2. Compute \(\langle E_m|\hat H|E_n\rangle\) in two ways. If Ĥ acts to the right: \(E_n\langle E_m|E_n\rangle\).
  3. If Ĥ acts to the left: since Ĥ is Hermitian, \(\langle E_m|\hat H=\big(\hat H|E_m\rangle\big)^\dagger=E_m^*\langle E_m|\); since \(E_m\) is real, this is \(E_m\langle E_m|\). The result: \(E_m\langle E_m|E_n\rangle\).
  4. Subtract the two: \((E_n-E_m)\langle E_m|E_n\rangle=0\). If \(E_n\neq E_m\), then \(\langle E_m|E_n\rangle=0\).

Reality comes from the same calculation: taking m = n, \(\langle E_n|\hat H|E_n\rangle\) is \(E_n\langle E_n|E_n\rangle\) and \(E_n^*\langle E_n|E_n\rangle\) by the two routes; since the norm is not zero, \(E_n=E_n^*\).

Board note

The board says “eigenstates of distinct energy eigenvalues are orthonormal”. Hermiticity guarantees only orthogonality. The length is our choice: if you multiply an eigenvector by 3 it is still an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue. Setting the length to 1 (normalizing) gives \(\langle E_m|E_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\). And if two different states have the same energy (degeneracy, Board 12), the proof says nothing; in that case we choose orthogonal ones ourselves (Gram–Schmidt).

High school example

Perpendicular axes. To find the x coordinate of a point you do not think about the y axis at all; you drop straight down to the x axis and read it. If the axes were slanted you would have to account for the share of y when finding x. Orthogonality lets you find each component independently of the others with a single projection. That is why the \(c_n=\langle E_n|\psi\rangle\) on Board 6 is so simple.

Step 3. To turn \(\langle E_m|\hat H\) into \(E_m\langle E_m|\) you need \(\hat H^\dagger=\hat H\). With a non-Hermitian operator, Ĥ acting to the left gives something else and you cannot subtract the two expressions. The eigenvalue being real comes from the same Hermiticity.

Completeness and finding the coefficients

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceSince energy eigenstates are complete, \(\sum_n|E_n\rangle\langle E_n|=\hat I\); putting this identity in front of a ket splits it into components, and each coefficient is a projection, \(c_n=\langle E_n|\psi\rangle\).

The mathematical form of completeness is the resolution of identity:

\[ \sum_n|E_n\rangle\langle E_n|=\hat I \]

\(|E_n\rangle\langle E_n|\) is a projection operator (projector): applied to a ket it leaves only the part along \(|E_n\rangle\). If you add the projections onto all directions you lose nothing and get the identity operator. If one direction were missing, the sum would not be Î but a projection onto a smaller space; that is what the word “complete” says.

Using it takes one line: multiply \(|\psi\rangle\) by 1, then expand the 1.

\[ |\psi\rangle=\hat I|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|E_n\rangle\underbrace{\langle E_n|\psi\rangle}_{c_n}\;\Longrightarrow\;c_n=\langle E_n|\psi\rangle \]

You can get the same result from the other direction: multiply both sides of \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_kc_k|E_k\rangle\) from the left by \(\langle E_n|\); orthogonality \(\langle E_n|E_k\rangle=\delta_{nk}\) leaves only the k = n term of the sum. The “by having projections or inner products” on the board says both at once.

In the position representation the inner product is an integral. In the infinite well this is the familiar Fourier sine series:

\[ c_n=\int u_n^*(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx=\sqrt{\frac2L}\int_0^L\sin\Big(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big)\,\psi(x)\,dx \]

Two more useful results. Normalization: \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2=1\), so all the energy probabilities add up to 1. The time evolution operator written with projections (spectral form): putting Î next to Û gives

\[ \hat U(t)=\sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,|E_n\rangle\langle E_n| \]

Each direction is multiplied by its own phase. This line collects all the formulas of Parts A and B in one place; it may be the only formula you need to memorize.

High school example

RGB colors. Every color on a screen is a mix of red, green and blue: color = r·R + g·G + b·B. To find the share of red you put in front of it a filter that passes only red; this is a projection, \(c_n=\langle E_n|\psi\rangle\). If you add the light passing through the three filters you get back the color you started with; that is \(\sum|E_n\rangle\langle E_n|=\hat I\). With only red and green you can make yellow but not blue: that basis is not complete.

Board 7642685

The infinite square well: setting up the problem

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe particle is free between 0 and L and the walls are infinitely high. Outside, the wave function is zero; inside, the potential is zero, so the eigenvalue equation becomes \(u''+k^2u=0\).

The title of Chapter 3 is “Boundary Conditions, Wells and Cavities”. The idea: put specific potential shapes into Ĥ and solve the eigenvalue problem. The first is the simplest model, the infinite square well:

\[ V(x)=\begin{cases}0, & 0\lt x\lt L\\[2pt] \infty, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]

Outside, u has to be zero: with V infinite, the term \(Vu\) stays finite only if u = 0. The probability of finding the particle there is zero. Since the wave function is continuous it is also zero at the walls: \(u(0)=u(L)=0\). This is the “\(u_n=0\)” written outside the walls in the drawing on the board.

Inside, V = 0 and only the kinetic term is left. The three steps on the bottom line of the board:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_n}{dx^2}=E_n\,u_n\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{d^2u_n}{dx^2}+k_n^2\,u_n=0,\qquad k_n=\frac{\sqrt{2mE_n}}{\hbar} \]

The second step just multiplies both sides by \(-2m/\hbar^2\) and collects everything on the left. The meaning of \(k_n\) is familiar: with de Broglie's relation \(p=\hbar k\), \(E=\frac{p^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\). So k is the wave number, \(k=2\pi/\lambda\). The particle inside behaves like a free wave with a definite wavelength; the walls will only select which wavelengths are allowed.

A small but exam-worthy point: E cannot be negative or zero. If E = 0, then \(u''=0\), so \(u=a+bx\); to vanish at both ends, a = b = 0. If E < 0 the solution would be exponential (\(e^{\pm\kappa x}\)) and could not vanish at both ends at once. Inside the well the potential is zero, so all the energy is kinetic and positive.

High school example

The de Broglie wavelength (12th grade modern physics): \(\lambda=h/(mv)\). An electron moving at 10⁶ m per second has a wavelength of about 0.7 nm, a few atoms wide. If the well width is on this order, the wave nature of the electron dominates and quantum results become visible. A soccer ball at the same speed has a wavelength on the order of \(10^{-40}\) m, unnoticeable in any box.

The same equation as a classical spring

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe equation \(u''+k^2u=0\) is identical to the equation of a mass on a spring, with position in place of time; that is why the solution is sin and cos, and the condition \(u(0)=0\) at the left wall eliminates the cos.

The board connects the equation to something familiar: the classical harmonic oscillator. Newton's law for a mass on a spring:

\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+kx=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\omega^2x=0,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{k/m} \]

(On the board it is written like \(m\frac{dx^2}{dt^2}\); what is meant is the second derivative \(m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}\).) The k in this line is the spring constant, with unit N/m. The \(k_n\) on the previous board is the wave number, with unit 1/m. The same letter, two different things; do not mix them up in the exam.

The matching goes like this: time ↔ position, \(x(t)\) ↔ \(u(x)\), ω ↔ \(k_n\). The note on the right of the board says this: on a spring the independent variable is time, so there is a frequency; in a well it is position, so there is a wave number. Since the mathematics is the same, the solution is the same too:

\[ u_n(x)=A\sin(k_nx)+B\cos(k_nx) \]

Why sin and cos? We need a function whose second derivative is \(-k^2\) times itself: \((\sin kx)''=-k^2\sin kx\), and the same for cos. A second order linear equation has two independent solutions; the general solution has two constants (A, B), and determining them takes two conditions. The two walls of the well give exactly two conditions. The argument of the sine must be dimensionless, so \(k_n\) has units of 1/length; this is the note “\(k_n\) is in 1/Length dimensions” on the board.

The first condition is the left wall:

\[ u_n(0)=A\sin0+B\cos0=B=0\;\Longrightarrow\;u_n(x)=A\sin(k_nx) \]

You could also have written the same solution as \(u=Ce^{ikx}+De^{-ikx}\) (Board 11 writes it this way), because \(e^{\pm ikx}=\cos kx\pm i\sin kx\). The left wall then gives D = −C and \(u=2iC\sin kx\): again a sine, only with a different constant in front.

High school example

The u(x) in the well is the same curve in space: for the spring a film in time, for the well a photograph in space. The same equation, the same curve, only the name of the horizontal axis differs.

Quantized energies, nodes, zero-point energy

In one sentenceThe condition \(\sin(k_nL)=0\) at the right wall locks k to integer multiples of π/L; the energy splits into discrete steps growing as n², and even the lowest step is not zero.

The second condition is the right wall:

\[ u_n(L)=A\sin(k_nL)=0\;\Longrightarrow\;k_nL=n\pi\;\Longrightarrow\;k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots \]

A = 0 also satisfies the condition, but then u ≡ 0 and there is no particle. That leaves \(\sin(k_nL)=0\). n = 0 again gives u ≡ 0. Negative n is not a new state either: \(\sin(-x)=-\sin x\), the same function multiplied by −1, which is a global phase. So n = 1, 2, 3, …

Get the energy from \(k_n\). The word “discrete” is in quotes on the board because this is what matters:

\[ E_n=\frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2n^2}{2mL^2}=n^2E_1,\qquad E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} \]

For a free particle k could take any value. Once it is trapped in a box, only waves that fit the walls remain and the energy is quantized. The source of the quantization is entirely the boundary conditions.

The last step is normalization. The average of \(\sin^2\) over whole half waves is 1/2, so \(\int_0^LA^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx=A^2L/2=1\) and \(A=\sqrt{2/L}\):

\[ u_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\Big(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big) \]

The three starred notes on the right of the board are the three lessons of this result:

  1. The modes are standing waves. \(k_nL=n\pi\) means \(L=n\cdot\lambda_n/2\): a whole number of half waves fits in the well.
  2. The nth state has n − 1 interior nodes. A node is a point other than the walls where u is zero. None at n = 1, one in the middle at n = 2, two at L/3 and 2L/3 at n = 3. More nodes mean more bending, and more bending means a larger \(|u''|\), that is, a larger kinetic energy.
  3. The energy of the ground state is not zero (zero-point energy). The reason is confinement and the uncertainty principle. The particle is somewhere within a width L, \(\Delta x\lesssim L\), so its momentum cannot be exactly zero. In the ground state \(\langle p\rangle=0\) and \(\Delta p=\pi\hbar/L\), so \(E_1=(\Delta p)^2/2m\) is exactly the formula above. As the box narrows, \(E_1\propto1/L^2\) increases.

The levels are not evenly spaced: \(E_{n+1}-E_n=(2n+1)E_1\), they spread out as you go up. Browse the modes with the slider below.

A guitar string. A string tied at both ends can vibrate only in patterns where a whole number of half waves fits along it: the fundamental (1st harmonic) and the higher harmonics. The reason is that on a string the wave speed is constant (\(f\propto k\)), while for a particle the energy goes as the square of the momentum (\(E\propto k^2\)).

Light between mirrors and the soap bubble

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceLight between two mirrors, like a particle in an infinite well, has to vanish at the ends; the same \(k_n=n\pi/L\) comes out, and the cavity keeps only certain wavelengths and frequencies alive.

Section 3.2, the optical cavity analogy. Think of light between two mirrors, a classical electromagnetic field. A good mirror forces the electric field to zero at its surface, just as an infinite wall forces u to zero. The spatial part of the field satisfies the same equation (\(\mathcal E''+k^2\mathcal E=0\)), so the result is the same:

\[ k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}\;\Longrightarrow\;\lambda_n=\frac{2\pi}{k_n}=\frac{2L}{n} \]

“Only standing waves can survive”: the n = 1, 2, 3 drawings on the board are the same as the modes on Board 9. Wavelengths that do not fit the cavity interfere with their own reflections and die out.

The difference from the well: in the well what was quantized was the energy of the particle, going as \(n^2\). In the cavity what is quantized is the frequency of the modes, and it goes linearly with n, because for light \(\omega=ck\). Neighboring modes are evenly spaced:

\[ \nu_n=\frac{c}{\lambda_n}=\frac{nc}{2L},\qquad \omega_n=2\pi\nu_n=ck_n=\frac{n\pi c}{L},\qquad \Delta\nu=\frac{c}{2L} \]

This is how lasers work: the laser cavity feeds only light at these frequencies. In a 15 cm cavity the spacing between modes is \(\Delta\nu=1\) GHz.

Board note

The last line of the board has \(c=\lambda_n\omega_n\) and \(\omega_n=nc/2L\). What multiplies the wavelength is the ordinary frequency, not the angular frequency: \(c=\lambda\nu\). The correct form is \(\nu_n=nc/(2L)\); the angular frequency is \(\omega_n=2\pi\nu_n=n\pi c/L\). The quantity written as ω on the board is really ν.

The middle of the board is an everyday example: the colors in soap films. White light hits a thin soap film. Part of it reflects from the top surface (R). Part goes into the film (T), reflects from the bottom surface and comes back out. Both rays reach your eye and interfere. Since the second ray travels about 2L farther, at some wavelengths they meet in phase (constructive) and at others in opposite phase (destructive). The thickness of the film varies from place to place, so a different color is reinforced everywhere.

Board note

The condition \(2L=\frac{\lambda}{2}n\) on the board becomes correct with two details. First, inside the film the wavelength is \(\lambda/n_f\) (\(n_f\approx1.33\) for soapy water), so the path difference is \(2n_fL\). Second, reflection from air to water adds a half wave (π) phase shift to the light, while reflection from water to air does not. So the condition for constructive interference in reflection is:

\[ 2n_fL=\Big(m+\tfrac12\Big)\lambda,\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots \]

The formula on the board is constructive only for odd n; even n are actually destructive. This has a nice consequence: just before the bubble bursts, when it gets very thin (L ≪ λ), the two reflections stay in opposite phase and that region looks black.

As was said in the lecture, this is not a true cavity: only two rays interfere in a soap film and the reflection is weak (a few percent), so the colors are broad and soft. In a cavity with highly reflective mirrors, light goes back and forth thousands of times and the selected frequencies are very sharp. Visible light is 400–700 nm; thicknesses on this order can select a color.

A microwave oven. The metal walls act like mirrors for microwaves, and standing waves form inside the oven. The wavelength at 2.45 GHz is about 12 cm, so the nodes are λ/2 ≈ 6 cm apart. Without a turntable, food would heat in hot and cold spots 6 cm apart; the turntable rotates to carry the food between nodes and peaks. The thin film interference of high school physics (soap bubbles, an oil slick on the road) belongs to the same family.

Board 11173127

The finite well and tunneling tails

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceWith a finite wall, the wave function of a particle with energy below the wall does not drop to zero at the wall; it leaks into the wall with a tail decaying as \(e^{-\kappa x}\), and the penetration depth is \(1/\kappa\).

3.3: finite wells and tunneling tails. The walls now have finite height, \(V_{out}\). We look at bound states: \(0\lt E\lt V_{out}\), meaning the energy of the particle is not enough to get over the wall.

Inside everything is as before: \(u''+k_n^2u=0\), with solutions \(e^{\pm ik_nx}\) or sin and cos. Outside the potential term comes back:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u''+V_{out}\,u=E_n\,u\;\Longrightarrow\;u''-\frac{2m(V_{out}-E_n)}{\hbar^2}\,u=0\;\Longrightarrow\;u''-\kappa^2u=0,\qquad \kappa\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_{out}-E_n)}}{\hbar} \]

It is all in the sign flip. Inside \(u''=-k^2u\): the second derivative points opposite to the function, the curve always bends toward the axis and an oscillation results. Outside \(u''=+\kappa^2u\): the curve bends away from the axis, and the solutions are \(e^{\kappa x}\) and \(e^{-\kappa x}\). Classically this region is forbidden, because the kinetic energy \(E-V_{out}\) would be negative.

Which exponential stays? On the side x > L, \(e^{\kappa x}\) blows up to infinity (“blows up”); the probability of finding the particle at infinity cannot grow, and it could not be normalized. That leaves the decaying \(e^{-\kappa x}\), the “surviving mode” on the board. On the side x < 0 it is the reverse and \(e^{+\kappa x}\) stays. The red arrows on the board point to the blowing up solutions and eliminate them.

The penetration depth is \(1/\kappa\): going \(1/\kappa\) into the wall, the amplitude falls to \(1/e\approx0.37\) and the probability density to \(1/e^2\approx0.14\). As \(V_{out}-E\) grows, κ grows and the tail gets shorter; in the limit \(V_{out}\to\infty\) the tail goes to zero and the condition \(u(0)=u(L)=0\) of the infinite well returns.

To find the energies you join the two regions at the walls: at x = 0 and x = L both u and \(u'\) must be continuous (since the potential jumps by a finite amount, \(u''\) also jumps by a finite amount and \(u'\) does not break). These conditions give a transcendental equation, solved graphically or numerically. This part is not on the board, but the results matter: the number of bound states is finite (in one dimension there is always at least one) and the energies are lower than in an infinite well of the same L, because the wave function leaks out and spreads over a wider region.

Predict first: when you lower the wall, does the tail get longer or shorter, and does the ground energy go up or down? Then drag the slider to the left.

\(z_0=\frac{L}{2}\frac{\sqrt{2mV_{out}}}{\hbar}\) combines the height of the wall and the width of the well into a single number; a large \(z_0\) means a high or wide well. The blue curve is the ground state of the finite well, the dashed curve that of the infinite well of the same L (both scaled to 1 at the peak), and the shaded regions are the walls. The red line is \(1/\kappa\) inside the right wall.

It was asked in the lecture: is this absorption? No. In a stationary state no probability is lost; the particle can really be found inside the wall, only with a probability that falls exponentially with distance. If the wall is thin, the tail reaches the other side and the particle passes a barrier it could not cross classically: tunneling. The probability of getting through is roughly \(e^{-2\kappa d}\), with d the wall thickness. The scanning tunneling microscope (STM), flash memory and alpha decay all rely on this exponential tail.

Board note

Under the drawing of the well the equation of the inner region is written as \(\frac{d^2u}{dx^2}+\frac{\sqrt{2mE_n}}{\hbar}u=0\), with the note \(k_n^2\) below it. The coefficient is the square, not the square root itself: \(u''+\frac{2mE_n}{\hbar^2}u=0\), that is, \(k_n^2=2mE_n/\hbar^2\).

High school example

A fingerprint on a glass of water. Look at the side of a glass full of water from above at a steep angle: the wall shines like a mirror, because the light undergoes total internal reflection. But where you hold the glass, the lines of your fingerprint are clearly visible. Even in total reflection light leaks a very short distance outside the glass; this is the optical counterpart of the \(e^{-\kappa x}\) tail (the evanescent wave). Where the ridges of your finger enter this tail the light escapes, and the valleys cannot reach it. A fingerprint is tunneling you can see with your eyes.

In one sentenceIn a three-dimensional box the energy is the sum of three one-dimensional energies; when the box is a cube, different triples give the same energy (degeneracy), and a small change that breaks the symmetry separates these levels.

3.4: D > 1 and degeneracy. In a box with sides \(L_x,L_y,L_z\), V = 0 inside. Since the kinetic energy is the sum over three directions, the solution is a product of three one-dimensional solutions (separation of variables again):

\[ u(x,y,z)=\sqrt{\frac{8}{L_xL_yL_z}}\,\sin\frac{n_x\pi x}{L_x}\,\sin\frac{n_y\pi y}{L_y}\,\sin\frac{n_z\pi z}{L_z} \]

A state is now labeled by three quantum numbers. When the box is a cube (\(L_x=L_y=L_z=L\)), the energy depends only on the sum \(n_x^2+n_y^2+n_z^2\), and different triples can give the same sum. The first levels in units of \(E_0=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)\):

The source of degeneracy is symmetry. In a cube the x, y and z axes are equivalent; renaming the axes does not change the energy, so all orderings of a triple have the same energy. The last line of the board is a link to what comes later: perturbations reduce symmetry and split degenerate levels. If you stretch \(L_z\) a little, z is now a special direction. The splitting of a line in atomic spectra into several lines by a magnetic field (the Zeeman effect) is the same idea; we will calculate it later with perturbation theory.

Board 13173127

The spring potential and natural scales

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe problem contains only m, ω and ħ; from these one can build a single length \(x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}\) and a single momentum \(p_0=\sqrt{m\omega\hbar}\), and measuring everything in these scales simplifies the equation.

Chapter 4: the harmonic oscillator in position space. The potential is that of a classical spring, \(V=\frac12kx^2\); writing the spring constant as \(k=m\omega^2\):

\[ V(x)=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2,\qquad \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\tfrac12m\omega^2\hat x^2 \]

Why is this model so important? Any smooth potential looks like a parabola near its minimum: in the Taylor expansion \(V(x_0)+\frac12V''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2+\dots\), with the first derivative zero at the minimum. The vibrations of molecules, ions in an ion trap and atoms in a crystal are all harmonic oscillators. More importantly, every mode of the cavity on Board 10 is a harmonic oscillator, and the quanta of light, the photons, come from here. This is the basic model of quantum technologies.

4.1 (Hamiltonian and dimensionless variables) first lists the units: the unit of Ĥ is the joule, \(\mathrm{J=kg\,m^2/s^2}\); m is kg, ħ is kg·m²/s, ω is 1/s. To build a length from these three constants, the only thing that brings in meters is ħ (it carries m²). Divide by m to get rid of kg and by ω to get rid of 1/s: the unit of \(\hbar/(m\omega)\) is \(\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}}{\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{1/s}}=\mathrm{m^2}\); take the square root. For momentum the unit of \(m\omega\hbar\) is \(\mathrm{kg^2\,m^2/s^2}\), with square root kg·m/s:

\[ x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}},\qquad p_0=\sqrt{m\omega\hbar},\qquad x_0\,p_0=\hbar,\qquad \frac{p_0^2}{m}=m\omega^2x_0^2=\hbar\omega \]

These are the characteristic length and momentum scales. Then the dimensionless position and momentum are defined: \(X=x/x_0\), \(P=p/p_0\). Now “X = 2” means “two \(x_0\)”.

The physical meaning of \(x_0\): the length at which kinetic and potential energy balance. If you squeeze the particle into a width Δx, the uncertainty gives \(p\sim\hbar/\Delta x\) and the kinetic energy becomes \(\sim\hbar^2/(2m\,\Delta x^2)\); the spring wants \(\frac12m\omega^2\Delta x^2\). The first penalizes being narrow, the second being wide. The minimum of their sum is exactly at \(\Delta x=x_0\), with an energy of order ħω. The size of the ground state will come out as \(x_0\) and its energy as \(\hbar\omega/2\) (we will see this on Board 14).

High school example

The period of a pendulum from unit analysis. If the period of a simple pendulum depends only on the string length L (m) and g (m/s²), the only combination producing seconds is \(\sqrt{L/g}\). The real formula is \(T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\); unit analysis cannot give the 2π but gives the rest for free. This is exactly what we did to find \(x_0\): the only combination of m, ω and ħ that produces meters.

Board 14

The dimensionless Hamiltonian and [X̂, P̂] = i

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceDividing Ĥ by ħω, x by \(x_0\) and p by \(p_0\), all the constants disappear: \(\hat H/\hbar\omega=(\hat P^2+\hat X^2)/2\) and \([\hat X,\hat P]=i\). When factoring, this commutator leaves an extra −1; next week's ladder operators come exactly from here.

Let us divide Ĥ by the energy scale ħω. The rings on the board show the parts that cancel:

\[ \frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}=\frac{\hat p^2}{2\,\underbrace{m\hbar\omega}_{p_0^2}}+\frac12\underbrace{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}_{1/x_0^2}\hat x^2=\frac{\hat P^2}{2}+\frac{\hat X^2}{2} \]

No constants are left. This means that all harmonic oscillators (molecule, ion, LC circuit, cavity mode) are the same dimensionless problem: solve it once, then scale with \(x_0\) and ħω.

The commutator simplifies too. The basic relation \([\hat x,\hat p]=\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x=i\hbar\):

\[ [\hat X,\hat P]=\frac{[\hat x,\hat p]}{x_0\,p_0}=\frac{i\hbar}{\hbar}=i \]

In the position representation, since \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\), \(\hat P=\hat p/p_0=-i\frac{\hbar}{p_0x_0}\frac{d}{dX}=-i\frac{d}{dX}\), that is, \(\hat P^2=-d^2/dX^2\). The dimensionless time-independent Schrödinger equation at the bottom of the board comes from this:

\[ \left[-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dX^2}+\frac{X^2}{2}\right]u_n(X)=\epsilon_n\,u_n(X),\qquad \epsilon_n\equiv\frac{E_n}{\hbar\omega} \]

Energies are measured in units of ħω. You can try something right away: take \(u(X)=e^{-X^2/2}\). \(u'=-Xe^{-X^2/2}\), \(u''=(X^2-1)e^{-X^2/2}\). Substitute: \(-\frac12(X^2-1)u+\frac12X^2u=\frac12u\). The Gaussian is an eigenfunction with \(\epsilon=1/2\), that is, \(E=\hbar\omega/2\). We have found the ground state and the zero-point energy without learning the solution method; this is the oscillator's counterpart of the \(E_1\ne0\) of the infinite well.

The idea on the right of the board is the topic of the next lecture. For numbers \(a^2+b^2=(a+ib)(a-ib)\), the squared length of a complex number. When you try the same with operators the order starts to matter:

\[ (\hat P+i\hat X)(\hat P-i\hat X)=\hat P^2-i\hat P\hat X+i\hat X\hat P+\hat X^2=\hat P^2+\hat X^2+i[\hat X,\hat P]=\hat P^2+\hat X^2-1 \]
Board note

On the board only \(\hat P^2+\hat X^2\) is written under the product. This would be correct only if \(\hat X\) and \(\hat P\) commuted. Since \([\hat X,\hat P]=i\), the product leaves an extra \(i\cdot i=-1\). This is also the answer to the question asked in the lecture, “under what condition does it factor this way?”: if the commutator were zero.

This −1 is not a flaw but a treasure. Defining \(\hat a=(\hat X+i\hat P)/\sqrt2\) and \(\hat a^\dagger=(\hat X-i\hat P)/\sqrt2\), the same calculation gives \(\hat a^\dagger\hat a=\frac12\big(\hat X^2+\hat P^2-1\big)\), that is,

\[ \hat H=\hbar\omega\Big(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\tfrac12\Big) \]

The ½ at the end is the zero-point energy we found above with the Gaussian. This last part is not on the board; it is a preview of next week's ladder operator method.

High school example

Order in matrices. For numbers \((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\). For matrices this breaks: \((A+B)^2=A^2+AB+BA+B^2\) and in general \(AB\neq BA\). For example, for \(A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(AB=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) but \(BA=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Operators are the same; the difference \(\hat X\hat P-\hat P\hat X=i\) is not a calculation error but quantum mechanics itself.

4 · Proof: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenstates
