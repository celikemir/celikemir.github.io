Part A · Boards 1–3

Quantization

From last week's history to a recipe: how do you turn a classical formula into a quantum one? There are two answers. The first turns the quantities of a single particle into operators, the second counts how many particles are in a mode.

Board 1

Last week in one paragraph, then first quantization

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe recipe for first quantization is one line: turn the x and p of the classical formula into operators; in the position language \(\hat p\) becomes a derivative and the energy formula becomes a differential operator, the Hamiltonian.

The top half of the board is a summary of last week. Each experiment answers the question opened by the previous one; let us read the chain straight through once:

YearWhat happenedThe lesson it left

1913Bohr atom modelThe energy of the electron sits on discrete levels

1923de BroglieThe reverse is also true: matter behaves like a wave, \(\lambda=h/p\)
1927Electron diffractionDe Broglie confirmed by experiment (Davisson–Germer, G. P. Thomson)

The arrow on the board (Compton ⇒ electron diffraction) sums up the logic: if light, which we took for a wave, behaves like a particle, then the electron, which we took for a particle, should behave like a wave. The two years written with question marks on the board are correct: the Bohr model is 1913 and electron diffraction 1927.

The bottom half is the real topic of this week, first quantization. In classical mechanics the total energy of a particle is kinetic plus potential:

\[ H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(x) \]

The recipe for turning this into quantum mechanics: replace position and momentum with operators, \(x\to\hat x\), \(p\to\hat p\). This is the “operators” under the curly brace on the board. Energy then becomes an operator too:

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^{\,2}}{2m}+V(\hat x) \]

An operator is a machine: a wave function goes in and another function comes out. In the position language (position space) \(\hat x\) means “multiply by x” and \(\hat p\) is a derivative:

\[ \hat p=-i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat p^{\,2}=(-i\hbar)^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}=-\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \]
\[ \hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x) \]

Why is \(\hat p\) this derivative? The idea in the reminder box: according to de Broglie, a wave of definite wavelength \(e^{ikx}\) carries momentum \(p=\hbar k\). Apply this derivative to that wave:

\[ -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}e^{ikx}=-i\hbar\,(ik)\,e^{ikx}=\hbar k\,e^{ikx}=p\,e^{ikx} \]

The operator does not distort the wave, it only multiplies it by the momentum. So \(\hat p\) is the machine that gives exactly de Broglie's momentum on waves of definite momentum. (In week 6 we also derived the same formula from translations; the intuitive version is enough here.)

Board note

At the very bottom of the board it says \(\hat H=-\hbar^2\,\partial^2/\partial x^2+V(x)\); the \(1/2m\) has been dropped. The correct form is \(\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\). Two lines above it stands correctly as \(\hat p^{\,2}/2m\) and got lost on the way down. A unit check catches it immediately: the unit of \(\hbar^2\,\partial_x^2\) is J·kg, so it has to be divided by a mass to be an energy.

High school example

A 2 kg ball moves at 3 m/s, 5 m above the ground (g = 10 m/s²). The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12mv^2=9\) J. Write the same thing with momentum: \(p=mv=6\) kg·m/s, \(p^2/2m=36/4=9\) J. The potential energy is \(mgh=100\) J. The total is \(H=109\) J.

In classical physics H is a number. In quantum mechanics H is a machine: you put in a wave function and a function comes out. If what you put in is an energy eigenstate, what comes out is E times what went in (Board 12).

Board 2

Order matters: \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\)

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceWith operators the order of multiplication matters: \(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x\) is not zero but \(i\hbar\). All the strangeness of going from classical to quantum physics is in this line.

The commutator of two operators is the difference between “B first, then A” and “A first, then B”:

\[ [\hat A,\hat B]\equiv\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A \]

For numbers this is always zero (3·5 = 5·3). For operators it does not have to be zero. Read an operator product from right to left: \(\hat p\hat x\,f\) means apply \(\hat x\) first (multiply by x), then apply \(\hat p\) (take the derivative).

The safe way to compute a commutator is to apply it to a test function f(x). Compute the two orders separately:

\[ \hat x\hat p\,f=x\Big(-i\hbar\,\frac{df}{dx}\Big)=-i\hbar\,x f' \]
\[ \hat p\hat x\,f=-i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}\big(x f\big)=-i\hbar\,\big(f+x f'\big) \]

In the second one the product rule produces an extra f. The starred arrow at the bottom of the board points to exactly this \(\hat p\hat x\) term; that is the place to watch. Take the difference:

\[ [\hat x,\hat p]\,f=-i\hbar\,x f'+i\hbar\,f+i\hbar\,x f'=i\hbar\,f \]

The terms with \(x f'\) cancel and \(i\hbar f\) is left. This holds for every f, so as operators \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) (there is a hidden identity operator on the right side).

Why does it matter? If two operators do not commute they cannot have all their eigenstates in common: there is no state with both a definite position and a definite momentum. Later the quantitative version of this will appear, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\). For now what you need to know is that the \(i\hbar\) on the right is the quantum value of something that is zero in classical physics.

Board note

The board has “anti-commutator” written next to the square bracket. The square bracket \([\,,\,]\) is the commutator, a difference with a minus sign inside. The anticommutator is written with curly braces and is a sum: \(\{\hat A,\hat B\}=\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A\). Both appear on the next board; bosons are defined with the commutator and fermions with the anticommutator.

High school example

Apply two operations to a number. “Add 2, then multiply by 3”: \(x\to3(x+2)=3x+6\). “Multiply by 3, then add 2”: \(x\to3x+2\). The results differ, and the difference is the same for every x: 4. The “commutator” of these two operations is the constant 4.

The same is true for \(\hat x\) and \(\hat p\): whatever function you apply them to, the difference between the two orders is the same constant, \(i\hbar\) (times the function itself).

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why can there be no state with both a definite position and a definite momentum?

If there were such a ψ, then \(\hat x\psi=a\psi\) and \(\hat p\psi=b\psi\). That would give \([\hat x,\hat p]\psi=(ab-ba)\psi=0\). But the commutator gives \(i\hbar\psi\) on every function, and this is not zero. A contradiction, so no such state exists.

Board 3

Second quantization: counting particles

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIn first quantization, quantities such as the energy of a particle come out discrete; in second quantization the number of particles in a mode is discrete, and particles are created with \(\hat a^\dagger\) and annihilated with \(\hat a\).

The first sentence of the board draws the distinction. First quantization quantizes the particle itself: the energy or angular momentum of the electron in a hydrogen atom can take only certain values. This is the recipe on Board 1.

Second quantization (field quantization) quantizes something else: the occupation numbers of the modes. A mode is a pattern in which the field can vibrate on its own, like the standing waves of the electromagnetic field in a box or the harmonics on a guitar string. Each mode can hold 0, 1, 2, … particles, never a fractional number. The board separates two kinds of field: matter fields such as electrons, and the electromagnetic field for photons.

There are two operators that change the number. The annihilation operator \(\hat a\) removes one particle from a mode; in the EM field it destroys a photon. The creation operator \(\hat a^\dagger\) adds one particle to a mode. For bosons the full form is this (we will see it as the course goes on):

\[ \hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle,\qquad \hat a|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}\,|n-1\rangle,\qquad \hat a|0\rangle=0 \]

You cannot remove a particle from an empty mode: the result is the zero vector. The two kinds of particle are told apart by a single sign:

\[ \text{Bozonlar: }\;[\hat a_n,\hat a_m^\dagger]=\hat a_n\hat a_m^\dagger-\hat a_m^\dagger\hat a_n=\delta_{nm} \]
\[ \text{Fermiyonlar: }\;\{\hat b_n,\hat b_m^\dagger\}=\hat b_n\hat b_m^\dagger+\hat b_m^\dagger\hat b_n=\delta_{nm},\qquad \{\hat b_n^\dagger,\hat b_m^\dagger\}=0 \]

\(\delta_{nm}\) is the Kronecker delta: 1 if n = m and 0 otherwise. Operators of different modes do not disturb each other; within the same mode the order matters.

In the lecture someone asked whether the Pauli exclusion principle follows from these operators, and the lecturer said yes. Let us see how. In the second part of the fermion line take n = m: \(\{\hat b^\dagger,\hat b^\dagger\}=2(\hat b^\dagger)^2=0\), so \((\hat b^\dagger)^2=0\). If you try to put a second fermion into the same mode you get the zero vector. The occupation of a fermion mode is only 0 or 1. Bosons have no such limit; you can put as many photons as you like into the same mode (the laser is a consequence of this).

Predict first: what happens if you press + twice on the same mode in the fermion row? In the boson row? Then try it.

+ applies the creation operator, − the annihilation operator. In the boson row the coefficient is \(\sqrt{n+1}\) or \(\sqrt n\); for fermions the coefficient is 1, but a minus sign can appear in front when occupied modes lie in between (the order sensitivity of the anticommutator). For the drawing we stop the boson mode at 6 particles.

Board note

On the board the label “anticommutator” is on the line between the two rows, and “commutator” is also written under the fermion equation. Do not mix them up: the boson row has a square bracket, a minus sign and is the commutator; the fermion row has a curly brace, a plus sign and is the anticommutator. The indices on the right side of the boson row have also been dropped; the correct form is \(\hat a_n\hat a_m^\dagger-\hat a_m^\dagger\hat a_n\).

High school example

Think of modes as boxes and particles as marbles. You can throw as many marbles as you like into a boson box. A fermion box is like a numbered cinema seat: one person sits in a seat and a second cannot. \(\hat a^\dagger\) means “put a marble in the box”, \(\hat a\) means “take a marble from the box”. You cannot take a marble from an empty box; the result is “nothing”, that is, zero.

Board 4

Every measurable quantity is a Hermitian operator

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceObservable quantities are represented by Hermitian operators; being Hermitian guarantees that the measurement results, the eigenvalues, come out real.

The first line of the board wraps up second quantization. The quantized field is a sum over modes:

\[ \hat\psi(\vec r)=\sum_n u_n(\vec r)\,\hat a_n \]

\(u_n(\vec r)\) is the shape of the nth mode (a function), and \(\hat a_n\) is the operator that plays with the number of particles in that mode. It resembles writing a wave as a sum of its harmonics; the difference is that the coefficients are operators rather than numbers. The lecture left this topic here and moved on to 1.4.

The two basic sentences of 1.4:

  • Quantum state ⇒ probabilities of measurement outcomes. A state does not tell you “the result will be this”; it tells you “this result comes out with this probability, that result with that probability”.
  • Observables ⇒ Hermitian operators. Everything you can measure, such as energy, position and momentum, has an operator, and this operator is Hermitian. A measurement result is always one of the eigenvalues of that operator.

The note in the box separates two equivalent languages:

Schrödinger formalismHeisenberg formalism
StateA function, ψ(x)A column vector
OperatorDerivative and multiplication operationsA square matrix
DynamicsA wave equation, a differential equationMatrix equations

The definition of Hermitian in matrix language is in the bottom right corner of the board. First two operations: the transpose swaps rows and columns (ij → ji), and the complex conjugate flips the sign of i (\(3+2i\to3-2i\)). Doing both is the dagger operation:

\[ A^\dagger\equiv(A^{T})^{*},\qquad (A^\dagger)_{ij}=A_{ji}^{*} \]

A matrix is Hermitian if it equals its own dagger: \(A^\dagger=A\), that is, \(A_{ij}=A_{ji}^{*}\). This has two practical consequences. On the diagonal i = j, so \(A_{ii}=A_{ii}^*\): diagonal elements are real. Every off-diagonal element is the conjugate of the one in its mirror position:

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2-i\\2+i&3\end{pmatrix} \]

Why Hermitian? Someone asked in the lecture; the lecturer did not go into the proof, but it takes two lines. Let \(\hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle\). Multiply from the left by \(\langle a|\), then take the complex conjugate of both sides:

\[ \langle a|\hat A|a\rangle=a\,\langle a|a\rangle\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \langle a|\hat A^\dagger|a\rangle=a^{*}\langle a|a\rangle \]

Since \(\hat A^\dagger=\hat A\), the two left sides are the same. Since \(\langle a|a\rangle\ne0\), \(a=a^*\): the eigenvalue is real. A measuring device shows a number and that number is real; Hermiticity guarantees it.

High school example

Complex numbers: if \(z=3+4i\), then \(z^*=3-4i\) and \(z\,z^*=9+16=25=|z|^2\) is real. A number equal to its own conjugate (\(z=z^*\)) has zero imaginary part, so it is real. The last step of the proof above is exactly this.

A thermometer never shows “3 + 2i degrees”. A measurement result has to be a real number, and quantum mechanics places this requirement on the operator being Hermitian.

Board 5

The eigenvalue equation and bra-ket notation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentence\(\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n|a_n\rangle\): if the system is in an eigenstate, the measurement gives that eigenvalue with certainty; different eigenstates are orthogonal and have length 1, that is, \(\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\).

When an operator acts on a ket, another ket generally comes out, in a different direction. For some special kets the direction does not change and they are only multiplied by a number. These are the eigenstates:

\[ \hat A\,|a_n\rangle=a_n\,|a_n\rangle \]

The labels on the board: on the left \(\hat A|a_n\rangle\), the action of the operator on the eigenstate; \(a_n\) is the eigenvalue, a number; \(|a_n\rangle\) is the eigenstate. The physical meaning is the bottom left sentence of the board: if the system is in state \(|a_n\rangle\), measuring \(\hat A\) gives \(a_n\) with certainty. However many times you measure, the result is the same.

The notation was also introduced here. \(|\,\cdot\,\rangle\) is a ket, like a column vector. \(\langle\,\cdot\,|\) is a bra, the ket turned into a row and conjugated. Put side by side they form the “bracket” \(\langle a_m|a_n\rangle\): the inner product, a number. In matrix language:

\[ |v\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\ i\end{pmatrix}\;\Longrightarrow\;\langle v|=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad \langle v|v\rangle=1\cdot1+(-i)(i)=1+1=2 \]

Without the conjugate in the bra we would find \(1+i\cdot i=0\): a nonzero vector would have length zero. The conjugate keeps the inner product of a vector with itself positive. The length of this ket is \(\sqrt2\) and its normalized form is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\,i)\).

The formula at the bottom right is the orthonormality of two eigenstates:

\[ \langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\delta_{mn} \]

Zero if m ≠ n (orthogonal), 1 if m = n (normalized). Orthogonality is no accident, it comes from Hermiticity; normalization is our choice, scaling the length to 1.

Why are eigenstates with different eigenvalues orthogonal? (Not on the board, but it may come up in the exam.)

Compute \(\langle a_m|\hat A|a_n\rangle\) in two ways. If \(\hat A\) acts on the ket on the right, \(a_n\langle a_m|a_n\rangle\). If it acts to the left on the bra (since it is Hermitian and \(a_m\) is real, \(\langle a_m|\hat A=a_m\langle a_m|\)), \(a_m\langle a_m|a_n\rangle\). The two are the same number:

\[ (a_n-a_m)\,\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=0 \]

If \(a_n\ne a_m\), then \(\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=0\).

The last line of the board is a bridge to the next step: a system does not have to be in an eigenstate all the time. Take a general state \(|\psi\rangle\) and let it be normalized: \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\).

High school example

A mirror reflection about the x axis in the plane. An arrow along the x axis stays the same in the mirror: eigenvalue +1. An arrow along the y axis flips: eigenvalue −1. A slanted arrow, say (1, 1), becomes (1, −1) in the mirror; its direction changed, so it is not an eigenvector. With a matrix:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}=+1\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} \]

Board 6475264

Every state expands in eigenstates, and the inner product finds the coefficient

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceA general state is a sum of eigenstates, \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|a_n\rangle\); to find a coefficient, take the inner product with that eigenstate: \(c_m=\langle a_m|\psi\rangle\), the probability amplitude.

The lecturer's analogy is on the right of the board. In 3D every vector is a sum of three unit vectors:

\[ \vec r=x\,\hat\imath+y\,\hat\jmath+z\,\hat k=\sum_{n=1}^{3}c_n\,\hat x_n,\qquad \hat x_1\equiv\hat\imath,\;\hat x_2\equiv\hat\jmath,\;\hat x_3\equiv\hat k,\quad c_1=x,\;c_2=y,\;c_3=z \]

These unit vectors are normalized (\(\hat\imath\cdot\hat\imath=1\)) and orthogonal to each other (\(\hat\imath\cdot\hat\jmath=0\)); in short \(\hat x_i\cdot\hat x_j=\delta_{ij}\). The lecturer's question: given \(\vec r\), how do you find \(c_1\)? The answer is the dot product, a projection:

\[ \hat x_1\cdot\vec r=\sum_{n=1}^{3}c_n\,\hat x_1\cdot\hat x_n=\sum_{n=1}^{3}c_n\,\delta_{1n}=c_1 \]

δ leaves only the n = 1 term of the sum. In quantum mechanics it is the same step by step, with the inner product taking the place of the dot product:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|a_n\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle a_m|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\sum_n c_n\,\delta_{mn}=c_m \]

\(c_m=\langle a_m|\psi\rangle\) is called the probability amplitude. The lecturer stressed this: it is not yet a probability but an amplitude, because \(c_m\) can be complex. The probability will appear on the next board as \(|c_m|^2\).

The two concepts on the left half of the board:

  • Complete orthonormal basis. Orthogonal + normalized = orthonormal. Being complete means that every state can be written in this basis (the red circle “span” on the board). In 3D, if you use only \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) you can describe only a plane; for all of space you also need \(\hat k\).
  • Hilbert space. The vector space in which all the state vectors of the physical system live. Its distinguishing feature is having an inner product; the parenthesis on the board says this too: you get the probabilities of observations from the inner product. The lecturer said the mathematical definition is not needed for now.

Board 7

The Born rule, expectation value and variance

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe probability of measuring \(a_m\) is the absolute square of the amplitude, \(|c_m|^2\); the average of many measurements is \(\langle A\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2a_n\); the variance tells how widely the results spread around the average.

The Born rule. An amplitude can be complex, a probability cannot. Born's solution is the absolute square:

\[ P(a_m)=|c_m|^2=|\langle a_m|\psi\rangle|^2 \]

\(|z|^2=z^*z\) is always real and greater than or equal to zero. Example: if \(c=0.8i\), then \(|c|^2=(-0.8i)(0.8i)=0.64\). Careful: \(c^2=(0.8i)^2=-0.64\) would be the result otherwise; squaring without the conjugate is the most common mistake.

Since the state is normalized (\(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\)), all the probabilities add up to 1: \(\sum_n|c_n|^2=1\). Something will certainly be measured.

Expectation value. If you measure \(\hat A\) on many identically prepared systems and average the results:

\[ \langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2\,a_n \]

A weighted average: each result is multiplied by its probability and summed. The lecturer's coin toss example: win 1 lira on heads, lose 1 lira on tails. The expectation value is 0, but no toss gives 0. \(\langle A\rangle\) does not have to be an eigenvalue; it is not the result of a single measurement but the average of many.

Variance. A measure of how far the results deviate from the average: the average of the squared deviation.

\[ (\Delta A)^2=\big\langle(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)^2\big\rangle=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2,\qquad \langle A^2\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2a_n^2 \]

If the system is in an eigenstate every measurement gives the same result and the variance is zero. If the variance is greater than zero the result changes from measurement to measurement.

Board note

On the board the left side of the equality says \(\Delta A\). The right side is the variance, \((\Delta A)^2\). \(\Delta A\) is its square root: the standard deviation, the “uncertainty” in the uncertainty principle. In the exam write \(\Delta A=\sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2}\).

A concrete calculation. A quantity with two eigenvalues (spin, say): \(a_1=+1\), \(a_2=-1\), with state \(|\psi\rangle=0.6\,|a_1\rangle+0.8i\,|a_2\rangle\).

\[ P(+1)=0.36,\quad P(-1)=0.64,\quad \langle A\rangle=0.36-0.64=-0.28,\quad \langle A^2\rangle=0.36+0.64=1 \]
\[ (\Delta A)^2=1-(-0.28)^2=0.9216,\qquad \Delta A=0.96 \]

Homework 1 · derive the probability and expectation value in explicit representation

The first homework the lecturer gave is to show the results on the board by actually writing out the expansion. Four steps:

  1. Write the bra. The bra of \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|a_n\rangle\) is a sum with conjugated coefficients. Use m so the indices do not get mixed up:

    \[ \langle\psi|=\sum_m c_m^{*}\,\langle a_m| \]

  2. Normalization. Multiply the two sums and use orthonormality:

    \[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_m\sum_n c_m^{*}c_n\,\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\sum_m\sum_n c_m^{*}c_n\,\delta_{mn}=\sum_n c_n^{*}c_n=\sum_n|c_n|^2=1 \]

    Because of δ the double sum reduces to a single sum. The result: the probabilities add up to 1.

  3. Expectation value. First apply the operator to the ket; on eigenstates its action is just multiplication by the eigenvalue:

    \[ \hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n\,\hat A|a_n\rangle=\sum_n c_n\,a_n|a_n\rangle \]
    \[ \langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_m\sum_n c_m^{*}c_n\,a_n\,\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\sum_m\sum_n c_m^{*}c_n\,a_n\,\delta_{mn}=\sum_n|c_n|^2\,a_n \]

    In the same way, since \(\hat A^2|a_n\rangle=a_n^2|a_n\rangle\), \(\langle A^2\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2a_n^2\).

  4. The variance identity. \(\langle A\rangle\) is a number; open the parenthesis and take the expectation value of each term. The expectation value of a number is itself, because \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\):

    \[ \big\langle(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)^2\big\rangle=\big\langle\hat A^2-2\langle A\rangle\hat A+\langle A\rangle^2\big\rangle=\langle A^2\rangle-2\langle A\rangle\langle A\rangle+\langle A\rangle^2=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2 \]

The same thing in matrix representation: \(\vec c=(c_1,c_2)^T\) is a column, \(\hat A\) is diagonal in its own eigenbasis, \(A=\mathrm{diag}(a_1,a_2)\). \(\langle A\rangle=\vec c^{\,\dagger}A\,\vec c=c_1^*a_1c_1+c_2^*a_2c_2=|c_1|^2a_1+|c_2|^2a_2\). In the position representation (weeks 3 and 6) the sum becomes an integral: \(\langle A\rangle=\int\psi^*(x)\,\hat A\,\psi(x)\,dx\). If the exam says “show in explicit representation”, one of these three forms is expected; the safest is the four steps above.

High school example

A fair die. Each face has probability 1/6. The expectation value is \((1+2+3+4+5+6)/6=3.5\); no roll ever gives 3.5. The average of the squares is \((1+4+9+16+25+36)/6=91/6\approx15.17\). The variance is \(15.17-3.5^2=15.17-12.25\approx2.92\) and the standard deviation \(\approx1.71\). The only difference in quantum mechanics is that the probabilities come from \(|c_n|^2\).

Board 8

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe Hamiltonian determines how a state changes in time, \(i\hbar\,\partial_t|\psi\rangle=\hat H|\psi\rangle\); in the position representation this is a partial differential equation for \(\psi(t,x)\).

The equation in the red box is basis independent, in ket form:

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle=\hat H|\psi\rangle,\qquad \hat H=\frac{\hat p^{\,2}}{2m}+V(\hat x) \]

It reads like this: on the left the rate of change of the state, on the right the action of the Hamiltonian (the total energy operator) on the state. The energy of a state determines how it will change.

To calculate you need to choose a basis. In the position basis the components of \(|\psi\rangle\) are the wave function: \(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\). It is the same as the \(c_n=\langle a_n|\psi\rangle\) on Board 6, except that the index is continuous (x). Since \(\hat p\) is a derivative in this representation, the equation becomes:

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(t,x)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\psi(t,x)+V(x)\,\psi(t,x) \]

The qualifier on the board matters: a non-relativistic single particle in one dimension. Non-relativistic means the kinetic energy is \(p^2/2m\); particles near the speed of light need a different equation.

Where does the equation come from? It is not derived but postulated, yet you can see why it takes this form. For a free particle (V = 0), substitute the plane wave \(\psi=e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\):

\[ i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi=i\hbar(-i\omega)\psi=\hbar\omega\,\psi=E\,\psi,\qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(ik)^2\psi=\frac{(\hbar k)^2}{2m}\psi=\frac{p^2}{2m}\psi \]

Equating the two sides, the equation says \(E=p^2/2m\): the energy of a classical free particle. The Schrödinger equation is the energy formula written with the Planck (\(E=\hbar\omega\)) and de Broglie (\(p=\hbar k\)) links.

Board note

In the lower red box the \(1/2m\) has been dropped again: it says \(-\hbar^2\,\partial_x^2\psi\). The correct form is \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi\), as in the formula above. In the unit check on the next board the 2m is written correctly.

High school example

Newton's second law \(F=ma\), that is, \(m\,dv/dt=F\): if you know the force you know how the velocity will change. The Schrödinger equation plays the same role: if you know \(\hat H\) you know how ψ will change.

As a pattern it also resembles compound interest: the rate at which money grows is proportional to the money itself, \(dP/dt=rP\), with solution \(P_0e^{rt}\). In the Schrödinger equation the rate of change is proportional to \(\hat H\psi\); but because of the i in front, the solution rotates instead of growing (Board 13).

Board 9

How many conditions are needed, and solutions add

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceSince it is first order in time, knowing ψ at the start is enough; since it is second order in space, two boundary conditions are needed; since the equation is linear, a sum of solutions is also a solution, and this is where interference comes from.

Time: a single initial condition. The equation has only a first derivative with respect to t. So knowing \(\psi(x,0)\) determines the entire future. Compare with classical physics: Newton's equation is second order in time (\(m\ddot x=F\)), so you must give both the position and the velocity separately at the start. This was the lecturer's space station example: the pair (position, velocity) is the classical state itself. In quantum mechanics a single function \(\psi(x,0)\) is enough; the shape of that function carries the position information, and the way its phase changes carries the momentum information.

Space: two boundary conditions. There is a second derivative with respect to x, so two conditions are needed: either ψ at two separate points (such as \(\psi=0\) at the two walls of a box), or both ψ and \(\partial_x\psi\) at a single point.

The note on the right of the board, “since there is a single spatial dimension \(\partial/\partial x=d/dx\)”: we talk about boundary conditions at a fixed instant, when ψ depends only on x and the partial derivative becomes an ordinary one. In the general form ψ(t, x) there is also t, so a partial derivative is needed.

Linearity. In the equation ψ and its derivatives always appear to the first power, never multiplied together. So if \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are solutions, then for any two complex numbers α, β:

\[ i\hbar\,\partial_t(\alpha\psi_1+\beta\psi_2)=\alpha\,i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi_1+\beta\,i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi_2=\alpha\hat H\psi_1+\beta\hat H\psi_2=\hat H(\alpha\psi_1+\beta\psi_2) \]

The sum is also a solution. This is what guarantees that the superposition \(|\psi\rangle=\sum c_n|a_n\rangle\) of Board 6 is not destroyed in time.

Interference. Amplitudes add, probabilities do not. If there are two amplitudes at a point:

\[ |\psi_1+\psi_2|^2=|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+\underbrace{2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi_1^{*}\psi_2\big)}_{\text{interference term}} \]

If the last term is positive the probability increases (constructive interference); if negative it decreases and can even reach zero (destructive interference). The dark bands in the double slit experiment come from this term.

High school example

Throw two stones into still water. The waves pass through each other, and where they meet the heights add: crest on crest makes a higher crest, crest on trough makes flat water. This is superposition. With numbers: if two amplitudes at a point are 0.5 and 0.5, the square of the sum is 1, while the sum of the individual squares is 0.5. If the amplitudes are 0.5 and −0.5, the sum is 0 and there is no wave at that point.

Board 10399651

Probability flows but does not vanish: the continuity equation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentence\(\rho=|\psi|^2\) is the probability density and j the probability current; the Schrödinger equation gives \(\partial_t\rho+\partial_x j=0\): the probability at a point can change only by flowing to neighboring points.

The top three lines of the board are three definitions:

\[ \rho(x,t)=|\psi(x,t)|^2=\psi^{*}\psi,\qquad j(x,t)=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\Big(\psi^{*}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}-\psi\frac{\partial\psi^{*}}{\partial x}\Big) \]
\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0 \]

ρ is the continuous form of the Born rule: the probability of finding the particle in an interval of width dx around x is \(\rho\,dx\). j is the probability passing point x to the right per unit time. The inside of the parenthesis is the difference of a number and its conjugate (\(z-z^*=2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z\)), which is imaginary; multiplied by \(1/i\), j comes out real.

The continuity equation is the local conservation of probability (the “local statement” on the board). It is not enough for the total probability to stay 1; probability cannot vanish at one place and suddenly appear far away, it has to flow from neighbor to neighbor. The lecturer's analogy: the number of students in a classroom changes only through those entering or leaving by the door. The sign: if j increases to the right at a point (\(\partial_x j>0\)), more leaves that point than arrives, so \(\partial_t\rho\lt0\).

Integrating over an interval makes this even clearer:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int_a^b\rho\,dx=-\int_a^b\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}\,dx=j(a)-j(b) \]

The change of probability in the interval = what enters from the left − what leaves on the right. Over all space (\(a\to-\infty\), \(b\to\infty\)) ψ goes to zero at the ends, so j is zero too and the total probability is constant. A state you normalize once stays normalized.

Homework 2 · derive the continuity equation

The recipe on the board is written in red: \(\psi^{*}\times\)(Schrödinger) \(-\;\psi\times\)(Schrödinger)\(^{*}\). Step by step:

  1. The equation and its conjugate. Let V be real. Shorthand: \(\psi'=\partial_x\psi\), \(\dot\psi=\partial_t\psi\).

    \[ i\hbar\,\dot\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+V\psi\qquad(1) \]
    \[ -i\hbar\,\dot\psi^{*}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi^{*\prime\prime}+V\psi^{*}\qquad(2) \]

    Taking the conjugate flips the sign of i; ħ, m and V are real, so they do not change.

  2. Multiply. Multiply (1) from the left by \(\psi^*\) and (2) by \(\psi\):

    \[ i\hbar\,\psi^{*}\dot\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi^{*}\psi''+V\psi^{*}\psi \]
    \[ -i\hbar\,\psi\dot\psi^{*}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi\,\psi^{*\prime\prime}+V\psi\psi^{*} \]

  3. Subtract. The potential terms are the same (\(V\psi^*\psi\)) and cancel:

    \[ i\hbar\,\big(\psi^{*}\dot\psi+\psi\dot\psi^{*}\big)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(\psi^{*}\psi''-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime\prime}\big) \]

  4. The left side. The derivative of a product: \(\partial_t(\psi^*\psi)=\dot\psi^*\psi+\psi^*\dot\psi\). The left side is \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\rho\).

  5. The right side: the total derivative trick. Expand this derivative:

    \[ \partial_x\big(\psi^{*}\psi'-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime}\big)=\psi^{*\prime}\psi'+\psi^{*}\psi''-\psi'\psi^{*\prime}-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime\prime}=\psi^{*}\psi''-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime\prime} \]

    The two middle terms are the same and cancel. The parenthesis on the right side turns out to be the x derivative of something.

  6. Add it up. Divide both sides by \(i\hbar\):

    \[ \partial_t\rho=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\partial_x\big(\psi^{*}\psi'-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime}\big)=-\partial_x\Big[\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\big(\psi^{*}\psi'-\psi\,\psi^{*\prime}\big)\Big]=-\partial_x j \]
    \[ \boxed{\;\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0\;} \]

Two critical points for the exam: (a) The potential terms cancel only if V is real; if V had an imaginary part, probability would not be conserved (an imaginary potential is deliberately used to describe absorption of the particle). (b) Seeing that \(\psi^*\psi''-\psi\psi^{*\prime\prime}\) is a total derivative; you can guess it by looking at the definition of j.

The unit check is the bottom half of the board. First the unit of ħ:

\[ [\hbar]=\text{J}\cdot\text{s}=\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}=\text{kg}\,\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{s}=\text{kg}\,\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}} \]

Then the three terms of the equation (leaving out the common unit of ψ): \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\) → \(\text{J·s}/\text{s}=\text{J}\). \(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\) → \(\frac{\text{kg}^2\text{m}^4/\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2}=\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2=\text{J}\). You can check j too: in one dimension the unit of ρ is 1/m (probability is unitless, divided by length). \(\hbar/m\) → m²/s, the inside of the parenthesis → 1/m². The product is 1/s: the probability passing per unit time.

\(\psi'=ik\psi\), \(\psi^{*\prime}=-ik\psi^*\). The parenthesis: \(\psi^*(ik\psi)-\psi(-ik\psi^*)=2ik|A|^2\). Multiply:

\[ j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}\cdot2ik|A|^2=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2=\frac{p}{m}\,\rho=v\,\rho \]

Current = density × speed. The same formula as water flow in a pipe: the amount of water per meter times the flow speed.

High school example

A 1 km stretch of highway holds 50 cars. 12 cars enter per minute and 9 leave. The number of cars on this stretch rises by 3 per minute. Cars do not appear from nothing or vanish; the number changes only by the difference between what enters and leaves at the ends. The continuity equation for probability is this sentence applied at every point.

Board 11

Separation of variables: \(\psi=T(t)\,X(x)\)

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIf the potential does not depend on time, we look for a solution as the product of a function of time and a function of space; the equation splits into a left side depending only on t and a right side depending only on x, and both must equal the same constant.

The guess (ansatz): \(\psi(t,x)=T(t)\,X(x)\). Put this into the Schrödinger equation. The time derivative sees only T and the space derivative only X; the other comes out like a constant. This is what is under the curly braces on the board:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(T X\big)=X\,\frac{dT}{dt},\qquad \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big(T X\big)=T\,\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} \]
\[ i\hbar\,X\,\frac{dT}{dt}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,T\,\frac{d^2X}{dx^2}+V(x)\,T\,X \]

The partial derivatives became ordinary derivatives, because T depends only on t and X only on x. Now divide both sides by \(T(t)X(x)\) (the \(1/T(t)X(x)\) on the left edge of the board):

\[ \underbrace{\frac{i\hbar}{T}\,\frac{dT}{dt}}_{\text{only }t}=\underbrace{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{1}{X}\,\frac{d^2X}{dx^2}+V(x)}_{\text{only }x} \]

The key argument: the left side depends only on t, the right side only on x, and this equality must hold for every t and every x. Hold x fixed and change t: the right side does not change, so the left side cannot change either, so it is a constant. Do the same the other way: the right side is constant too. Both equal the same constant. The note on the left edge of the board: this is possible if and only if the two sides equal the same constant.

The condition: V must not depend on time. If it were \(V(x,t)\), the right side would also depend on t and the separation would collapse. The note “no time dependence!” on Board 13 stresses this.

Board note

On the board there is a hat-like mark over X(x); it is not an operator hat, just a handwriting habit. X is an ordinary function. Also, the form \(d^2/d_x^2\) at the bottom right should be read as \(d^2/dx^2\).

High school example

Let \(f(t)=g(x)\) hold for every t and every x. It cannot be \(f(t)=t\) and \(g(x)=x\): if you choose \(t=1\), \(x=2\), then \(1\ne2\). The only way for this equality to always hold is for both to be the same number, like \(f(t)=5\) and \(g(x)=5\). If two people, one looking only at their watch and the other only at their address, say the same thing at every moment, they must both always be saying the same fixed sentence.

Board 12399651

The constant is named E: the eigenvalue equation of the Hamiltonian

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe separation constant is the energy; the space part becomes \(\hat H u_n=E_n u_n\), the eigenvalue equation of the first hour written for the Hamiltonian.

Set the right side equal to the constant and multiply by X:

\[ E=\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2X}{dx^2}+V(x)X(x)\Big]\frac{1}{X(x)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x)\Big]}_{\hat H}X(x)=E\,X(x) \]

The square bracket is exactly the Hamiltonian operator \(\hat H\). Why do we call the constant E? In the unit check (Board 10) we saw that every term on the right side is in joules, so the constant is an energy. Moreover it is an eigenvalue of \(\hat H\), the result of an energy measurement.

This equation generally has many solutions; we number them with n and write \(u_n\) for X and \(E_n\) for E (the arrows on the board):

\[ \hat H\,u_n(x)=E_n\,u_n(x) \]

This is called the time-independent Schrödinger equation. Now look back at Board 5: \(\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n|a_n\rangle\). The same equation, with \(\hat A\to\hat H\), \(a_n\to E_n\), \(|a_n\rangle\to u_n(x)\). This is the moment the lecturer meant by “things that look separate will connect later”. The consequences come ready-made from Part B:

  • Since \(\hat H\) is Hermitian, \(E_n\) is real and the \(u_n\) with different energies are orthogonal.
  • The \(u_n\) form a basis; every state expands as \(\psi=\sum_n c_n u_n\).
  • The probability of finding \(E_n\) when measuring energy is \(|c_n|^2\) (the Born rule).

The boundary conditions select which values of E are allowed (Board 9). For a particle trapped in a box, only certain energies can satisfy the condition \(u=0\) at the boundaries; the quantization of energy comes from this. Week 3 makes it concrete with potential wells.

Try it yourself: for a free particle (V = 0), is \(u(x)=\sin(kx)\) a solution, and what is its energy?

\(u''=-k^2\sin(kx)\). So \(\hat Hu=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(-k^2)\sin(kx)=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,u\). The eigenvalue equation holds, with \(E=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}=\frac{p^2}{2m}\): the classical kinetic energy of a free particle.

High school example

A guitar string. Because its two ends are fixed the string cannot make every vibration, only standing waves with nodes at the ends: \(\lambda_n=2L/n\). On a 65 cm string, \(\lambda_1=130\) cm, \(\lambda_2=65\) cm, \(\lambda_3\approx43\) cm. The boundary conditions select the allowed waves. A quantum particle in a box likewise allows only certain \(u_n\), and so only certain \(E_n\).

Board 13

The time part: a phase that rotates rather than grows

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe time equation is \(dT/dt=-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_nT\); its solution is \(T(t)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\), a complex number of constant length 1 rotating with angular speed \(E_n/\hbar\).

Set the left side equal to the same constant:

\[ \frac{i\hbar}{T}\,\frac{dT}{dt}=E_n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{E_n}{i\hbar}\,T=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\,E_n\,T \]

The last step used \(1/i=-i\) (since \(i\cdot(-i)=-i^2=1\)). A function whose derivative is proportional to itself has an exponential solution. Separate the variables and integrate:

\[ \int\frac{dT}{T}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_n\int dt\;\Longrightarrow\;\ln T=-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_nt+C\;\Longrightarrow\;T(t)=e^{C}\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar} \]

The board drops the constant \(e^C\); this is harmless, because that constant can be absorbed into the space part (normalization is set there anyway). Differentiate to check: \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}=-\frac{iE_n}{\hbar}e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\). It holds.

What does this number do? By Euler's formula, \(e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta\) and \(|e^{-i\theta}|=1\). As θ grows the point rotates clockwise on the unit circle and its length does not change. The angle is \(\theta=E_nt/\hbar\) and the angular speed \(\omega_n=E_n/\hbar\). The Planck link \(E=\hbar\omega\) from the reminder box has appeared here by itself.

Board 14

The stationary state: ψ rotates, \(|\psi|^2\) stands still

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentence\(\psi_n(t,x)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}u_n(x)\) is a stationary state: time enters only as a phase, so the probability density \(|\psi_n|^2=|u_n|^2\) does not change in time at all.

Combine the two parts. There is a separate solution for each n, and we name it \(\psi_n\) (the “redefining” on the board):

\[ \psi_n(t,x)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,u_n(x) \]

Compute the probability density. Taking the conjugate flips the sign of the phase and the two phases cancel:

\[ |\psi_n(t,x)|^2=\psi_n^{*}\psi_n=e^{+iE_nt/\hbar}\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,|u_n(x)|^2=|u_n(x)|^2 \]

This is where the name comes from: a stationary state. Careful: ψ itself is not standing still, its phase rotates. What stands still is everything measurable: the probability density, the expectation value of every time-independent operator, and the energy (a measurement gives exactly \(E_n\)). It is consistent with the continuity equation too: since \(\partial_t\rho=0\), \(\partial_xj=0\).

All the pieces of the week come together in one line. The Schrödinger equation is linear (Board 9), so a sum of stationary states is also a solution. Expand the initial state in energy eigenstates (Board 6) and attach to each piece its own phase:

\[ \psi(x,0)=\sum_n c_n\,u_n(x),\quad c_n=\langle u_n|\psi(0)\rangle\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \psi(t,x)=\sum_n c_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,u_n(x) \]

This is the recipe for time evolution of any initial state. But the mixture is not stationary. With two terms a cross term remains, and since the phases rotate at different speeds, that term oscillates:

\[ |\psi|^2=|c_1|^2|u_1|^2+|c_2|^2|u_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}\Big(c_1^{*}c_2\,u_1^{*}u_2\,e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar}\Big) \]

The angular frequency of the oscillation is \((E_2-E_1)/\hbar\). In the Bohr model the frequency of the light emitted by the atom is exactly this difference: \(h\nu=E_2-E_1\). Motion, “something changing”, comes from the interference of stationary states with different energies. Week 3 builds on this.
