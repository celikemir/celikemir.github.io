Board 1915747

Last week: every transformation has a generator

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceEvery continuous transformation is written as \(\hat U(\epsilon)=e^{-i\epsilon\hat G/\hbar}\), and the generator \(\hat G\) must be Hermitian. If the Hamiltonian commutes with \(\hat G\) this is a symmetry, and the Heisenberg equation says that \(\hat G\) is conserved.

The lecture opened with a two-board summary of last week. The first item: every unitary transformation depending on a continuous parameter (a shift, a rotation angle, an elapsed time) can be written as an exponential:

\[ \hat U(\epsilon)=e^{-i\epsilon\hat G/\hbar}=\hat I-\frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}\,\hat G+O(\epsilon^2) \]

We call \(\hat G\) the generator of the transformation. The note “must be Hermitian” on the board is last week's proof: for \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\) we need \(\hat G^\dagger=\hat G\) at first order. Being Hermitian, \(\hat G\) is an observable, a measurable quantity.

TransformationOperatorGeneratorConserved if there is a symmetry
Translation, Δx\(\hat T(\Delta x)=e^{-i\Delta x\,\hat p/\hbar}\)\(\hat p\)momentum
Rotation about the z axis, Δθ\(\hat R(\Delta\theta)=e^{-i\Delta\theta\,\hat L_z/\hbar}\)\(\hat L_z\)z component of angular momentum
Translation in time, Δt\(\hat U(\Delta t)=e^{-i\Delta t\,\hat H/\hbar}\)\(\hat H\)energy

On the board a small “kg·m/s” is written next to \(\hat p\). This is a unit check: the \(\Delta x\,\hat p/\hbar\) in the exponent must be dimensionless. m × kg·m/s = kg·m²/s, and the unit of ħ is J·s = kg·m²/s, so their ratio is dimensionless. The same rule holds for the other two: angle (radians, dimensionless) times angular momentum (J·s), time (s) times energy (J). The product of the parameter and the generator is always in units of ħ.

The second item: conservation and symmetry. If a transformation does not change the Hamiltonian (\(\hat U^\dagger\hat H\hat U=\hat H\)) we call it a symmetry; at first order this means \([\hat H,\hat G]=0\). The Heisenberg equation, assuming \(\hat G\) has no explicit time dependence:

\[ \frac{d\hat G}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,[\hat H,\hat G]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -i\hbar\,\frac{d\hat G}{dt}=[\hat H,\hat G] \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\frac{d\hat G}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,[\hat H,\hat G]\\[2pt]\Longleftrightarrow\;&-i\hbar\,\frac{d\hat G}{dt}=[\hat H,\hat G]\end{aligned} \]

The form on the right is written on the board. Multiplying both sides of the left one by \(-i\hbar\) gives the right one, because \((-i\hbar)\cdot\frac{i}{\hbar}=-i^2=1\). If there is a symmetry the right side is zero, so \(d\hat G/dt=0\) and \(\hat G\) is conserved. This is the quantum version of Noether's theorem: if space is the same everywhere momentum is conserved, if it is the same in every direction angular momentum is conserved, and if nothing changes in time energy is conserved.

The generator of translation in time is \(\hat H\) itself, and \([\hat H,\hat H]=0\) is always true. So if \(\hat H\) does not depend explicitly on time, energy is conserved automatically.

High school example

A figure skater spinning on the ice slows down when opening their arms and speeds up when closing them. The reason is the conservation of angular momentum, and it is conserved because the ice behaves the same in every direction. If you turned the skater a little on the rink the physics would not change. “It does not change when turned” is the symmetry and “angular momentum is constant” the conservation law; the two are two sides of the same coin.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: what is the link between symmetry and conservation?

If a transformation does not change the Hamiltonian, its generator \(\hat G\) commutes with the Hamiltonian: \([\hat H,\hat G]=0\). The Heisenberg equation ties the change of a quantity in time to exactly this commutator; if the commutator is zero the change is zero. Symmetry makes the commutator vanish, and a zero commutator means conservation.

Board 2

Commutator algebra: the difference of the orders

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe commutator is the difference between “A then B” and “B then A”. It is linear, and on products it expands like the product rule of the derivative; \([\hat x,\hat p^n]=i\hbar n\hat p^{\,n-1}\) and \([f(\hat x),\hat p]=i\hbar f'(\hat x)\) come from these rules.

Numbers commute: 3·5 = 5·3. Operators generally do not. The commutator is the operator measuring this difference:

\[ [\hat A,\hat B]\equiv\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A \]

If it is zero the order does not matter; if it is not zero it does. Immediately from the definition, \([\hat B,\hat A]=-[\hat A,\hat B]\) and \([\hat A,\hat A]=0\).

The first two properties on the board are linearity: a constant comes out (\(a\in\mathbb C\)) and a sum distributes. The third and fourth are the product rule:

\[ [\hat A\hat B,\hat C]=[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B+\hat A[\hat B,\hat C],\qquad[\hat A,\hat B\hat C]=[\hat A,\hat B]\hat C+\hat B[\hat A,\hat C] \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat A\hat B,\hat C]&=[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B+\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]\\[2pt][\hat A,\hat B\hat C]&=[\hat A,\hat B]\hat C+\hat B[\hat A,\hat C]\end{aligned} \]

The memory rule: the factor that does not enter the commutator stays in its place in the product. In iii, Â is on the left so it is on the left in the second term, and B̂ is on the right so it is on the right in the first term. The proof is expanding the right side:

\[ [\hat A,\hat C]\hat B+\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]=\hat A\hat C\hat B-\hat C\hat A\hat B+\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat A\hat C\hat B=\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat C\hat A\hat B \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&[\hat A,\hat C]\hat B+\hat A[\hat B,\hat C]\\&\;=\hat A\hat C\hat B-\hat C\hat A\hat B+\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat A\hat C\hat B\\&\;=\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat C\hat A\hat B\end{aligned} \]

The \(\hat A\hat C\hat B\) in the middle cancel; what remains is exactly \([\hat A\hat B,\hat C]\).

These rules and \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) give two results (last week's homework). v by induction, from iv:

\[ [\hat x,\hat p^{n}]=[\hat x,\hat p]\,\hat p^{n-1}+\hat p\,[\hat x,\hat p^{n-1}]=i\hbar\,\hat p^{n-1}+\hat p\cdot i\hbar(n-1)\,\hat p^{n-2}=i\hbar\,n\,\hat p^{n-1} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat x,\hat p^{n}]&=[\hat x,\hat p]\,\hat p^{n-1}+\hat p\,[\hat x,\hat p^{n-1}]\\&=i\hbar\,\hat p^{n-1}+\hat p\cdot i\hbar(n-1)\,\hat p^{n-2}\\&=i\hbar\,n\,\hat p^{n-1}\end{aligned} \]

For vi take a test function g(x), in the position representation \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\):

\[ [f(\hat x),\hat p]\,g=f\cdot(-i\hbar g')-(-i\hbar)(fg)'=-i\hbar fg'+i\hbar f'g+i\hbar fg'=i\hbar\,f'(x)\,g \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[f(\hat x),\hat p]\,g&=f\cdot(-i\hbar g')+i\hbar(fg)'\\&=-i\hbar fg'+i\hbar f'g+i\hbar fg'\\&=i\hbar\,f'(x)\,g\end{aligned} \]

In short, the commutator of x̂ and p̂ means “take the derivative”: when p̂ enters a commutator with a function of x, it produces the derivative of that function with respect to x. There is also the mirror version: \([\hat x,g(\hat p)]=i\hbar\,g'(\hat p)\), and v is its \(g=p^n\) case.

Why does it matter? Combine it with Board 1. For \(\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V(\hat x)\):

\[ \frac{d\hat x}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Big[\frac{\hat p^2}{2m},\hat x\Big]=\frac{\hat p}{m},\qquad\frac{d\hat p}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,[V(\hat x),\hat p]=-V'(\hat x) \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{d\hat x}{dt}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Big[\frac{\hat p^2}{2m},\hat x\Big]=\frac{\hat p}{m}\\[2pt]\frac{d\hat p}{dt}&=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,[V(\hat x),\hat p]=-V'(\hat x)\end{aligned} \]

Velocity = momentum / mass and force = −derivative of the potential: Newton's equations come back as operators (Ehrenfest's theorem). The first used \([\hat p^2,\hat x]=-[\hat x,\hat p^2]=-2i\hbar\hat p\), the second used vi.

High school example

The product rule of the derivative: \((uv)'=u'v+uv'\). Each time you differentiate one factor and leave the other in place; iii and iv have exactly this structure. Why does the order matter? Socks then shoes does not give the same result as shoes then socks. The commutator is the difference between these two results.

Board 3915747

Two commuting observables have a common eigenbasis

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIf \([\hat A,\hat B]=0\), there is a basis in which every vector is an eigenvector of both Â and B̂. We label these vectors with the two eigenvalues as \(|a,b\rangle\).

We know that the eigenvectors of a Hermitian operator form a basis and that the operator is diagonal in that basis. The new question: can we make two operators diagonal in the same basis? The answer on the board: if and only if they commute.

\[ [\hat A,\hat B]=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{common eigenbasis }\{|a,b\rangle\}:\quad\hat A|a,b\rangle=a|a,b\rangle,\quad\hat B|a,b\rangle=b|a,b\rangle \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&[\hat A,\hat B]=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{common eigenbasis }\{|a,b\rangle\}:\\&\hat A|a,b\rangle=a|a,b\rangle,\quad\hat B|a,b\rangle=b|a,b\rangle\end{aligned} \]

The easy direction (⇐). In the common basis both are diagonal, and diagonal matrices always commute: \(\mathrm{diag}(a_1,a_2)\,\mathrm{diag}(b_1,b_2)=\mathrm{diag}(a_1b_1,a_2b_2)\), in whichever order you multiply.

The main direction (⇒), the nondegenerate case. Let each eigenvalue have a single eigenvector and let \(\hat A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle\). Apply B̂ to this vector, then apply Â to the result:

\[ \hat A\big(\hat B|a\rangle\big)=\hat B\hat A|a\rangle=a\,\big(\hat B|a\rangle\big) \]

In the first equality we swapped the order; \([\hat A,\hat B]=0\) is needed exactly here. The result: \(\hat B|a\rangle\) is also an eigenvector of Â with eigenvalue a. There is a single direction for a, so \(\hat B|a\rangle\) must be along it: \(\hat B|a\rangle=b|a\rangle\). So |a⟩ is already an eigenvector of B̂ too.

The degenerate case. If an a has several eigenvectors, the same lines say this: B̂ takes the eigen-subspace of a into itself and does not carry it outside. Inside that subspace B̂ is a Hermitian matrix; diagonalize it there. The resulting vectors both keep a and are eigenvectors of B̂. An example:

\[ \hat A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix} \]

They commute: B̂ is block diagonal and the first block of Â is the identity matrix. The eigenvalue a = 1 of Â is twofold; every combination of (1,0,0) and (0,1,0) gives a = 1. But (1,0,0) is not an eigenvector of B̂: B̂ takes it to (0,1,0). Diagonalize B̂ in the two-dimensional block:

\[ |1,1\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0),\qquad|1,-1\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0),\qquad|2,5\rangle=(0,0,1) \]
\[ \begin{aligned}|1,1\rangle&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\\|1,-1\rangle&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)\\|2,5\rangle&=(0,0,1)\end{aligned} \]

The label is (a, b). Saying just “a = 1” was not enough to tell the two states apart; adding b gives each basis vector its own label. The idea of a “complete set” on Board 4 comes from here.

Physical meaning. Measure Â and get a; the state collapses to the subspace of a. Then measure B̂: since B̂ does not take the state out of the subspace, the value a is not disturbed. Measure Â again and you get a again. It is not so for x̂ and p̂: a momentum measurement erases the position information.

High school example

Compatible: height and weight. Measuring your height does not change your weight; the two numbers together form a label describing you. Incompatible: a photo of a fast car. With a short exposure the car comes out sharp and you know where it is, but you cannot see its speed. With a long exposure it leaves a streak and you see its speed, but where it is becomes blurry. You cannot get both sharp in one photo.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: where in the proof was \([\hat A,\hat B]=0\) used?

When showing that \(\hat B|a\rangle\) is still an eigenvector of Â with eigenvalue a, we wrote \(\hat A\hat B=\hat B\hat A\). If they did not commute, B̂ could turn an eigenvector of Â into a mixture of other eigenvalues and there would be no common basis.

Board 4

The complete set of labels and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIf you collect enough commuting observables, each basis state is identified on its own by its list of values (a complete set); in hydrogen this list is (n, l, m, mₛ). Then 9.3 begins: for any two kets \(|\langle u|v\rangle|^2\le\langle u|u\rangle\langle v|v\rangle\).

The first sentence of the board is the physical counterpart of Board 3: the values of compatible observables can be determined at the same time with an ideal measurement. In a common eigenstate the uncertainty of both measurements is zero.

The starred item: a complete set (complete set of commuting observables, CSCO). The eigenvalue of a single observable is often degenerate, with many states at the same value. As you add new commuting observables the list of labels grows; you continue until each basis state is determined by a single list.

The hydrogen example. The energy depends only on n, \(E_n=-13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2\). How many states are in the n = 2 shell? l = 0 (1 state) and l = 1 (3 states with m = −1, 0, 1), two each with spin: 2 × 4 = 8. Ĥ alone cannot separate these 8 states. The labels:

OperatorEigenvalueLabel
\(\hat H\)\(-13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2\)n = 1, 2, 3, …
\(\hat L^2\)\(\hbar^2\,l(l+1)\)l = 0, 1, …, n − 1
\(\hat L_z\)\(\hbar\,m\)m = −l, …, l
\(\hat S_z\)\(\hbar\,m_s\)\(m_s=\pm\tfrac12\)

The four commute with each other and (n, l, m, mₛ) determines each state on its own. The “spin operators” on the board in practice mean \(\hat S_z\); \(\hat S^2\) is \(\tfrac34\hbar^2\) in every state for an electron and adds no distinguishing label. You cannot add \(\hat L_x\) to the set: it does not commute with \(\hat L_z\), and there is no basis diagonalizing both.

Then 9.3 begins. The goal is the uncertainty relation, but first we need a tool, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\[ |\langle u|v\rangle|^2\le\langle u|u\rangle\,\langle v|v\rangle \]

You saw this for real vectors in high school: \(\vec u\cdot\vec v=|\vec u||\vec v|\cos\theta\) and \(|\cos\theta|\le1\), so \((\vec u\cdot\vec v)^2\le|\vec u|^2|\vec v|^2\). In a complex Hilbert space there is no angle but the inequality still holds; Boards 5 and 6 prove it. Equality holds only if the two vectors are along the same line (\(|u\rangle=\alpha|v\rangle\)). This fact will be needed on Board 11 when finding minimum uncertainty states.

High school example

Finding Ali at school: say “Ali” and 5 people turn up, “Ali in class 10-B” and 2 people, “Ali in 10-B with number 123” and one person. Name, class and number are a complete set; in hydrogen, n, l, m, mₛ. Class and number do not disturb each other; you can state both at the same time.

Board 5915747

Proof of Cauchy–Schwarz: a squared length cannot be negative

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceWhatever α you choose, the squared length of \(|u\rangle-\alpha|v\rangle\) cannot be negative. Expanding this expression into four terms and choosing α as the projection coefficient \(\langle v|u\rangle/\langle v|v\rangle\) gives Cauchy–Schwarz.

The only foundation of the proof is the positive definiteness of the inner product. For every ket:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle\ge0,\qquad\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=0\iff|\psi\rangle=0 \]

The parenthesis on the right of the board says this: \(\langle u|u\rangle\) is a squared length, \(|u|^2\); a squared length cannot be negative.

The trick is to build a new ket from the two kets. Let α be any complex number:

\[ |\psi\rangle\equiv|u\rangle-\alpha|v\rangle,\qquad\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\ge0\quad(*) \]

The rule stated in the lecture when writing the bra: the Hermitian conjugate turns kets into bras and numbers into their conjugates. The number α becomes \(\alpha^*\):

\[ \langle\psi|=\langle u|-\alpha^*\langle v| \]

A classmate asked in the lecture “why is there a conjugate in the first parenthesis and not in the second?”. In ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ the left is the bra and the right is the ket; the conjugate is taken only on going to the bra, and the ket stays as it is. Multiply the two parentheses term by term:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\big(\langle u|-\alpha^*\langle v|\big)\big(|u\rangle-\alpha|v\rangle\big)=\langle u|u\rangle-\alpha^*\langle v|u\rangle-\alpha\langle u|v\rangle+|\alpha|^2\langle v|v\rangle \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle&=\big(\langle u|-\alpha^*\langle v|\big)\big(|u\rangle-\alpha|v\rangle\big)\\&=\langle u|u\rangle-\alpha^*\langle v|u\rangle\\&\quad-\alpha\langle u|v\rangle+|\alpha|^2\langle v|v\rangle\end{aligned} \]

The last term is \((-\alpha^*)(-\alpha)\langle v|v\rangle=|\alpha|^2\langle v|v\rangle\). On the board this term is written with \(\langle v|u\rangle\); the correct form is \(\langle v|v\rangle\) (see the note below). This expression is ≥ 0 for every α. Now choose α cleverly:

\[ \alpha=\frac{\langle v|u\rangle}{\langle v|v\rangle} \]

Why this one? Three routes give the same answer:

  1. Projection. \(\alpha|v\rangle\) is the shadow of |u⟩ along v. With this choice |ψ⟩ stays orthogonal to v: \(\langle v|\psi\rangle=\langle v|u\rangle-\alpha\langle v|v\rangle=0\). |u⟩ = (part along v) + (part orthogonal to v).
  2. Smallest value. ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ is a parabola in α. Treating α and α* as independent variables, set \(\partial/\partial\alpha^*\) to zero: \(-\langle v|u\rangle+\alpha\langle v|v\rangle=0\), the same α again. The inequality holds for every α; it gives the most information at the smallest value.
  3. Pythagoras. The squared length of the orthogonal part = the total squared length − the squared length of the projection. On the next board we will find exactly this.

If \(\langle v|v\rangle=0\), α is undefined. But then |v⟩ = 0 and both sides of the inequality are 0, so it is already true; the proof is done for \(|v\rangle\neq0\).

The Robertson uncertainty relation

We write the variance as the squared length of a vector and feed it into Cauchy–Schwarz. With the commutator giving the imaginary part and the anticommutator the real part, the Heisenberg relation comes out.

Board 7915747

The Robertson relation and the deviation from the mean operator

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe goal is \((\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge\frac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2\). For the proof, “deviation from the mean” operators \(\delta\hat A=\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I\) are defined; \(|u\rangle=\delta\hat A|\psi\rangle\) and \(|v\rangle=\delta\hat B|\psi\rangle\) are the two kets that go into Cauchy–Schwarz.

First the goal at the very top of the board:

\[ (\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\ge\frac{|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2}{4} \]

It reads: the product of the uncertainties of two observables is bounded from below by the expectation value of their commutator. If the commutator is zero the bound is zero and the two can be sharp together (Board 3). If not, you cannot sharpen both at once.

Then the definitions. All are taken in the same state |ψ⟩:

\[ (\Delta\hat A)^2=\langle\hat A^2\rangle-\langle\hat A\rangle^2=\big\langle(\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I)^2\big\rangle=\langle\psi|(\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I)^2|\psi\rangle \]
\[ \begin{aligned}(\Delta\hat A)^2&=\langle\hat A^2\rangle-\langle\hat A\rangle^2\\&=\big\langle(\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I)^2\big\rangle\\&=\langle\psi|(\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I)^2|\psi\rangle\end{aligned} \]

\(\langle\hat A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle\) is the expectation value: the average of the measurement results on many identically prepared copies. \((\Delta\hat A)^2\) is the variance and \(\Delta\hat A\) the standard deviation, the spread of the results around the average. Expand to see the second equality (⟨Â⟩ is a number, call it a):

\[ \langle(\hat A-a)^2\rangle=\langle\hat A^2\rangle-2a\langle\hat A\rangle+a^2=\langle\hat A^2\rangle-a^2 \]

An important distinction: ΔÂ is not the error of the device or the disturbance of the system by the measurement, but the state's own spread. It is the same with a perfect device. This is called preparation uncertainty.

The parenthesis on the right is the setup of the proof. The deviation operators:

\[ \delta\hat A\equiv\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\hat I,\qquad\delta\hat B\equiv\hat B-\langle\hat B\rangle\hat I \]

⟨Â⟩ is a number; multiplied by the identity operator it becomes an operator. It has three properties:

  • \(\delta\hat A\) is Hermitian. Since Â is Hermitian, ⟨Â⟩ is real, a real number times Î is also Hermitian, and a difference of Hermitian operators is Hermitian.
  • \(\langle\delta\hat A\rangle=\langle\hat A\rangle-\langle\hat A\rangle=0\). The average of the deviation is zero.
  • \(\langle(\delta\hat A)^2\rangle=(\Delta\hat A)^2\). The variance is the average of the squared deviation.

The last step is defining the two kets:

\[ |u\rangle\equiv\delta\hat A|\psi\rangle,\qquad|v\rangle\equiv\delta\hat B|\psi\rangle \]

Their squared lengths are exactly the variances: \(\langle u|u\rangle=\langle\psi|\delta\hat A^\dagger\delta\hat A|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|(\delta\hat A)^2|\psi\rangle=(\Delta\hat A)^2\). The first step used \(\delta\hat A^\dagger=\delta\hat A\). Once the variance is written as the squared length of a vector we can use Cauchy–Schwarz; that is the whole idea.

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceEvery product is written as \(\hat A\hat B=\frac12[\hat A,\hat B]+\frac12\{\hat A,\hat B\}\). The commutator is anti-Hermitian, with a purely imaginary expectation value; the anticommutator is Hermitian, with a real expectation value. This separates the imaginary and real parts of \(\langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle\).

The top line is now correct, with the /4 erased. The decomposition is written below it. The anticommutator is \(\{\hat A,\hat B\}\equiv\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A\). Add it to the commutator and divide by two:

\[ \frac12(\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A)+\frac12(\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A)=\hat A\hat B \]

The \(\hat B\hat A\) cancel. This is just adding 0, but the two parts behave very differently. Take the dagger; the rule at the bottom right of the board is \((\hat A\hat B)^\dagger=\hat B^\dagger\hat A^\dagger=\hat B\hat A\) (for Hermitian operators):

\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat A,\hat B]^\dagger&=\hat B\hat A-\hat A\hat B=-[\hat A,\hat B]&&\text{anti-Hermitian}\\\{\hat A,\hat B\}^\dagger&=\hat B\hat A+\hat A\hat B=\{\hat A,\hat B\}&&\text{Hermitian}\end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat A,\hat B]^\dagger&=-[\hat A,\hat B]\\[-2pt]&\scriptstyle\text{anti-Hermitian}\\\{\hat A,\hat B\}^\dagger&=\{\hat A,\hat B\}\\[-2pt]&\scriptstyle\text{Hermitian}\end{aligned} \]

Why it matters: whether an operator is Hermitian determines whether its expectation value is real or imaginary. Since \(\langle\psi|\hat K|\psi\rangle^*=\langle\psi|\hat K^\dagger|\psi\rangle\):

  • Hermitian (\(\hat K^\dagger=\hat K\)): \(\langle K\rangle^*=\langle K\rangle\), real.
  • Anti-Hermitian (\(\hat K^\dagger=-\hat K\)): \(\langle K\rangle^*=-\langle K\rangle\), purely imaginary.

Example: \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\hat I\). \((i\hbar)^*=-i\hbar\), indeed anti-Hermitian, and its expectation value iħ is purely imaginary. In general every anti-Hermitian operator is i times a Hermitian operator: \([\hat A,\hat B]=i\hat C\), \(\hat C^\dagger=\hat C\).

Do the same decomposition with the δ's:

\[ \delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B=\frac12[\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B]+\frac12\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\} \]

In the commutator the δ's drop out: \([\hat A-a,\hat B-b]=[\hat A,\hat B]\), because a and b are numbers and numbers commute with everything. The lecture put it as “the scalar parts naturally commute”. In the anticommutator they do not drop; \(\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\) still depends on the averages. The result:

\[ \langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle=\underbrace{\tfrac12\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle}_{\text{purely imaginary}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle}_{\text{real}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle&=\tfrac12\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\;\;(\text{imaginary})\\&\quad+\tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle\;\;(\text{real})\end{aligned} \]
Board note

The arrow and check mark under δÂ mean “δÂ is Hermitian”; the product \(\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\) is not Hermitian (Board 8). Of the two “Hermitian” labels on the board, one belongs to δÂ and the other to the anticommutator.

High school example

Every function is the sum of an even and an odd part: \(f(x)=\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}{2}+\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}{2}\). Example: \(e^x=\cosh x+\sinh x\). Schrödinger–Robertson, Robertson and Heisenberg

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceAdding the squares of the imaginary and real parts gives the Schrödinger–Robertson relation; dropping the nonnegative real part gives Robertson, and for x̂ and p̂, \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\). The uncertainty principle is not a separate assumption but a consequence of Hilbert space.

The very top of the board recalls the foundation of the whole derivation: ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ ≥ 0. Combine Boards 8 and 9:

\[ (\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\ge\Big|\underbrace{\tfrac12\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle}_{i\,y}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle}_{x}\Big|^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&(\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\\&\;\ge\Big|\tfrac12\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle+\tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle\Big|^2\end{aligned} \]

The squared modulus of a complex number: \(|x+iy|^2=(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2+y^2\). The cross terms (\(-ixy+ixy\)) cancel, because one is real and the other imaginary. The lecture said exactly this: the anti-Hermitian part gives the imaginary part and the Hermitian part the real part, and for the modulus we add their squares.

\[ \boxed{(\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\ge\frac14\big|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\big|^2+\frac14\big|\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle\big|^2} \]
\[ \boxed{\begin{aligned}&(\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\\&\;\ge\frac14\big|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\big|^2+\frac14\big|\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle\big|^2\end{aligned}} \]

This is the Schrödinger–Robertson relation. The meaning of the second term:

\[ \tfrac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle=\tfrac12\langle\{\hat A,\hat B\}\rangle-\langle\hat A\rangle\langle\hat B\rangle \]

This is the covariance of Â and B̂: nonzero if the results of the two measurements rise and fall together. The covariance term is the modulus of a square, so it is ≥ 0. If you drop it the right side gets smaller and the inequality stays true. This step was asked about in the lecture: if A ≥ B + C and C ≥ 0, then A ≥ B. The weaker but more useful form:

\[ \boxed{(\Delta\hat A)^2(\Delta\hat B)^2\ge\frac14\big|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\big|^2}\qquad\text{Robertson} \]

Now Â = x̂, B̂ = p̂. \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) is a number (times Î), its expectation value is iħ in every state, and its squared modulus is ħ²:

\[ (\Delta x)^2(\Delta p)^2\ge\frac14\hbar^2\;\Longrightarrow\;\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\frac{\hbar}{2} \]

In the last step we took the square root, since both sides are positive. The last sentence of the board: Heisenberg's uncertainty relation comes out as a property of Hilbert space. No separate postulate is needed; the positivity of the inner product (Cauchy–Schwarz) and the canonical commutator \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) are enough.

Two subtle points that may be asked in the exam:

  • For x̂, p̂ the bound is the same in every state, ħ/2, because the commutator is a number. In general the bound depends on the state. For spin \([\hat S_x,\hat S_y]=i\hbar\hat S_z\), so \(\Delta S_x\,\Delta S_y\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}|\langle\hat S_z\rangle|\). In the state |↑⟩ the bound is ħ²/4 and is met with equality; in a state polarized along x, ⟨Ŝz⟩ = 0, the bound is 0, and indeed ΔSx = 0.
  • Robertson is a lower bound, and the true value is generally larger. If the covariance is not zero, Robertson cannot be met with equality but Schrödinger–Robertson can. The plot below shows this.

Minimum uncertainty: the circle and the squeezed ellipse

In one sentenceThe equality \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\) holds only if the wave function is a Gaussian with no phase slope. These are the states with the smallest uncertainty area in phase space; a circle is a coherent state, and an ellipse flattened in one direction a squeezed state.

Robertson is a lower bound. Which states sit exactly on that bound? Two conditions follow from Boards 6 and 10, and both must hold:

  1. Cauchy–Schwarz equality: \(|v\rangle\propto|u\rangle\), that is, \(\delta\hat B|\psi\rangle=c\,\delta\hat A|\psi\rangle\) for some number c (Board 6).
  2. The dropped covariance term is zero: \(\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle=0\) (Board 10).

The second condition constrains c. From the first condition \(\langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle=\langle u|v\rangle=c\,\langle u|u\rangle=c\,(\Delta A)^2\); its real part is \(\frac12\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle=\mathrm{Re}(c)\,(\Delta A)^2\). For it to vanish c must be purely imaginary: c = iλ, with λ real.

Solve for x̂ and p̂ (\(x_0=\langle\hat x\rangle\), \(p_0=\langle\hat p\rangle\)). In the position representation \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\):

\[ \Big(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}-p_0\Big)\psi=i\lambda\,(x-x_0)\,\psi\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{d\psi}{dx}=\Big[\frac{ip_0}{\hbar}-\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}(x-x_0)\Big]\psi \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\Big(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}-p_0\Big)\psi=i\lambda\,(x-x_0)\,\psi\\\Longrightarrow\;&\frac{d\psi}{dx}=\Big[\frac{ip_0}{\hbar}-\frac{\lambda}{\hbar}(x-x_0)\Big]\psi\end{aligned} \]

A first order equation; its solution:

\[ \psi(x)=N\exp\!\Big[-\frac{\lambda}{2\hbar}(x-x_0)^2+\frac{ip_0x}{\hbar}\Big],\qquad(\Delta x)^2=\frac{\hbar}{2\lambda},\quad(\Delta p)^2=\frac{\hbar\lambda}{2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\psi(x)=N\exp\!\Big[-\frac{\lambda}{2\hbar}(x-x_0)^2+\frac{ip_0x}{\hbar}\Big]\\&(\Delta x)^2=\frac{\hbar}{2\lambda},\qquad(\Delta p)^2=\frac{\hbar\lambda}{2}\end{aligned} \]

To be normalizable, λ > 0. The result: a Gaussian wave packet centered at x₀ with average momentum p₀; λ selects the width, and for every λ, \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\). No shape other than a Gaussian satisfies the equality. If you add a phase slope \(e^{i\kappa x^2}\) to a Gaussian (the plot on Board 10), covariance appears and the equality breaks.

The quantum model of laser light is a coherent state.

The harmonic oscillator: evenly spaced steps

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe Hamiltonian of the harmonic oscillator is \(\hat p^2/2m+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2\). Its eigenstates are |n⟩ (number or Fock states), with energies \(\hbar\omega(n+\frac12)\): evenly spaced, and the lowest is not zero. Dividing by ħω makes the problem dimensionless.

Chapter 10 begins. Every Hamiltonian is kinetic + potential; the kinetic part is always \(\hat p^2/2m\) and the potential depends on the system (“problem/sys. dep.” on the board). For the harmonic oscillator the potential is the spring potential \(\frac12kx^2\), \(k=m\omega^2\):

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2 \]

Why is it so important? In the Taylor expansion of any potential around a minimum, the first meaningful term is quadratic; small vibrations are always harmonic. Molecular vibrations, phonons in a crystal and a light mode in an optical cavity are written with the same mathematics. In week 3 we saw the oscillator in the language of wave functions; this chapter will solve the same problem with operators alone.

The result is given on the board; its proof will come with ladder operators:

\[ \hat H|n\rangle=\underbrace{\hbar\omega\Big(n+\frac12\Big)}_{E_n}|n\rangle,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots \]
  • The steps are evenly spaced: \(E_{n+1}-E_n=\hbar\omega\). Going up one step adds one quantum of energy (ħω). |n⟩ is called a number or Fock state, because n answers “how many quanta are there”; in a light mode n is the number of photons.
  • n = 0 is the ground state and its energy is not zero: \(E_0=\frac12\hbar\omega\), the zero-point energy.
  • ħω is the unit of energy. On the right of the board: dividing both sides by ħω gives \(\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}|n\rangle=\big(n+\frac12\big)|n\rangle\), with everything a dimensionless number. Board 13 works with this dimensionless Hamiltonian.

You can show with this week's result why the ground energy cannot be zero. \(\langle\hat p^2\rangle\ge(\Delta p)^2\), \(\langle\hat x^2\rangle\ge(\Delta x)^2\) and \(\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\Delta x\):

\[ E=\frac{\langle\hat p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\langle\hat x^2\rangle\ge\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\ge\frac{\hbar\omega}{2} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}E&=\frac{\langle\hat p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\langle\hat x^2\rangle\\&\ge\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\\&\ge\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\end{aligned} \]

The last step: the product of the two positive terms is constant (\(\hbar^2\omega^2/16\)); their sum is smallest when the terms are equal (arithmetic-geometric mean), \((\Delta x)^2=\hbar/2m\omega\), with sum ħω/2. The particle cannot be both exactly at the bottom (x = 0) and exactly at rest (p = 0); the zero-point energy is the price of the uncertainty principle. The bound comes out exactly equal to E₀, so the ground state is a minimum uncertainty state (the circle on Board 11).

Check: the first square is \(\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\hat x^2=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\cdot\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2\), the second \(\frac{\hat p^2}{2m\hbar\omega}=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\cdot\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}\).

Shorthand: \(\alpha=\sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\), \(\beta=1/\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}\). The units were checked in the lecture: the unit of \(m\omega/\hbar\) is kg·s⁻¹ / (kg·m²·s⁻¹) = 1/m², so \(\alpha\hat x\) is dimensionless; \(m\hbar\omega\) = kg × J = (kg·m/s)², so \(\beta\hat p\) is dimensionless too. The two things being added must have the same unit; both are unitless.

For numbers a sum of two squares factors: \(u^2+v^2=(u-iv)(u+iv)=|u+iv|^2\). This is the “complex number and its conjugate” analogy from the lecture. Let us try it with operators:

\[ \hat a=\alpha\hat x+i\beta\hat p,\qquad\hat a^\dagger=\alpha\hat x-i\beta\hat p \]

In â† the sign of i flips, because x̂ and p̂ are Hermitian and α and β real. â is not Hermitian (â† ≠ â), so it is not an observable on its own. Multiply:

\[ \hat a^\dagger\hat a=(\alpha\hat x-i\beta\hat p)(\alpha\hat x+i\beta\hat p)=\alpha^2\hat x^2+\beta^2\hat p^2+i\alpha\beta\,(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x) \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat a^\dagger\hat a&=(\alpha\hat x-i\beta\hat p)(\alpha\hat x+i\beta\hat p)\\&=\alpha^2\hat x^2+\beta^2\hat p^2+i\alpha\beta\,(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x)\end{aligned} \]

For numbers the last parenthesis would be zero. Here \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\): \(i\alpha\beta\cdot i\hbar=-\alpha\beta\hbar\) and \(\alpha\beta=\frac{1}{2\hbar}\), that is, −½:

\[ \hat a^\dagger\hat a=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\frac12\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}=\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12 \]

The note “comes from [x̂,p̂] = iħ” on the board is this ½, the algebraic source of the zero-point energy. \(\hat N\equiv\hat a^\dagger\hat a\) is the number operator, Hermitian: \((\hat a^\dagger\hat a)^\dagger=\hat a^\dagger\hat a\). Compare with Board 12: if \(\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}|n\rangle=(n+\frac12)|n\rangle\), then

\[ \hat N|n\rangle=\hat a^\dagger\hat a\,|n\rangle=n\,|n\rangle \]

The eigenvalue of N̂ is exactly the quantum number. It cannot be negative either: \(\langle n|\hat a^\dagger\hat a|n\rangle=\|\hat a|n\rangle\|^2\ge0\). This week's main tool, a squared length not being negative, works here too; n ≥ 0 comes from this.

Board note

The board says \(\hat H=\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\) and \(\hat H=\hat N+\frac12\); here Ĥ means the dimensionless \(\hat H/\hbar\omega\). The form with units is \(\hat H=\hbar\omega\big(\hat N+\frac12\big)\). Do not forget ħω in the exam.

Next lecture (not on the board): \([\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1\) and the ladder
\[ [\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=[\alpha\hat x+i\beta\hat p,\;\alpha\hat x-i\beta\hat p]=-i\alpha\beta[\hat x,\hat p]+i\alpha\beta[\hat p,\hat x]=-2i\alpha\beta\cdot i\hbar=2\alpha\beta\hbar=1 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]&=-i\alpha\beta[\hat x,\hat p]+i\alpha\beta[\hat p,\hat x]\\&=-2i\alpha\beta\cdot i\hbar=2\alpha\beta\hbar=1\end{aligned} \]

From this come \([\hat N,\hat a]=-\hat a\) and \([\hat N,\hat a^\dagger]=\hat a^\dagger\) (with iii). The meaning: â lowers one step and â† raises one step: \(\hat a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle\), \(\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle\), and at the very bottom \(\hat a|0\rangle=0\). This is where the name ladder comes from. Inverting the definitions:

\[ \hat x=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger),\qquad\hat p=i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2}}\,(\hat a^\dagger-\hat a) \]
High school example

In high school \(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\). With complex numbers \(a^2+b^2=(a-ib)(a+ib)\). With operators the order of multiplication matters, so expanding gives \((a-ib)(a+ib)=a^2+b^2+i(ab-ba)\), and the last term is not zero. The whole difference is in that term.

1. Compute \([\hat x^2,\hat p^2]\) and write the result in a single ordering.

With iii from Board 2: \([\hat x\hat x,\hat p^2]=\hat x[\hat x,\hat p^2]+[\hat x,\hat p^2]\hat x\). From v, \([\hat x,\hat p^2]=2i\hbar\hat p\):

\[ [\hat x^2,\hat p^2]=2i\hbar(\hat x\hat p+\hat p\hat x)=2i\hbar(2\hat x\hat p-i\hbar)=4i\hbar\,\hat x\hat p+2\hbar^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}[\hat x^2,\hat p^2]&=2i\hbar(\hat x\hat p+\hat p\hat x)\\&=2i\hbar(2\hat x\hat p-i\hbar)\\&=4i\hbar\,\hat x\hat p+2\hbar^2\end{aligned} \]

Check: the commutator of two Hermitian operators must be anti-Hermitian. \(\big(2i\hbar\{\hat x,\hat p\}\big)^\dagger=-2i\hbar\{\hat x,\hat p\}\). It holds.

2. For \(\hat A=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)\) and the \(\hat B\) of Board 3: show that they commute, find the common eigenbasis and say whether the eigenvalues of Â alone label the basis.

B̂ is block diagonal: the first block is \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) and the second block is 5. The first block of Â is the identity matrix, which commutes with everything; the second blocks are numbers. So \(\hat A\hat B=\hat B\hat A\).

a = 2 is nondegenerate: (0,0,1), b = 5. a = 1 is twofold; diagonalize B̂ in that block: eigenvalues ±1, eigenvectors \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\pm1,0)\).

The common basis: |1,1⟩, |1,−1⟩, |2,5⟩. The eigenvalues of Â alone are not enough (a = 1 belongs to two states); {Â, B̂} is a complete set.

3. Prove that \(|\langle u|v\rangle|^2\le\langle u|u\rangle\langle v|v\rangle\) for every |u⟩, |v⟩ in Hilbert space; say when equality holds.

If |v⟩ = 0, both sides are 0. Otherwise \(|\psi\rangle=|u\rangle-\alpha|v\rangle\), \(\alpha=\langle v|u\rangle/\langle v|v\rangle\). Positive definiteness:

\[ 0\le\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\langle u|u\rangle-\alpha^*\langle v|u\rangle-\alpha\langle u|v\rangle+|\alpha|^2\langle v|v\rangle=\langle u|u\rangle-\frac{|\langle v|u\rangle|^2}{\langle v|v\rangle} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}0\le\langle\psi|\psi\rangle&=\langle u|u\rangle-\alpha^*\langle v|u\rangle\\&\quad-\alpha\langle u|v\rangle+|\alpha|^2\langle v|v\rangle\\&=\langle u|u\rangle-\frac{|\langle v|u\rangle|^2}{\langle v|v\rangle}\end{aligned} \]

Multiply by ⟨v|v⟩ > 0, \(|\langle v|u\rangle|=|\langle u|v\rangle|\). Equality ⟺ ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 0 ⟺ \(|u\rangle=\alpha|v\rangle\).

4. For two Hermitian operators, derive \((\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge\frac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2\) and obtain \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\).

\(\delta\hat A=\hat A-\langle\hat A\rangle\), \(\delta\hat B=\hat B-\langle\hat B\rangle\) (Hermitian). Cauchy–Schwarz with \(|u\rangle=\delta\hat A|\psi\rangle\), \(|v\rangle=\delta\hat B|\psi\rangle\): \((\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge|\langle\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B\rangle|^2\).

\(\delta\hat A\,\delta\hat B=\frac12[\hat A,\hat B]+\frac12\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\); the first is anti-Hermitian (imaginary expectation value), the second Hermitian (real):

\[ (\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2\ge\tfrac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2+\tfrac14|\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle|^2\ge\tfrac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2&\ge\tfrac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2+\tfrac14|\langle\{\delta\hat A,\delta\hat B\}\rangle|^2\\&\ge\tfrac14|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|^2\end{aligned} \]

\([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) ⇒ the right side is ħ²/4 ⇒ \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\).

5. For \(\psi(x)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}\), compute Δx and Δp; is Robertson met with equality?

\(|\psi|^2\) is a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance σ²: \(\langle x\rangle=0\), \(\Delta x=\sigma\).

ψ is real ⇒ \(\langle p\rangle=0\). \(\psi'=-\frac{x}{2\sigma^2}\psi\), \(\langle p^2\rangle=\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2dx=\frac{\hbar^2}{4\sigma^4}\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar^2}{4\sigma^2}\), so \(\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2\sigma}\).

\(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\): equality. With the λ of Board 11, \(\lambda=\hbar/2\sigma^2\).

6. \(\hat S_x=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\hat S_y=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\hat S_z=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\). Check Robertson in the state \(|\!\uparrow\rangle=(1,0)\).

\([\hat S_x,\hat S_y]=i\hbar\hat S_z\) (by multiplying the matrices). \(\langle\hat S_z\rangle=\hbar/2\), and the right side is \(\frac14|i\hbar\cdot\frac\hbar2|^2=\frac{\hbar^4}{16}\).

\(\hat S_x^2=\hat S_y^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\hat I\), \(\langle\hat S_x\rangle=\langle\hat S_y\rangle=0\) ⇒ \((\Delta S_x)^2=(\Delta S_y)^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\). The product is \(\frac{\hbar^4}{16}\): equality.

Compare: in the state \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\), \(\langle\hat S_z\rangle=0\), the bound is 0, and indeed \(\Delta S_x=0\). The bound depends on the state.

7. Show that \(\hat H/\hbar\omega=\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\frac12\) and \([\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1\); find the lower bound \(E\ge\hbar\omega/2\) from the uncertainty principle.

With α and β as on Board 13, \(\alpha\beta=1/2\hbar\):

\[ \hat a^\dagger\hat a=\alpha^2\hat x^2+\beta^2\hat p^2+i\alpha\beta[\hat x,\hat p]=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\alpha\beta\hbar=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\frac12 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat a^\dagger\hat a&=\alpha^2\hat x^2+\beta^2\hat p^2+i\alpha\beta[\hat x,\hat p]\\&=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\alpha\beta\hbar=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\frac12\end{aligned} \]
\[ [\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=-2i\alpha\beta[\hat x,\hat p]=2\alpha\beta\hbar=1 \]

For the energy, ⟨p̂²⟩ ≥ (Δp)², ⟨x̂²⟩ ≥ (Δx)² and Δp ≥ ħ/2Δx:

\[ E\ge\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\ge\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}E&\ge\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\\&\ge\frac{\hbar^2}{8m(\Delta x)^2}+\frac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2\end{aligned} \]

The product of the two terms is \(\hbar^2\omega^2/16\); the sum is at least \(2\sqrt{\hbar^2\omega^2/16}=\hbar\omega/2\). Equality is at \((\Delta x)^2=\hbar/2m\omega\), in the ground state.
