Board 1241473

A ket is a list, an operator is a table

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe moment you choose a basis, an abstract ket becomes a list of numbers and an operator a table of numbers; \(\hat A|\psi\rangle=|\phi\rangle\) becomes ordinary matrix multiplication.

Last week we worked with abstract kets like \(|\psi\rangle\). You cannot calculate with something abstract; you need numbers. The way to numbers is to choose a basis, which is the “finite dimensional (N-dimensional) orthonormal basis” on the board: \(\{|e_n\rangle\}\), \(n=1,2,\dots,N\). Orthonormal means every direction has length 1 and the directions are perpendicular: \(\langle e_m|e_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\).

Then we write how much of each direction every ket contains:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|e_n\rangle,\qquad c_n=\langle e_n|\psi\rangle \]

Finding \(c_n\) means taking the shadow of \(|\psi\rangle\) on the nth direction. Written one below the other these numbers form the column vector \(\vec c_e\); this is the note “rows of \(\vec c_e\) vector” on the board. The subscript e means “written in the e basis”.

An operator also becomes a table: \(A_{mn}=\langle e_m|\hat A|e_n\rangle\). The index in the bra is the row and the index in the ket the column. So:

\[ \hat A|\psi\rangle=|\phi\rangle\;\Longrightarrow\;\overleftrightarrow{A}\,\vec c=\vec d,\qquad d_m=\sum_n A_{mn}\,c_n \]

An N×N table times an N×1 list gives an N×1 list again. The lecturer's arrows: \(\vec c\) a right arrow, a column (N×1); \(\overleftarrow{c}\) a left arrow, a row (1×N); \(\overleftrightarrow{A}\) a double arrow, a table (N×N).

High school example

You leave school and walk 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north. “East” and “north” are your basis and (3, 4) are your components. Now think of an operation: “double the east steps and leave north as it is”. The table of this operation and its effect:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot3+0\cdot4\\0\cdot3+1\cdot4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6\\4\end{pmatrix} \]

In quantum mechanics a ket is the counterpart of this position and an operator of this operation. The only difference is that the numbers can be complex.

Board 2

Hermitian: a mirrored table. Unitary: a length-preserving rotation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe table of a Hermitian operator is “mirror and conjugate” symmetric about the diagonal, and in its own eigenbasis only the diagonal remains; a unitary transformation preserves the inner product, and so lengths and probabilities.

First the complex conjugate: the conjugate of \(3+2i\) is \(3-2i\), so you flip the sign of i. A Hermitian operator means \(\hat A=\hat A^\dagger\), and in a table it looks like this:

\[ A_{mn}=A_{nm}^{*} \]

The number in box (m, n) is the conjugate of the number in the mirror box (n, m). The two notes on the board show these two operations: the “complex conjugate” star, and “notice the transpose mn→nm” for the swapped indices. On the diagonal m = n, so \(A_{nn}=A_{nn}^*\), and the diagonal is always real. The measurement results (eigenvalues) are always real too; that is why every measurable quantity is Hermitian.

The eigenvectors of a Hermitian operator form a basis, and in that basis the table is diagonal:

\[ \hat A=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\overleftrightarrow{A}=\mathrm{diag}(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N) \]

\(|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\) is the projection onto the nth direction. \(\hat A\) multiplies each direction by its own eigenvalue, and the directions do not mix.

Item 3, the unitary transformation: \(\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat I\). The bra of \(\langle U\phi|\) is \(\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\), so:

\[ \langle U\phi|U\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|\hat U^\dagger\hat U|\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \]

When the inner product is preserved, lengths, angles and probabilities are preserved too.

High school example

A table of distances between cities: if Ankara–Istanbul is 450 km, Istanbul–Ankara is also 450 km. The table is symmetric about the diagonal. A Hermitian table is the complex version of this:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}2&1+i\\1-i&3\end{pmatrix} \]

At the top right \(1+i\), in the mirror box its conjugate \(1-i\), and real 2 and 3 on the diagonal. For a unitary transformation, rotate a triangle on paper: the coordinates of the corners change, while the side lengths and angles stay the same.

Board 3241473

When the basis changes the numbers change, the physics does not

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceChanging basis is looking at the same arrow from other axes: the components transform with U, and the expectation value \(\langle A\rangle\) stays the same.

You can write the same \(|\psi\rangle\) in two bases: the old \(\{|e_n\rangle\}\) and the new \(\{|f_m\rangle\}\). The transition table U and the transformation rules:

\[ U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle,\qquad \vec c_f=\overleftrightarrow{U}\,\vec c_e,\qquad \overleftrightarrow{A}_f=\overleftrightarrow{U}\,\overleftrightarrow{A}_e\,\overleftrightarrow{U}^{\dagger} \]

\(U_{mn}\) is how much the new mth direction overlaps the old nth direction. The list of components is carried along by multiplying with U, and the operator table by wrapping it on both sides with U and \(U^\dagger\).

The lecturer's main message: the expectation value \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle\) does not depend on the basis. In matrix language \(\langle A\rangle=\vec c^{\,\dagger}A\,\vec c\). The note \(\vec c_f^{\,\dagger}=\overleftarrow{c}_f^{\,*}\) on the board says that the dagger of a column is a row with conjugated elements. Substituting:

\[ \vec c_f^{\,\dagger}A_f\,\vec c_f=(U\vec c_e)^\dagger(UA_eU^\dagger)(U\vec c_e)=\vec c_e^{\,\dagger}\underbrace{U^\dagger U}_{I}A_e\underbrace{U^\dagger U}_{I}\vec c_e=\vec c_e^{\,\dagger}A_e\,\vec c_e \]

The \(U^\dagger U\) in between turn into 1 and disappear. A prediction for a measurement cannot depend on the axes you draw in your notebook.

Board note

The board says \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|\hat U|e_n\rangle\). For the formulas on the board to hold, read it as \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\); do not put \(\hat U\) in between.

High school example

Take a photo of a pen on a table. With the phone straight, the tip of the pen is at the point (3, 4). Turn the phone along the pen and take another; the tip is at (5, 0). The numbers changed, but the length of the pen is 5 in both photos:

\[ \sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{5^2+0^2}=5 \]

Two different photos of the same operator. The eigenvalues, the measurement results, are the same in both.

Board 4

Function of an operator: first move to the easy axes

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe way to compute \(f(\hat A)\): go to the eigenbasis, apply f to each eigenvalue separately, come back.

The title of 6.6 is “Functions of operators and matrices”. It starts with the spectral decomposition:

\[ \overleftrightarrow{A}=\overleftrightarrow{U}\,\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\,\overleftrightarrow{U}^{\dagger} \]

The note on the right: U is the N×N table of the unitary transformation \(\hat U\), and its columns are the eigenvectors of A. Read from right to left: go to the eigenbasis with \(U^\dagger\), multiply each direction by its own eigenvalue with \(\mathrm{diag}(a_n)\), and come back with U.

Applying a function to a diagonal table is very easy; you apply it to each number separately:

\[ \mathrm{diag}(2,3)^2=\mathrm{diag}(4,9),\qquad e^{\mathrm{diag}(2,3)}=\mathrm{diag}(e^2,e^3) \]

With a non-diagonal table everything mixes when you square it. The solution is always the same:

\[ f(\hat A)=\overleftrightarrow{U}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(f(a_n)\big)\,\overleftrightarrow{U}^{\dagger},\qquad e^{\hat A}=\overleftrightarrow{U}\,\mathrm{diag}\big(e^{a_n}\big)\,\overleftrightarrow{U}^{\dagger} \]

Why is it true? When you square it the \(U^\dagger U\) in between disappears: \(A^2=UDU^\dagger\,UDU^\dagger=UD^2U^\dagger\). The same holds for every power, and \(e^{A}\) is a sum of powers.

High school example

A box sliding on an inclined plane. If you choose horizontal and vertical axes you have to split the weight, the normal force and the friction into parts, and the calculation gets messy. If you choose axes along the slope and perpendicular to it, you split only the weight once and the rest is one-dimensional motion. The eigenbasis is the “along the slope” axes of an operator: there each direction does its own job.

Board 5241473

Finding the same result with a Taylor series

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceExpanding \(e^{\hat A}\) in a series and looking term by term in the eigenbasis, every term comes out diagonal; their sum is \(e^{a_n}\delta_{mn}\).

A Maclaurin series is a Taylor series around zero. We write the high school expansion \(e^x=1+x+x^2/2!+\dots\) for an operator:

\[ e^{\hat A}=\hat I+\frac{\hat A}{1!}+\frac{\hat A^2}{2!}+\frac{\hat A^3}{3!}+O(\hat A^4) \]

The red circle on the board is the key: we put the series between \(\langle a_m|\) and \(|a_n\rangle\) in the eigenbasis of \(\hat A\). Term by term:

  • The \(\hat I\) term: \(\langle a_m|a_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\). The arrow on the board is \(\delta_{mn}\leftrightarrow I\): the identity table.
  • The \(\hat A\) term: since \(\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n|a_n\rangle\), \(\langle a_m|\hat A|a_n\rangle=a_n\delta_{mn}\).
  • The \(\hat A^2\) term, at the top right of the board: put \(\sum_k|a_k\rangle\langle a_k|=\hat I\) between the two \(\hat A\).
\[ \langle a_m|\hat A^2|a_n\rangle=\sum_k\underbrace{\langle a_m|\hat A|a_k\rangle}_{a_k\delta_{mk}}\,\underbrace{\langle a_k|\hat A|a_n\rangle}_{a_n\delta_{kn}}=\sum_k a_ka_n\,\delta_{mk}\delta_{kn}=a_n^2\,\delta_{mn} \]

\(\delta_{kn}\) leaves only the \(k=n\) term of the sum, and \(\delta_{mn}\) remains. Every term contains \(\delta_{mn}\), so factor it out:

\[ \big(e^{\hat A}\big)_{mn}=\delta_{mn}+a_n\delta_{mn}+\frac{a_n^2}{2!}\delta_{mn}+O(a_n^3)=\delta_{mn}\Big(1+a_n+\frac{a_n^2}{2!}+O(a_n^3)\Big)=e^{a_n}\,\delta_{mn} \]

The inside of the parenthesis is exactly the series of \(e^{a_n}\); the last note on the board is “\(e^{\hat A}\) is diagonal”. The lecturer's spoken warning: this diagonality is because we chose the eigenbasis. In a general basis there are also off-diagonal terms, produced by the \(U\,\mathrm{diag}(e^{a_n})\,U^\dagger\) of the previous step. There was also a bridge sentence: so far it was finite dimensional; in infinite dimensions these tables will become functions.

Board 6

The continuous basis and the Dirac delta

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceSince position is continuous, the basis vectors \(|x\rangle\) are too many to count; orthogonality is written with a Dirac delta instead of a Kronecker delta.

The title of Chapter 7 is “Position and Momentum Representations”, and the first subsection is 7.1, the position representation. Until now we could count the basis vectors: 1, 2, …, N. For position this ends; there are infinitely many points even between 0.50 m and 0.51 m. For every position value x there is a ket \(|x\rangle\):

\[ \hat x|x\rangle=x|x\rangle,\qquad \langle x'|x\rangle=\delta(x-x') \]

On the left \(\hat x\) is the measuring machine (operator), and on the right x is the number the machine gives (eigenvalue). In the inner product, \(\delta(x-x')\) takes the place of \(\delta_{mn}\). Why? In the continuous case “1 at a single point and 0 everywhere else” is useless, because in an integral the area of a single point is zero. We need something that lives at a single point and has integral 1: δ. The parenthesis on the board says this too; mathematically δ is not an ordinary function but a distribution, defined only by what it does inside an integral.

This has a price: \(\langle x|x\rangle=\delta(0)\) is infinite. So the length of \(|x\rangle\) is not 1; on its own it is not a real state but the idealization “exactly at this point”. Real states are mixtures of these.

High school example

The height distribution of a class. You count with 10 cm bins: 4 people between 150 and 160 cm, 9 between 160 and 170. As you narrow the bins (1 cm, 1 mm) fewer people fall in each; the answer to “how many people are exactly 165.000… cm tall?” eventually becomes zero. The meaningful question becomes “how many people per centimeter?”, a density. For continuous position too we talk in terms of density rather than individual points; in this language δ means “everything gathered at a single point”.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why is the Kronecker delta not enough in a continuous basis?

The completeness relation must be \(\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|x'\rangle=|x'\rangle\). If \(\langle x|x'\rangle\) were 1 only at the point x = x′, the integral would sum over a single point of zero width and the result would be zero. We need an object with area 1 squeezed into a single point.

Board 7892213

The Dirac delta: a box whose area stays 1

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThink of δ as the limit of a box that gets narrower while its area stays 1; inside an integral it selects the value of a function at a single point, and its unit is 1/length.

The board has two definitions. The rough one: 0 at \(x\neq0\) and infinite at \(x=0\), such that the total area is 1. The concrete one is the limit of a box:

\[ \delta(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\epsilon}, & -\epsilon\lt x\lt\epsilon\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]

The width of the box is \(2\epsilon\) and its height \(1/2\epsilon\). Area = width × height = 1, whatever ε is. This is the red hatched “Area = 1” on the board; the calculation on the right does the same with an integral:

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)\,dx=\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Big[x\Big]_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}=1 \]

Predict first: what happens to the height and area of the box as you shrink ε? Then drag the slider to the left.

The last line is the sifting property: the box takes the average of \(f(x)=x^2\) around x = 3. As ε shrinks the average goes to \(f(3)=9\).

The four properties on the left half of the board:

  1. \(\int\delta(x-x')\,dx'=1\). Shifting the peak to x does not change the area. The long arrow on the board shows that this comes from the definition above.
  2. \(\int f(x')\,\delta(x-x')\,dx'=f(x)\). The sifting property: δ selects the value of f only at the point x. Think with the box: the integral is the average of f over a narrow interval, and as the interval narrows this average goes to f(x). This is what appears as “skating property” in the transcript.
  3. \(\langle x'|x\rangle=\langle x|x'\rangle^*\), the conjugate symmetry of the inner product. From here \(\delta(x-x')=\delta(x'-x)\): δ is real (“Dirac-delta dist. is real”) and even (“evenness”). The box is also symmetric about zero.
  4. \([\delta(x-x')]=1/\text{length}\). \(\int dx\,\delta(x)=1\) is unitless and dx is in meters; for the product to be unitless δ must be 1/meter. The lecturer asked exactly this in class, and the answer was “one over meter”. The general rule: the unit of δ is the inverse of the unit of its variable.
High school example

A hammer blow. In high school, impulse = force × time (\(I=F\,\Delta t\)). A hammer applies a very large force to a nail for a very short time. If the time halves the force doubles, but the impulse stays the same. The area under the force-time graph is the impulse; δ is this graph in the limit “time goes to zero, area stays 1”. By the same logic a δ written in time has the unit 1/second.

Board 8

Completeness: cleverly inserting a 1

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentence\(\hat I=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\); inserting this 1 gives the wave function \(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\), the xth component of \(|\psi\rangle\).

In finite dimensions it was \(\sum_n|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|=\hat I\). In the continuous case the sum becomes an integral (the completeness relation, or resolution of identity):

\[ \hat I=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x| \]

The unit analysis on the right: \(\hat I\) is unitless and dx is in meters, so \(|x\rangle\langle x|\) must be 1/meter. \(|x\rangle\) and \(\langle x|\) are conjugates of each other and share the load equally: both are \(1/\sqrt{\text{meters}}\), the \(1/L^{1/2}\) on the board. Check: the unit of \(\langle x'|x\rangle=\delta\) is \(L^{-1/2}\cdot L^{-1/2}=1/L\). It holds.

The definition of the wave function \(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\): the component of \(|\psi\rangle\) along x, the continuous version of \(c_n=\langle e_n|\psi\rangle\). A wave function is an infinitely long list with a continuous index.

The bottom line of the board shows that this definition is consistent. Apply \(\langle x|\) from the left to \(\hat I|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle\) and expand the 1:

\[ \langle x|\psi\rangle=\langle x|\hat I|\psi\rangle=\int dx'\,\underbrace{\langle x|x'\rangle}_{\delta(x'-x)}\,\psi(x')=\psi(x) \]

The last step is sifting (prop. 2), and the result is the boxed formula \(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\). To be clear: this derives nothing new; it shows that the definitions do not contradict each other.

An important result that is not on the board: \(\int|\psi(x)|^2dx=1\). Since the unit of ψ is \(1/\sqrt{\text{meters}}\), \(|\psi|^2\) is 1/meter: probability per meter, a probability density (the Born rule).

High school example

Unit conversion. To convert 5 m to centimeters you write \(5\text{ m}\times\frac{100\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}}\). The value of the fraction is 1, so it does not change the result but changes its form. \(\hat I=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\) is such a 1: it opens \(|\psi\rangle\) into position components without changing it.

A second example: a bead is equally likely to be anywhere between 0 and 2 m. The density is \(|\psi|^2=\tfrac12\) (per meter), and the total is \(2\times\tfrac12=1\). The probability of finding it between 0 and 0.5 m is \(0.5\times\tfrac12=0.25\).

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why is \(|\psi(x)|^2\) a “probability density” rather than a “probability”?

Its unit is 1/meter. A probability must be a unitless number; to get one you must multiply by the length of an interval, that is, take \(\int_a^b|\psi|^2dx\). The probability of being at a single point is zero; the probability of being in an interval is meaningful.

The kernel: a continuous table

In one sentenceThe table of an operator in the position basis, \(A(x,x')\), is a function (the kernel); computing \(\hat A\psi\) is the integral version of matrix multiplication.

The matrix element was \(A_{mn}=\langle e_m|\hat A|e_n\rangle\). In the position basis the definition is the same, and its name is the “operator kernel”:

\[ \langle x'|\hat A|x\rangle=A(x',x) \]

The first argument is the bra (row), the second the ket (column). The board first writes \(A(x',x)\) and below uses \(A(x,x')\); only the letters are swapped, the rule is the same. The action of the operator, the “since” lines on the board, again by inserting a 1:

\[ (\hat A\psi)(x)\equiv\langle x|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\langle x|\hat A\int dx'\,|x'\rangle\langle x'|\psi\rangle=\int dx'\,A(x,x')\,\psi(x') \]

Put them side by side: \(d_m=\sum_nA_{mn}c_n\). The sum became an integral, the table a function, the list a wave function. Its meaning: the result at point x is a weighted sum of the values of ψ at all points, with the weights given by \(A(x,x')\).

High school example

This is a kernel: weight 1/3 for yourself and your two neighbors, 0 for distant days. The “blur” filter on your phone does the same with pixels.

The local potential: look only at your own point

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe kernel of a local potential is \(V(x)\,\delta(x-x')\); in the position representation it multiplies ψ point by point: \((\hat V\psi)(x)=V(x)\,\psi(x)\).

A potential is a function of position: \(\hat V=V(\hat x)\). Remember the rule from Boards 4–5: a function of an operator is diagonal in the eigenbasis of that operator. The eigenbasis of \(\hat x\) is \(|x\rangle\), so \(\hat V\) is diagonal in this basis too:

\[ \langle x'|\hat V|x\rangle=V(x',x)=V(x)\,\delta(x-x') \]

This is the continuous version of \(\mathrm{diag}(V)\): the table is filled only on the diagonal (x′ = x). This is also what the lecturer stressed aloud: a local potential is proportional to a Dirac delta. Compute its action:

\[ \langle x|\hat V|\psi\rangle=(\hat V\psi)(x)=\int dx'\,V(x)\,\delta(x-x')\,\psi(x')=V(x)\int dx'\,\delta(x-x')\,\psi(x')=V(x)\,\psi(x) \]

V(x) does not depend on x′, the variable of the integral, so it comes out; the remaining integral becomes ψ(x) by the sifting property (“ψ(x) from prop. 2” on the board). The result: in the position language the potential multiplies the wave function at each point by the value of V at that point.

High school example

The photo filter “brightness × 2”: each pixel multiplies its own value by two and does not look at its neighbors. This is local. A worked example: if \(V(x)=2x\) and \(\psi(x)=x+1\), then at x = 3, \((\hat V\psi)(3)=V(3)\,\psi(3)=6\times4=24\).

The nonlocal potential and the most common misconception

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceWith a nonlocal operator the result at x also depends on the values of ψ at other points; locality does not mean that the potential is confined to a narrow region.

The first line of the board sets up the analogy: the kernel \(V(x',x)\) is the counterpart of the diagonal table of \(\hat A\) in its eigenbasis in finite dimensions.

The board says \(\langle m|A|n\rangle=a_nA_{mn}\); the correct form is \(a_n\delta_{mn}\). Also, in the nonlocal formula φ is written on the left and ψ on the right; they are the same function, read it as ψ.

In one sentenceThe \(|p\rangle\) are also a continuous basis; every formula is the same as for position, only the units change to those of momentum.

The momentum eigenstate in position: a rotating wave

In one sentence\(\langle x|p\rangle\) is the solution of a simple differential equation: \(e^{ixp/\hbar}\), a rotating wave with the same magnitude everywhere.

The question on the board: what does a particle of definite momentum look like in position space, that is, what is \(\langle x|p\rangle\)? Apply \(\langle x|\) from the left to \(\hat p|p\rangle=p|p\rangle\):

\[ -i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}\langle x|p\rangle=p\,\langle x|p\rangle \]

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\langle x|p\rangle=\frac{ip}{\hbar}\,\langle x|p\rangle \]

\[ \int\frac{d\langle x|p\rangle}{\langle x|p\rangle}=\int\frac{i}{\hbar}\,p\,dx\;\Longrightarrow\;\ln\langle x|p\rangle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,xp\;\Longrightarrow\;\langle x|p\rangle=e^{ixp/\hbar} \]

It matches the change of basis on Board 3 exactly: \(U_{mn}=\langle f_m|e_n\rangle\) here becomes \(\langle p|x\rangle\), and \(\vec c_f=U\vec c_e\) becomes \(\phi(p)=\int dx\,\langle p|x\rangle\,\psi(x)\). Being unitary, it conserves the total probability: \(\int|\psi|^2dx=\int|\phi|^2dp\).

Board 17811622

The translation operator: shifting a graph

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentence\(\hat T(a)\) shifts the wave function to the right by a, \(\psi(x)\to\psi(x-a)\); for small a it expands with a derivative and carries a “generator” \(\hat G\) inside.

The title of 7.3 is “Deriving the momentum operator from translations”. The definition of the translation operator:

\[ \big(\hat T(a)\psi\big)(x)=\langle x|\hat T(a)|\psi\rangle=\psi(x-a) \]

Do not be thrown by the minus sign: \(\psi(x-a)\) shifts the graph to the RIGHT by a. At the point \(x_0+a\) the new graph takes the value of the old graph at \(x_0\).

For small (infinitesimal) a, the Taylor expansion, that is, the tangent line approximation:

\[ \big(\hat T(a)\psi\big)(x)=\psi(x-a)=\psi(x)-a\,\frac{d\psi}{dx}+O(a^2)\qquad(*) \]

Predict first: as a grows, does the tangent approximation (red ring) stay close to the true value (blue dot)? Then slide.

The dashed curve is \(\psi(x)=e^{-x^2}\) and the solid curve \(\psi(x-a)\). The vertical line is \(x_0=0.5\).

Properties of \(\hat T(a)\): shifting does not change the total probability (unitary), it depends continuously on a, \(\hat T(0)=\hat I\), and two shifts add: \(\hat T(a)\hat T(b)=\hat T(a+b)\). Such continuous unitary families can be written as exponentials:

\[ \hat T(a)=e^{-ia\hat G/\hbar}=\hat I-a\,\frac{i}{\hbar}\,\hat G+O(a^2) \]

\(\hat G\) is called the generator. On the board “Hermitian” is in quotes, because it is not an assumption but a result: unitarity gives \(\hat G^\dagger=\hat G\). The i in the exponent is there so that \(\hat G\) comes out as an observable (Hermitian), and \(1/\hbar\) is there for the units. \(a\hat G/\hbar\) must be unitless, so \([\hat G]=[\hbar]/\text{m}=\text{kg·m/s}\). Before calculating anything we know that \(\hat G\) has the unit of momentum.

Board note

In the exponential on the board no minus sign appears, it says \(e^{(i/\hbar)a\hat G}\); the expansion has a minus. The consistent form is the one with the minus. If you started with a plus you would end up with \(\hat G=-\hat p\). The lecturer also said this pattern will become clear within a few weeks (when the same structure returns with rotations and time evolution).

Board 18

Momentum, the generator of translation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceComparing two expansions of the same thing, the action of \(\hat G\) in position comes out as \(-i\hbar\,d/dx\); so \(\hat G=\hat p\), and momentum is the generator of translation.

Put the exponential expansion between \(\langle x|\) and \(|\psi\rangle\):

\[ \big(\hat T(a)\psi\big)(x)=\langle x|\,\hat I-a\,\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\,\hat G+O(a^2)\,|\psi\rangle=\psi(x)-a\,\frac{i}{\hbar}\,\langle x|\hat G|\psi\rangle+O(a^2)\qquad(**) \]

We have two expansions of the same thing, (∗) and (∗∗). They must be equal for every small a, so the coefficients of a are equal:

\[ \frac{d\psi(x)}{dx}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\,\langle x|\hat G|\psi\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\langle x|\hat G|\psi\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\frac{d\psi}{dx}=-i\hbar\,\frac{d\psi}{dx} \]

In the last step \(1/i=-i\). This is exactly the \(\hat p\) of the position representation. The last line of the board: “So Ĝ is nothing but p̂ and p̂ is the generator of translations!”

On Board 13 we took \(\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\) as given; here we saw where it comes from. This is the answer to a classmate's question in the lecture, “why do we use this like a postulate?”: the principle “momentum is the generator of translation” produces that formula. To be honest, the assumption does not vanish completely, it moves: identifying the generator with the physical momentum comes from classical physics and experiment.

Why “generator”? Many tiny shifts make one large shift:

\[ \hat T(a)=\Big[\hat T\big(\tfrac{a}{n}\big)\Big]^n\approx\Big(\hat I-\tfrac{a}{n}\,\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\,\hat p\Big)^n\;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\;e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar} \]
Three checks (not on the board)
  1. Finite translation: since \(-ia\hat p/\hbar=-a\,d/dx\), \(e^{-a\,d/dx}\psi(x)=\sum_n\frac{(-a)^n}{n!}\psi^{(n)}(x)=\psi(x-a)\). Plainly a Taylor series.
  2. In the momentum basis: \(\hat T\) is a function of \(\hat p\), so it is diagonal in this basis: \(\hat T(a)|p\rangle=e^{-iap/\hbar}|p\rangle\).
  3. The kernel in the position basis: \(\langle x|\hat T(a)|x'\rangle=\delta(x-a-x')\). The diagonal shifted by a; the cleanest example of the nonlocal operator of Board 11.
High school example

Comparing coefficients: if “\(3+5a=3+ka\) for every a”, then k = 5. In a polynomial equality the coefficients of terms of the same degree are equal; here too we equated the coefficients of a.

Compound interest is the high school version of the “generator” idea. With 100% annual interest added once a year your money doubles, added monthly it grows by a factor of \((1+\tfrac{1}{12})^{12}\approx2.613\), and added continuously by a factor of \(e\approx2.718\). Tiny steps piled up give an exponential.

A shift by a small a behaves like \(\hat I-a\,(i/\hbar)\,\hat p\); a large shift is these small steps piled up, \(e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar}\). So \(\hat p\) “generates” the shift. In the position language this corresponds to \(\hat p\) taking a derivative, the question “how much does ψ change when x changes a little?”.
