Board 1154713

Dropping the units simplifies the equation

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceDivide the equation by ħω and measure length in units of \(x_0=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}\); all the constants disappear and a single pure number problem remains: what can \(\varepsilon_n=E_n/\hbar\omega\) be?

Last week we solved the time-independent Schrödinger equation for several potentials (“Previous week”). The next potential is the harmonic oscillator: \(V(x)=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\), a spring with spring constant \(k=m\omega^2\). In the position representation the equation is:

\[ \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac12m\omega^2x^2\right]u_n(x)=E_n\,u_n(x) \]

It contains three constants: ħ, m, ω. The note “1/ħω” on the left of the board is the first move: divide both sides by ħω. With one ħω gone from each term in the square bracket:

\[ \left[-\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac12\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^2\right]u_n(x)=\varepsilon_n\,u_n(x),\qquad \varepsilon_n=\frac{E_n}{\hbar\omega} \]

The \(\hbar/m\omega\) circled in red has units of square meters. We call it \(x_0^2\); the \(m\omega/\hbar\) in the second term is its inverse:

\[ x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left[-\frac12\,x_0^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac12\Big(\frac{x}{x_0}\Big)^2\right]u_n=\varepsilon_n\,u_n \]

The last step is the dimensionless position \(X=x/x_0\). By the chain rule \(\frac{d}{dx}=\frac{1}{x_0}\frac{d}{dX}\), so \(x_0^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2}=\frac{d^2}{dX^2}\). No constants remain in the equation:

\[ \left[-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dX^2}+\frac12X^2\right]u_n=\varepsilon_n\,u_n \]

Solve this equation once and it holds for every oscillator: the vibration of a molecule, an ion in a trap, an LC circuit. To return to real size, multiply lengths by \(x_0\) and energies by ħω. The “non-dimensional” arrow on the board points to \(\varepsilon_n\): a unitless number.

\(x_0\) also has a physical meaning: the distance over which the particle roughly spreads in the ground state. A heavy particle (large m) or a stiff spring (large ω) makes \(x_0\) smaller, and the particle gets squeezed toward the bottom.

High school example

A map scale. On a 1:100,000 map, 3 cm is 3 km in reality. You draw the map once and multiply by the scale to find a real distance. \(x_0\) and ħω are the two scale bars of this oscillator: \(X=2\) means “two scale units”, which is really \(2x_0\) meters; \(\varepsilon=2.5\) is really \(2.5\,\hbar\omega\) joules.

Board 2

The abstract ket form and evenly spaced steps

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe allowed energies of the oscillator are \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)\), n = 0, 1, 2, …: discrete, with a gap of always ħω, and even the lowest step is not zero.

The top half of the board does the same nondimensionalization without choosing coordinates, in the “abstract ket form”:

\[ \hat H|u_n\rangle=E_n|u_n\rangle\;\Longrightarrow\;\left[\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2\right]|u_n\rangle=E_n|u_n\rangle \]

Here \(\hat p\) and \(\hat x\) are operators; there is no derivative yet. The derivative appears on going to the position representation (\(\hat p\to-i\hbar\,d/dx\)). Dividing by ħω, this time the \(m\hbar\omega\) circled in red becomes the momentum scale:

\[ p_0=\sqrt{m\hbar\omega},\qquad \hat P=\frac{\hat p}{p_0},\quad \hat X=\frac{\hat x}{x_0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Big(\tfrac12\hat P^2+\tfrac12\hat X^2\Big)|u_n\rangle=\varepsilon_n|u_n\rangle \]

Position and momentum look perfectly symmetric: both enter squared with a coefficient of ½. This symmetry will be useful later; the ground state takes the same shape (a Gaussian) in both position and momentum. Note: \(x_0\,p_0=\hbar\). The “or” line at the bottom right is the same equation in the position representation, the last line of Board 1.

4.2 Spectrum and Eigenfunctions. Normalizable solutions of this equation exist only for these numbers:

\[ \varepsilon_n=n+\frac12\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_n=\hbar\omega\Big(n+\frac12\Big),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots \]

The three notes at the bottom of the board sum up the character of the spectrum:

  1. Similar to Planck quanta of radiation. To explain blackbody radiation, Planck assumed that energy is absorbed and emitted in packets of ħω. Here the same ħω step comes out of the oscillator's own equation; it is a result, not an assumption.
  2. Discrete. The energy takes individual values rather than a continuous range. The reason is that the particle is trapped in a well; Board 3 shows the mathematics.
  3. Equally spaced. The gap between two consecutive levels is always \(E_{n+1}-E_n=\hbar\omega\). In the infinite well the levels spread out as \(n^2\); in the oscillator the gap is constant.
Board note

Next to the spectrum it says n = 1, 2, 3, …. The correct form is n = 0, 1, 2, …; on the next whiteboard the lecturer adds the 0. Without n = 0 the lowest energy would be \(\tfrac32\hbar\omega\). The result \(E_0=\hbar\omega/2\) on Board 4 comes exactly from this 0.

The bottom right line has the function \(u_n(X)\) on the left and the ket \(\varepsilon_n|u_n\rangle\) on the right. The two sides of an equality must be of the same type; read it as a function: \(\varepsilon_n\,u_n(X)\).

High school example

A ramp and a staircase. On a ramp you can stop at any height; a classical oscillator is like this, with any energy allowed. On a staircase you can stop only on the steps. On the oscillator's staircase the steps have equal height (ħω), but the first step starts half a step above the floor rather than at the floor. If ħω = 0.2 eV, the allowed energies are 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 eV, … There is no state at 0.4 eV.

Board 3154713

Normalized eigenfunctions and parity

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe wave function has to go to zero at infinity; this condition locks the energy to the values \(n+\tfrac12\), and the solutions take the form “Hermite polynomial times Gaussian”.

Why only \(n+\tfrac12\)? The probabilities must add up to 1:

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|u_n(x)|^2\,dx=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}u_n(x)=0 \]

Solving the equation with a power series generally gives an infinite series, and at large x this series blows up like \(e^{+X^2/2}\). The only way to stop the blowup is to cut the series somewhere, setting everything after one coefficient to zero. This is the note on the right of the board, “requires cutting the higher powers in series exp. via εₙ form”: the series terminates only when \(\varepsilon=n+\tfrac12\), leaving a polynomial of degree n. This boundary condition is the source of the discrete spectrum.

Why does the series cut off exactly at \(\varepsilon=n+\tfrac12\)? (The board has only the note.)

Separate the Gaussian envelope: \(u=h(X)\,e^{-X^2/2}\). Substituting into the dimensionless equation gives for h:

\[ h''-2X\,h'+(2\varepsilon-1)\,h=0 \]

Writing \(h=\sum_ka_kX^k\) and matching equal powers gives a ladder relation between the coefficients:

\[ a_{k+2}=\frac{2k+1-2\varepsilon}{(k+1)(k+2)}\,a_k \]

If the numerator vanishes for some k = n, then \(a_{n+2},a_{n+4},\dots\) are all zero and the series turns into a polynomial. The condition is \(2n+1-2\varepsilon=0\), that is, \(\varepsilon=n+\tfrac12\). If it does not terminate, at large k \(a_{k+2}/a_k\approx2/k\); this is the ratio of the series of \(e^{X^2}\), and \(u\sim e^{X^2}e^{-X^2/2}=e^{+X^2/2}\) blows up.

The normalized eigenfunctions:

\[ u_n(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n\,n!\,\sqrt\pi\,x_0}}\;H_n\!\Big(\frac{x}{x_0}\Big)\;e^{-x^2/(2x_0^2)} \]

It has three parts. The Gaussian \(e^{-x^2/2x_0^2}\) is the envelope that pulls everything to zero at infinity. \(H_n\) is the Hermite polynomial of degree n, giving the oscillation and the nodes (Board 4). The fraction in front is the normalization constant that makes the total probability 1. The \(x_0\) in the denominator is inside the square root, because the unit of \(|u_n|^2\) must be 1/meter for \(|u_n|^2dx\) to be a unitless probability.

Parity. The potential is even, \(V(-x)=V(x)\): the left side is the mirror image of the right. So every eigenfunction is either even or odd:

\[ u_n(-x)=(-1)^n\,u_n(x) \]

If n is even the function stays the same in the mirror; if n is odd it changes sign. The parenthesis on the board says where this helps: in the integral \(\langle u_n|u_m\rangle=\int u_n^*(x)\,u_m(x)\,dx\), if one is odd and the other even the product is odd, and the integral over a symmetric range is zero (“if odd ⇒ 0”). Most of the “answer without calculating” exam questions are solved with this trick. For example in the ground state \(\langle x\rangle=\int x\,|u_0|^2\,dx=0\), because x is odd and \(|u_0|^2\) is even.

High school example

\(f(x)=x^2\) stays the same in the mirror (even), \(g(x)=x^3\) changes sign (odd). You can state \(\int_{-2}^{2}x^3\,dx\) without calculating: the positive area on the right exactly cancels the negative area on the left, and the result is 0. Even times odd is odd again: \(x^2\cdot x^3=x^5\), whose integral is again 0.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: \(u_0\) and \(u_2\) are orthogonal. Is the reason parity?

No. Both are even and so is their product; parity does not say this integral is zero. Orthogonality comes from Ĥ being Hermitian: eigenvectors with different eigenvalues (\(\hbar\omega/2\) and \(5\hbar\omega/2\)) are always orthogonal. Parity only settles the pairs with n + m odd without calculation.

Board 4

Hermite polynomials, nodes and the ladder

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceHermite polynomials are generated from a single derivative formula; the nth state has exactly n nodes; since the levels are evenly spaced, one can build ladder operators that move one step up or down.

The Rodrigues formula (at the very top of the board) generates all Hermite polynomials in one line:

\[ H_n(x)=(-1)^n\,e^{x^2}\,\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\big(e^{-x^2}\big) \]

Let us try it. n = 1: \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^2}=-2x\,e^{-x^2}\); with the \((-1)\) and \(e^{x^2}\) in front, \(H_1=2x\). n = 2: one more derivative, with the product rule \(\frac{d}{dx}\big(-2x\,e^{-x^2}\big)=(4x^2-2)\,e^{-x^2}\). The result:

\[ H_0=1,\qquad H_1=2x,\qquad H_2=4x^2-2,\qquad H_3=8x^3-12x \]

Instead of differentiating each time there is a shortcut: \(H_{n+1}=2x\,H_n-2n\,H_{n-1}\). For example \(H_2=2x\cdot2x-2\cdot1\cdot1=4x^2-2\).

Board note

The board says \(H_2(x)=4x^2-2x\). The correct form is \(4x^2-2\). The easy check is parity: \(H_2\) must be even, but \(4x^2-2x\) is neither even nor odd. Or the roots: those of \(4x^2-2\) are \(\pm1/\sqrt2\), symmetric; those of \(4x^2-2x\) are 0 and ½, not symmetric.

“The state n has n nodes.” A node is a point where the wave function passes through zero (the zeros at infinity do not count). Since the Gaussian is nowhere zero, the nodes are set entirely by the roots of \(H_n\), and a Hermite polynomial of degree n has n real roots. More nodes mean a shorter wavelength and larger curvature, that is, a larger kinetic energy. This is the intuition for why energy grows with n.

The drawing has the parabola \(V(x)\) with the levels n = 0, 1, 2 inside it, at heights \(\hbar\omega/2\), \(3\hbar\omega/2\), \(5\hbar\omega/2\), always ħω apart. Each level line ends where it meets the parabola: the classical turning points, \(x=\pm x_0\sqrt{2n+1}\). A classical particle cannot go beyond these points; the wave function spills a little past them.

Predict first: how many nodes does the n = 4 state have, and is the function even or odd? Then bring the slider to 4.

The horizontal axis is \(x/x_0\), the vertical axis is energy in units of ħω. The blue curve is \(u_n\), drawn on top of its level; the red rings are the nodes.

“Equally spaced levels ⇒ suitable for use of ladder operators.” Since the steps are evenly spaced, one can write two operators that take a level to its neighbor:

\[ \hat a\,|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle,\qquad \hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle \]

\(\hat a\) is the lowering and \(\hat a^\dagger\) the raising operator. The board has only the arrows (\(|n\rangle\to|n-1\rangle\), \(|n\rangle\to|n+1\rangle\)); the \(\sqrt n\) coefficients are a detail. The idea for this week: even spacing means a single “add ħω / remove ħω” operation.

The heading 4.3 at the bottom of the board (\(E_0=\hbar\omega/2\), \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\), \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\)) opens on the next board.

High school example

A guitar string. If you lightly hold the string at exactly one third of its length rather than the middle and pluck it, the string stays still at two points and vibrates in three parts, giving a higher pitch. As the number of still points grows the wavelength shortens and the frequency and energy rise. The nodes of the oscillator are the same: no node in \(u_0\), one in \(u_1\), two in \(u_2\). The difference is that the ends of a string are fixed, while in the oscillator the ends die off smoothly with a Gaussian tail.

Board 5698172

The ground state: nonzero energy, minimum uncertainty

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceIn the ground state the particle is on average at the center and at rest, but both its position and momentum are spread out; with \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\) this is the narrowest state the uncertainty principle allows, and this spread makes \(E_0=\hbar\omega/2\) unavoidable.

The last line of the previous board: \(E_0=\hbar\omega/2\). A classical spring can sit motionless at the bottom with energy 0. In quantum mechanics it cannot, because “at the bottom and motionless” means knowing \(x=0\) and \(p=0\) exactly at the same time. \([\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\) forbids this:

\[ \Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\frac{\hbar}{2} \]

The results on the board for the ground state:

\[ \langle x\rangle=\langle p\rangle=0,\qquad (\Delta x)^2=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}=\frac{x_0^2}{2},\qquad (\Delta p)^2=\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}=\frac{p_0^2}{2} \]

Why are the averages zero? \(|u_0|^2\) is even and x is odd: \(\int x\,|u_0|^2dx=0\), the parity trick of Board 3. The same symmetry holds for momentum. But a zero average does not mean zero spread; \((\Delta x)^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2=\langle x^2\rangle\), and that is not zero. Multiplying:

\[ \Delta x\,\Delta p=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}=\frac{\hbar}{2} \]

The inequality has become an equality: “uncertainty relation saturates with its minimum for the ground state”. The shape that does this is the Gaussian; this is the “minimum uncertainty Gaussian” on the board. In excited states the product grows: in the nth state \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\).

Where does \((\Delta x)^2=x_0^2/2\) come from? (The board has only the result.)

\(|u_0|^2=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0}e^{-x^2/x_0^2}\). With the Gaussian integral \(\int x^2e^{-x^2/a^2}dx=\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}a^3\):

\[ \langle x^2\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}x_0^3=\frac{x_0^2}{2}=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega} \]

You do not need to repeat the calculation for momentum: the dimensionless equation on Board 2 is symmetric in X and P, so \(\langle P^2\rangle=\langle X^2\rangle=\tfrac12\), that is, \(\langle p^2\rangle=p_0^2/2\).

The connection with energy:

\[ \langle\hat H\rangle=\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}+\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2} \]

The energy splits half and half between kinetic and potential. Why exactly this width? If you narrow the packet (Δx shrinks) the potential energy falls but Δp and the kinetic energy grow. Their sum has a minimum:

Predict first: if you make the Gaussian packet very narrow, does the total energy fall? Drag the slider to the left.

Units: \(x_0=1\), ħω = 1. For each σ the narrowest Gaussian is taken (\(\Delta p=\hbar/2\sigma\)). Blue is the total, dashed gray the kinetic and red the potential energy. The minimum is at σ = 0.71\(\,x_0\) and is exactly \(E_0=0.5\,\hbar\omega\).

The last line: “We will discuss coherent states also minimize the uncertainty.” A coherent state is the Gaussian of the ground state simply displaced; it oscillates back and forth without changing shape and \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\hbar/2\) is always preserved. The displaced oscillator on Board 6 is a preparation for this.

High school example

Among rectangles of area 16 m², the one with the shortest perimeter is the square: 4 × 4. If the product of the sides is fixed, their sum is smallest when the two are equal. In the ground state too, the product of the kinetic and potential energy is bounded from below by the uncertainty principle: \(\tfrac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}\cdot\tfrac12m\omega^2(\Delta x)^2=\tfrac{\omega^2}{4}(\Delta x\Delta p)^2\ge\tfrac{(\hbar\omega)^2}{16}\). The sum is smallest when the two are equal: \(\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{4}+\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{4}=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\).

The displaced oscillator: completing the square

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceAdding a constant force to the oscillator only shifts the parabola and lowers it: the equilibrium point moves to \(x_{eq}=-F/m\omega^2\), all levels drop by \(F^2/2m\omega^2\), but the step spacing is still ħω.

A uniform force, the same force everywhere, adds a linear term to the potential. The Hamiltonian on the board:

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2+F\hat x \]

The trick is completing the square from high school. Expand \(\tfrac12m\omega^2(x-x_{eq})^2\):

\[ \frac12m\omega^2(x-x_{eq})^2=\frac12m\omega^2x^2-m\omega^2x_{eq}\,x+\frac12m\omega^2x_{eq}^2 \]

For the middle term to be \(Fx\) we need \(-m\omega^2x_{eq}=F\), that is, \(x_{eq}=-F/m\omega^2\). The constant at the end is then \(\tfrac12m\omega^2\cdot F^2/m^2\omega^4=F^2/2m\omega^2\). The lecturer adds this constant in red on the board and subtracts it outside (“add and subtract”):

\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2(\hat x-x_{eq})^2-\frac{F^2}{2m\omega^2},\qquad x_{eq}=-\frac{F}{m\omega^2} \]

Reading it: the first two terms are an oscillator with the same ω, only centered at \(x_{eq}\). The last term is a constant that pulls every energy down by the same amount. The momentum term is not affected by the shift, because \(\frac{d}{dx}=\frac{d}{d(x-x_{eq})}\). The new problem is an exact copy of the old one:

\[ u_n^{(F)}(x)=u_n(x-x_{eq}),\qquad E_n=\hbar\omega\Big(n+\frac12\Big)-\frac{F^2}{2m\omega^2},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots \]
A sign trap

The \(+F\hat x\) on the board is a potential energy term. The force is minus the derivative of the potential, \(-\tfrac{d}{dx}(Fx)=-F\), so this term pushes the particle in the −x direction. That is why the equilibrium point comes out on the negative side. If the question says “a force of magnitude F in the +x direction”, the potential is \(-Fx\) and \(x_{eq}=+F/m\omega^2\).

Predict first: when you increase F, does the gap between the steps change? Can the ground state energy be negative?

Where does this appear in physics? Applying a constant electric field to a charged particle (an ion in a trap, an atom in a crystal): \(V=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2-qEx\). The note on the last line, “coherent states will be related to displaced oscillator”, says this too: a coherent state is a displaced copy of the ground state.

High school example

The frequency is set by the curvature of the parabola, \(V''(x)=m\omega^2\). The second derivative of a linear term is zero; \(Fx\) does not change the curvature, it only slides the parabola sideways and lowers it. Same curvature, same ω, same ħω spacing.

Board 7698172

Ket, bra and inner product

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceA state is an arrow, the ket \(|\psi\rangle\); the transpose and conjugate of that arrow is the bra \(\langle\psi|\); a bra and a ket side by side give a single complex number, the inner product \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\).

Chapter 5 changes the language. Until now we wrote a state with a function such as \(\psi(x)\); that is the translation of the state into the position language. Dirac's idea: write the state itself as an arrow independent of any language, \(|\psi\rangle\), and translate it into a language only when a calculation requires it.

“A quantum state is represented by a nonzero ket \(|\psi\rangle\) in the complex Hilbert space.” Three words in this sentence matter:

  • nonzero: the zero vector is not a state. You cannot normalize something with zero norm or get a probability from it.
  • complex: the components can be complex numbers. The phases on Board 15 come from here.
  • Hilbert space: a vector space with an inner product, where you can ask “how much do these two arrows overlap?”. Its dimension can be finite (spin, a qubit) or infinite (position).

The bra. Think of kets as column vectors; a bra is the row obtained by transposing it and conjugating its elements:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\langle\psi|=\big(|\psi\rangle\big)^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}c_1^*&c_2^*\end{pmatrix} \]

The inner product is row times column. If \(|\phi\rangle=(b_1,b_2)^T\):

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}b_1^*&b_2^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}=b_1^*c_1+b_2^*c_2 \]

The name comes from here too: bra + ket = bracket, \(\langle\;|\;\rangle\).

Board note

The board says “Its complex conjugate is the bra”. More precisely it is the Hermitian conjugate (dagger, †): take the transpose and the conjugate. If you only take the conjugate, a column stays a column; what makes the bra a row is the transpose.

The first property, conjugate symmetry:

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^* \]

If you swap the order you get the conjugate of the result. For real vectors \(\vec a\cdot\vec b=\vec b\cdot\vec a\); in the complex world this symmetry becomes a “conjugated” symmetry. The result on the right of the board: putting \(|\phi\rangle=|\psi\rangle\) gives \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^*\). A number equal to its conjugate is real. Good news, because \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\) is the squared length of the arrow and a length cannot be complex.

High school example

For real vectors, \(\vec a\cdot\vec a=9+16=25=|\vec a|^2\) for \(\vec a=(3,4)\). For a complex number the same job is done with \(|z|^2=z^*z\): if \(z=3+4i\), then \(z^*z=(3-4i)(3+4i)=9+16=25\). Multiplying without the conjugate would give \(z\cdot z=-7+24i\), which cannot be the square of a length. That is exactly why the bra has a conjugate.

Board 8

The rules of the inner product

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceCoefficients on the ket side come out as they are, those on the bra side come out conjugated; the inner product of a ket with itself is never negative and is zero only for the zero ket.

There are four rules, and all can be read from the row × column picture of Board 7. Linearity in the ket:

\[ \langle\phi|\big(\alpha|\psi\rangle+\beta|\chi\rangle\big)=\alpha\langle\phi|\psi\rangle+\beta\langle\phi|\chi\rangle \]

Conjugate linearity in the bra:

\[ \big(\alpha|\phi\rangle+\beta|\chi\rangle\big)^\dagger|\psi\rangle=\alpha^*\langle\phi|\psi\rangle+\beta^*\langle\chi|\psi\rangle \]

When turning a ket into a bra the coefficients are conjugated too: \((\alpha|\phi\rangle)^\dagger=\alpha^*\langle\phi|\). The most common exam mistake is forgetting this star.

Positive definiteness:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle\ge0,\qquad \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=0\iff|\psi\rangle=0 \]

On Board 7 we saw that \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\) is real; here we say it is not negative either. Written with components it is clear: \(\sum_n|c_n|^2\), a sum of squares.

The norm: \(\|\psi\|^2=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\). You can normalize any nonzero ket by dividing it by its own norm:

\[ |\psi\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}}\quad\text{after}\quad\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1 \]

Example: for \(|\psi\rangle=3|1\rangle+4i|2\rangle\), \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=9+16=25\); the normalized form is \(\tfrac15(3|1\rangle+4i|2\rangle)\).

In position space the inner product becomes an integral:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,|\psi(x)|^2,\qquad \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\phi^*(x)\,\psi(x) \]

An integral instead of a sum, \(\psi(x)\) instead of \(c_n\). Board 10 will show why. The last line: if \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=0\), the two states are orthogonal.

Board note

The board says “Orthonormal states: ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = 0”. \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=0\) says only orthogonal. For orthonormal both must also have length 1: \(\langle\phi|\phi\rangle=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\). The \(\langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\) on Board 9 writes both at once. And a note on notation: the \(|\psi|^2\) on the board is the square of the norm; writing \(\|\psi\|^2\) with double bars prevents confusion with \(|\psi(x)|^2\).

High school example

Normalizing is like converting to percentages. If a class has 12 girls and 18 boys, the shares are 12/30 = 40% and 18/30 = 60%, for a total of 100%. Dividing the components of a ket by its norm likewise makes the probabilities add up to 1. The only difference: in quantum mechanics the probability is the square of the component, so the division is by \(\sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\).

Expansion in an orthonormal basis

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceOnce you choose an orthonormal and complete basis, every ket is a sum along the directions of that basis; the coefficient of each direction is \(c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle\), and for a normalized ket \(\sum_n|c_n|^2=1\).

A basis must satisfy two conditions; both are underlined on the board:

\[ \underbrace{\langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{mn}}_{\text{orthonormal}},\qquad \underbrace{\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat I}_{\text{complete}} \]

Orthonormal: every basis vector has length 1 and different ones are orthogonal. \(\delta_{mn}\) is the Kronecker delta: 1 if m = n and 0 otherwise. The analogy on the left of the board is \(\hat i\cdot\hat j=0\), \(\hat i\cdot\hat i=1\), …, three perpendicular unit vectors in three dimensions.

Complete: the directions are enough and nothing is left out. In three dimensions, taking only \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) is orthonormal but not complete; you cannot write anything along \(\hat k\). \(\sum|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat I\) is the formula for this sufficiency: if you add the projections onto all directions you get the vector back.

The expansion comes from exactly this “insert a 1” trick:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\hat I|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle,\qquad c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle \]

A second way to find \(c_n\): multiply both sides from the left by \(\langle m|\). \(\langle m|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n\langle m|n\rangle=\sum_nc_n\delta_{mn}=c_m\). The delta picks a single term of the sum.

Normalization:

\[ \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_m\sum_nc_m^*\,c_n\,\langle m|n\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2=1 \]

The physical meaning is on Board 14: \(|c_n|^2\) is the probability of finding the nth result in a measurement. Their sum being 1 means “some result certainly comes out”.

Board note

The board says \(\sum_nc_n^2=1\). Since \(c_n\) can be complex, the correct form is \(\sum_n|c_n|^2=1\). The difference matters: for \(c_1=c_2=i/\sqrt2\), \(\sum c_n^2=-1\), but \(\sum|c_n|^2=1\). Also add “= 1” to the end of the sentence “With normalization ⟨ψ|ψ⟩”.

Predict first: as you turn \(|\psi\rangle\) from \(|1\rangle\) toward \(|2\rangle\), does \(|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2\) change?

\(|\psi\rangle=\cos\theta\,|1\rangle+\sin\theta\,|2\rangle\). The red dashed lines are the projections; the bars on the right are \(|c_1|^2\) and \(|c_2|^2\). As long as the arrow stays on the unit circle the total is 1: Pythagoras.

High school example

A plane flies at 500 km/h, 37° north of east. Its east component is \(500\cos37°\approx400\) and its north component \(500\sin37°\approx300\) km/h. Finding a component means taking the inner product of the velocity with a unit direction: \(v_{\text{east}}=\hat i\cdot\vec v\). The \(c_n=\langle n|\psi\rangle\) of quantum mechanics is the same thing. Pythagoras is normalization itself: \(400^2+300^2=500^2\).

The continuous position basis

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceSince position is continuous, sums become integrals and the Kronecker delta becomes the Dirac delta; the component of a ket in the position basis is the wave function itself: \(\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle\).

The results of a position measurement are not a countable list but a continuous line. For every x there is a basis ket \(|x\rangle\): “the particle is exactly at x”. The two conditions of Board 9 go over to the continuous version:

\[ \langle m|n\rangle=\delta_{mn}\;\longrightarrow\;\langle x'|x\rangle=\delta(x'-x),\qquad \sum_n|n\rangle\langle n|=\hat I\;\longrightarrow\;\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|=\hat I \]

The Dirac delta is the object that is “0 for x′ ≠ x but has area 1 under it”. It is not an ordinary function; the note “function / distribution” on the board says this. In mathematics it is called a distribution. Its value alone is meaningless; it makes sense inside an integral: \(\int f(x')\,\delta(x'-x)\,dx'=f(x)\).

Why is the Kronecker delta not enough? Check with completeness: we need \(|x'\rangle=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|x'\rangle\). If \(\langle x|x'\rangle\) were 1 at only a single point, the integral over a point of zero width would be 0. We need a sharp peak with area 1.

The same “insert a 1” trick:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\psi\rangle=\int dx\,\psi(x)\,|x\rangle,\qquad \psi(x)\equiv\langle x|\psi\rangle \]

This is one of the most important lines of the week: the wave function is the component of the ket along x, the continuous sibling of \(c_n\). The integral formula of Board 8 also comes from here; you only need to insert \(\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\):

\[ \langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\phi|\Big(\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\Big)|\psi\rangle=\int dx\,\langle\phi|x\rangle\langle x|\psi\rangle=\int dx\,\phi^*(x)\,\psi(x) \]

\(\langle\phi|x\rangle=\langle x|\phi\rangle^*=\phi^*(x)\), conjugate symmetry.

The bottom half of the board is the main message of the lecture: “The bra-ket notation separates abstract state vector from any representation.” The lecturer's analogy: you can write a vector \(\vec A\) with Cartesian components \(x\hat i+y\hat j+z\hat k\) or as length times direction \(A\,\hat u_A\) (in spherical coordinates). The arrow is the same; only the description changes. A quantum state can likewise be written in the energy basis (the \(c_n\)), the position basis (\(\psi(x)\)) or the momentum basis.

High school example

You can describe a city square by latitude and longitude or by a postal address. The square is the same, the description differs. The ket is the square itself, \(\psi(x)\) is the latitude and longitude description, and the \(c_n\) are the postal address. To go from one description to the other you need a translation table; in quantum mechanics that table is \(\langle x|n\rangle\), and for the oscillator \(u_n(x)\).

\(\langle x|x\rangle=\delta(0)\), infinite. A particle exactly at one point would need infinite momentum uncertainty; it is not a physical state. But it is very useful as a basis: every physical ket is written as an integral of these, and the coefficients \(\psi(x)\) are finite and normalizable. Real states are weighted mixtures of position kets.

5.3 Projectors and projective measurement. The projection operator (projector) onto a normalized \(|a_n\rangle\):

\[ \hat P_n=|a_n\rangle\langle a_n| \]

This is a “ket-bra”. The bra first takes the inner product with the incoming ket and produces a number, then that number is multiplied by \(|a_n\rangle\):

\[ \hat P_n|\psi\rangle=|a_n\rangle\underbrace{\langle a_n|\psi\rangle}_{c_n}=c_n\,|a_n\rangle \]

So \(\hat P_n\) takes the shadow of \(|\psi\rangle\) along \(|a_n\rangle\) and throws away the rest. Careful: \(\langle a_n|\psi\rangle\) is a number (bra-ket), \(|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\) is an operator (ket-bra). The order changes everything.

Why the “normalized” condition? If the length of \(|a\rangle\) were 2, \(|a\rangle\langle a|\psi\rangle\) would make the shadow 4 times larger. A projection must not invent length; the \(\hat P^2=\hat P\) on the next board relies on this condition.

High school example

The shadow of a slanted stick on the ground in the midday sun. The light comes straight down; the shadow is the component of the stick parallel to the ground: \((\hat i\cdot\vec v)\,\hat i\). This is exactly \(|a\rangle\langle a|\psi\rangle\): first “how much” (an inner product, a number), then “in which direction” (a unit vector).

Board 12

A projection cannot be taken twice: P̂² = P̂ and P̂† = P̂

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceA projection operator is recognized by two properties: applying it twice is the same as applying it once (\(\hat P^2=\hat P\)), and it equals its own Hermitian conjugate (\(\hat P^\dagger=\hat P\)).

The first property, idempotence:

\[ \hat P_n^2=\hat P_n\hat P_n=|a_n\rangle\underbrace{\langle a_n|a_n\rangle}_{=1}\langle a_n|=|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|=\hat P_n \]

The bra-ket in the middle is a number, and it is 1 because \(|a_n\rangle\) is normalized. The \(\delta_{nn}\) on the board also means 1. The geometric meaning: the shadow of a shadow is itself. An arrow you have already dropped onto the \(|a_n\rangle\) direction does not change when dropped there again.

The second property, Hermiticity:

\[ \hat P_n^\dagger=\big(|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\big)^\dagger=\big(\langle a_n|\big)^\dagger\big(|a_n\rangle\big)^\dagger=|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|=\hat P_n \]

The dagger reverses the order of a product, \((AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger\), and also turns a bra into a ket and a ket into a bra. The two reversals cancel. Being Hermitian, \(\hat P_n\) is an observable in its own right. Its only eigenvalues are 1 (“yes, along this direction”) and 0 (“no”): if \(\hat P|v\rangle=\lambda|v\rangle\), then from \(\hat P^2=\hat P\), \(\lambda^2=\lambda\). Every projection is a yes/no question.

Together the two properties give two more rules: projections onto different directions annihilate each other, and all of them added together give the identity operator.

\[ \hat P_m\hat P_n=|a_m\rangle\langle a_m|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|=\delta_{mn}\,\hat P_n,\qquad \sum_n\hat P_n=\hat I \]
Board note

In the parenthesized line, \(\hat P_x^2=|x\rangle\langle x|x\rangle\langle x|=|x\rangle\langle x|\) is written for \(\hat P_x=|x\rangle\langle x|\) and the middle term is marked as \(\delta(x-x)\). This step does not hold: \(\langle x|x\rangle=\delta(0)\) is infinite, not 1. Since position kets cannot be normalized (Board 10), \(|x\rangle\langle x|\) is not a true projection operator. A true projection is onto an interval: \(\hat P_{[a,b]}=\int_a^bdx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\), the question “is the particle between a and b?”. Its square really is itself; see the box below.

Why is the square of an interval projection itself? (Not on the board.)
\[ \hat P_{[a,b]}^2=\int_a^bdx\int_a^bdx'\,|x\rangle\underbrace{\langle x|x'\rangle}_{\delta(x-x')}\langle x'|=\int_a^bdx\,|x\rangle\langle x|=\hat P_{[a,b]} \]

δ pulls the x′ integral to a single point and a single integral remains. The troublesome \(\delta(0)\) never appears, because δ is inside an integral. The probability meaning is clean too: \(\langle\psi|\hat P_{[a,b]}|\psi\rangle=\int_a^b|\psi(x)|^2dx\) is the probability of finding the particle in the interval [a, b].

High school example

Polarized sunglasses or a camera filter. When light passes through the filter only the component along the filter's direction remains. If you put a second filter behind it in the same direction the light does not decrease at all; the first filter has already brought it to that direction: \(\hat P^2=\hat P\). If you turn the second filter by 90°, no light passes at all: \(\hat P_m\hat P_n=0\).

Board 13864112

An observable is a weighted sum of projections

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceEvery observable is a sum of projections onto its own eigenstates weighted by the eigenvalues; its expectation value is the average of the possible results weighted by their probabilities: \(\langle A\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2a_n\).

An observable is the operator of a measurable quantity: it is Hermitian, its eigenvalues are real, and its eigenstates form an orthonormal and complete basis. “Nondegenerate” means each eigenvalue has a single eigenstate, so no two different \(|a_n\rangle\) share the same number. In this case the operator expands in projections. This is called the spectral decomposition:

\[ \hat A=\sum_na_n|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|=\sum_na_n\,\hat P_n \]

Read it: \(\hat A\) takes a ket, separates the part along each eigendirection, multiplies each part by the eigenvalue of that direction and adds them back. In matrix language it is a table that is diagonal in its own eigenbasis: \(\mathrm{diag}(a_1,a_2,\dots)\).

The first check on the board: does this form give the eigenvalue equation?

\[ \hat A|a_m\rangle=\sum_na_n\,\hat P_n|a_m\rangle=\sum_na_n|a_n\rangle\underbrace{\langle a_n|a_m\rangle}_{\delta_{nm}}=a_m|a_m\rangle \]

It does. δ selects only the n = m term of the sum.

The second calculation is the expectation value. For \(|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|a_n\rangle\):

\[ \langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle=\sum_na_n\,\langle\psi|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\psi\rangle=\sum_na_n\,c_n^*c_n=\sum_n|c_n|^2\,a_n \]

The curly braces on the board: \(\langle a_n|\psi\rangle=c_n\), \(\langle\psi|a_n\rangle=c_n^*\) (conjugate symmetry), and their product is \(|c_n|^2\). The result is the same as a weighted average in statistics: each result times its probability, all added up.

The trap: the expectation value is not the result of a single measurement. A single measurement always gives one of the eigenvalues; \(\langle A\rangle\) is the average of measurements on many identically prepared systems. If the eigenvalues are 1 and 3 you can get \(\langle A\rangle=2.5\), but no measurement ever gives 2.5.

High school example

The example the lecturer gave in class: a die. On a fair die each face has probability 1/6, and the expectation value is \(\tfrac16(1+2+3+4+5+6)=3.5\). No roll gives 3.5, but the average of many rolls tends to 3.5. On a loaded die let the probability of 6 be ½ and of the others 1/10: \(\tfrac{1}{10}(1+2+3+4+5)+\tfrac12\cdot6=1.5+3=4.5\). In quantum mechanics the faces are the eigenvalues \(a_n\) and the probabilities are \(|c_n|^2\).

Board 14

Measurement probability and the state after measurement

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceThe probability of finding \(a_n\) is the expectation value of the projection, \(p_n=|c_n|^2\) (the Born rule); if the measurement gave \(a_n\), the state collapses onto the \(|a_n\rangle\) direction and is renormalized by dividing by \(\sqrt{p_n}\).

The measurement postulate has two parts: which result comes out with which probability, and what happens to the state after a result. First the probability:

\[ p_n=\langle\psi|\hat P_n|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\psi\rangle=|c_n|^2 \]

This is the average of the yes/no question of Board 12: the eigenvalues of \(\hat P_n\) are 1 and 0, and their average is the probability of “yes”. The ⟨A⟩ of Board 13 is really \(\sum_na_np_n\).

The state after measurement. Applying the projection:

\[ \hat P_n|\psi\rangle=|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\psi\rangle=c_n\,|a_n\rangle \]

The note on the board, “is not normalized”: its length is \(|c_n|\), not 1. To normalize, divide by its length. The square of the length is \(\langle\psi|\hat P_n^\dagger\hat P_n|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat P_n|\psi\rangle=p_n\); here \(\hat P^\dagger=\hat P\) and \(\hat P^2=\hat P\) were used together. So the length is \(\sqrt{p_n}\):

\[ |\psi_{\text{after}}\rangle=\frac{\hat P_n|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p_n}}=\frac{c_n}{|c_n|}\,|a_n\rangle \]

\(c_n/|c_n|\) is a phase factor of length 1, \(e^{i\arg c_n}\). Board 15 shows that it is physically irrelevant; as far as physics is concerned, the state after the measurement is \(|a_n\rangle\).

The result: if you immediately repeat the same measurement you will certainly find \(a_n\) again, because now the coefficient along \(a_n\) has length 1 and the others are 0. A measurement changes the state; this is called the collapse of the state.

The lecturer's summary in class went in this order: a projection represents a measurement; an observable is written with projections; the expectation value of a projection gives the probability; after a measurement the state is renormalized with \(\sqrt{p_n}\).

Predict first: \(|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.2}\,|a_1\rangle+i\sqrt{0.5}\,|a_2\rangle-\sqrt{0.3}\,|a_3\rangle\), with eigenvalues 1, 2, 3. What number will the average of 100 measurements be close to? Can a single measurement give that number?

The dashed frames are \(|c_n|^2\) and the filled bars are the measured frequency. The i and the minus sign do not change the probabilities; a preparation for Board 15.

High school example

A coin tossed in the air. Before it lands, heads and tails are half and half; after it lands, you see the same face every time you look again. A quantum measurement is like this: the first result is random, and measuring again right after gives the same result. Where the analogy falls short: the face of the coin was actually decided in the air, we just did not know it. In a quantum state the result is not decided before the measurement; \(|\psi\rangle\) really is a superposition. The proof is the interference on Board 15: classical ignorance does not interfere.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why is \(\hat P_n|\psi\rangle\) divided by \(\sqrt{p_n}\) rather than \(p_n\)?

Normalizing means dividing a ket by its length, and the length is the square root of the squared norm. The squared norm of \(\hat P_n|\psi\rangle\) is \(p_n=|c_n|^2\) and its length is \(|c_n|=\sqrt{p_n}\). If you divided by \(p_n\) the length would be \(1/|c_n|\), not 1.

Board 15864112

Global phase is invisible, relative phase is visible

This board is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this board: .

In one sentenceMultiplying a whole ket by the same \(e^{i\varphi}\) changes no measurement; but a phase difference between the parts of a superposition changes the probabilities when you measure in another basis.

Global (overall) phase. \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(e^{i\varphi}|\psi\rangle\) are the same physical state. The reason: everything measured contains a bra and a ket; on going to the bra the phase is conjugated and the two cancel:

\[ \langle\psi|e^{-i\varphi}\,\hat P_n\,e^{i\varphi}|\psi\rangle=e^{-i\varphi}e^{i\varphi}\,\langle\psi|\hat P_n|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\hat P_n|\psi\rangle \]

\(e^{i\varphi}\) is a number; it can pass through the operator and come out. Probabilities and expectation values are all the same. This is why the factor \(c_n/|c_n|\) on Board 14 does not matter.

Relative phase. Now put the phase on only one component:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\big) \]

If you measure in the \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\) basis the probabilities are \(|1/\sqrt2|^2=\tfrac12\) and \(|e^{i\varphi}/\sqrt2|^2=\tfrac12\), and φ does not show at all. But if, as the board suggests, you ask along the \(|+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\) direction:

\[ \langle+|\psi\rangle=\frac12\big(\langle0|+\langle1|\big)\big(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\big)=\frac{1+e^{i\varphi}}{2} \]
\[ p_+=|\langle+|\psi\rangle|^2=\frac{|1+e^{i\varphi}|^2}{4}=\frac{2+2\cos\varphi}{4}=\cos^2\frac{\varphi}{2} \]

At φ = 0, \(p_+=1\): the state is exactly \(|+\rangle\). At φ = π, \(p_+=0\): the state is \(|-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)\), orthogonal to \(|+\rangle\). These two states gave identical statistics in the \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\) basis, yet they are orthogonal. The difference comes from interference: when two amplitudes add, they reinforce if their phases are the same and cancel if opposite.

Predict first: when you turn φ from 0 to 180°, what do the two bars on the left do, and the two on the right? Then turn the global phase: does anything change?

On the left the sum \(1+e^{i\varphi}\) is drawn tip to tail: gray arrow 1, red arrow \(e^{i\varphi}\), blue arrow the sum. \(p_+\) is a quarter of the squared length of the blue arrow. A global phase rotates the whole picture and does not change the lengths.

The rule for the exam: if a phase multiplies all the terms together, drop it; if it multiplies only some of the terms, never drop it. This is exactly the language of qubits (\(|0\rangle\), \(|1\rangle\)), and most quantum gates play with relative phase. The lecturer left the operations in this section as homework.

You cannot notice this by listening to each alone; it appears only when both are heard together. Noise-canceling headphones use this cancellation. Moving both speakers back by the same amount is a global phase: nothing changes anywhere.
