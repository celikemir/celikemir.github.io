The birth of quantum physics

The first lecture of the term. After the course logistics we looked at the experiments that drove classical physics into a dead end between 1900 and 1927: blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, the line spectrum of hydrogen, Compton scattering and electron diffraction. Two ideas come out of these experiments. Light and matter both behave sometimes like particles and sometimes like waves, and a wave confined to a region quantizes on its own. The last two boards set up the language we will use for the rest of the term: operators and second quantization.

Planck and the blackbody

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceTo explain the blackbody spectrum, Planck assumed in 1900 that radiation energy can only be absorbed and emitted in packets of size \(E=h\nu\), with \(h=6.626\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}\).

A blackbody is an ideal surface that absorbs all light falling on it. In practice a small hole in the wall of a heated box behaves this way: light that enters cannot get out again, and the light that comes out depends only on the temperature of the box. In 1859 Kirchhoff realized that the spectrum of this radiation does not depend on the material and is a universal function of temperature and wavelength alone. Physicists spent the next 40 years looking for this function.

The classical answer came from Rayleigh. Every vibrational mode in the box receives an average energy \(kT\), like any degree of freedom in thermal equilibrium. Since the number of modes grows with the square of the frequency:

\[ u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}\,kT \]

At low frequency this agrees with measurements. But at high frequency it grows without bound, and summing over all frequencies gives an infinite energy in the box. This breakdown was later named the ultraviolet catastrophe.

Planck found the solution at the end of 1900, at the price of a radical assumption: radiation of frequency \(\nu\) can exchange energy with the walls only in packets of size \(h\nu\), that is, in quanta. This is the box on the board:

\[ E=h\nu,\qquad [h]=\frac{\text{J}}{1/\text{s}}=\text{J·s} \]

Exciting a high frequency mode takes \(h\nu\) at once. If \(h\nu\) is much larger than the thermal energy \(kT\), this almost never happens and the mode stays frozen. The result is the Planck distribution:

\[ u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi h}{c^3}\,\frac{\nu^3}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1} \]

At low frequency, where \(e^{x}-1\approx x\), it reduces to Rayleigh's formula; at high frequency it dies off exponentially. Two experimental laws also follow from the same formula, and for the first time their numerical values can be calculated: the total energy grows as \(T^4\) (Stefan–Boltzmann) and the peak shifts according to \(\lambda_{\max}T=2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}\) (Wien). In the plot below you can change the temperature and watch this shift.

The blue curve is Planck, the dashed curve is Rayleigh–Jeans. Both are at the same temperature and on the same scale, and the vertical axis is normalized to the peak. The two curves coincide at long wavelengths, and at short wavelengths the classical curve rises without bound.

High school example

An iron rod heated on a stove glows dark red first, then orange and finally whitish. In high school you learn this as “the color shifts toward blue as temperature rises”, and that is Wien's law in everyday form. For Planck's assumption, think of coins: if a vending machine only accepts 1 lira coins, you cannot pay 2.50 lira. Energy exchange also happens in coins, and the size of the coin is \(h\nu\). At high frequency the coin is so large that thermal energy can almost never pay it.

The photoelectric effect

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceA photon gives all of its energy to a single electron; the electron spends W of it to escape the metal and the rest becomes its kinetic energy: \(\tfrac12m_ev^2=h\nu-W\).

In 1887, during his electromagnetic wave experiments, Hertz noticed that sparks jump more easily when ultraviolet light falls on metal surfaces. Later experiments came down to four observations:

  • A metal under light emits electrons.
  • Whether electrons come out depends on the wavelength: every metal has a threshold frequency, and below it there is no current no matter how strong the light is.
  • The current is proportional to the intensity of the light.
  • The energy of the electrons does not depend on intensity and grows linearly with frequency. They also come out the moment light arrives; no delay longer than \(10^{-9}\) s has ever been seen.

The classical wave picture cannot explain the last two. The energy carried by a wave is proportional to its intensity and has nothing to do with frequency, and in weak light an electron would have to wait to collect enough energy. In 1905 Einstein went one step beyond Planck: not only the exchange, but light itself is made of quanta of \(h\nu\). One quantum is absorbed by a single electron all at once. The equation on the board:

\[ \frac12m_ev^2=h\nu-W \]

W is the work function of the metal (written “working function” on the board): the smallest energy needed to remove the most loosely bound electron from the metal, on the order of a few eV and specific to the metal. Electrons from deeper down lose more energy, so the left side is really the maximum kinetic energy, \(K_{\max}\).

All four observations are in this line. If \(h\nu<W\), no electrons come out: the threshold frequency is \(\nu_0=W/h\) and the threshold wavelength is \(\lambda_0=hc/W\). \(K_{\max}\) grows linearly with frequency with slope h. Raising the intensity raises the number of photons arriving per second, not the energy of each photon, so more electrons come out while \(K_{\max}\) stays the same. There is no delay because the photon is absorbed in one go.

The energy of the electrons is measured with a retarding voltage. A reverse voltage is applied to the collector plate and the value at which the current just reaches zero, the stopping potential \(V_s\), is read off: \(eV_s=K_{\max}\). Working in eV, \(K_{\max}\) and \(V_s\) are the same number. Although he did not believe in quanta, in 1915 Millikan plotted \(V_s\) against frequency and found \(h/e\) from the slope exactly as Einstein had predicted. In the lithium data in the book, the threshold is \(\nu_0\approx5.7\times10^{14}\) Hz, that is, \(W\approx2.4\) eV.

Predict first: You shine red light on sodium and no electrons come out. What happens if you make the intensity 4 times larger? Then shorten the wavelength and find the threshold.

metal

The blue line is \(K_{\max}=h\nu-W\), with slope h. The red dashed line is the threshold frequency \(\nu_0=W/h\). The work functions are approximate: cesium 2.1 eV, sodium 2.3 eV, zinc 4.3 eV. Intensity only changes the number of photons per second.

High school example

Opening a piggy bank takes a 2 lira token, and each child holds a single coin. A thousand children with 1 lira each can line up and none of them can open it, because the coins cannot be combined. One child with 5 lira opens it and gets 3 lira back. The children are photons, the coin in their hand is \(h\nu\), the price of the token is W and the change is \(K_{\max}\). Intensity is the number of children in line.

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: which two features of the photoelectric effect can the classical wave picture not explain?

One is the threshold: in the wave picture any sufficiently intense light should eventually knock out an electron, and frequency should not matter. The other is that the energy is independent of intensity and linear in frequency. There is also the lack of delay: in weak light the electron should have needed time to collect energy.

Step 4Board 4

Rutherford's two problems and Bohr's postulates

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn the Rutherford atom the electron should radiate, fall into the nucleus and give a continuous spectrum; in 1913 Bohr rescued both stability and the discrete spectrum with three postulates.

In 1908, under Rutherford's direction, Geiger and Marsden sent alpha particles at thin gold foils and saw an unexpected number of them scatter back at large angles. The only explanation was that nearly all of the positive charge and mass was concentrated in a very small nucleus at the center of the atom. The electrons had to orbit the nucleus like planets, under a \(1/r^2\) force. This is the drawing on the board: +Ze in the middle, e⁻ on the circle.

The model explains alpha scattering but has two problems:

  • Stability. In classical electrodynamics an accelerating charge radiates. An electron moving on a circle is always accelerating, so it should keep losing energy and spiral into the nucleus. According to the estimate in the book, the fall from an orbit of \(10^{-10}\) m takes about \(10^{-10}\) s. Atoms, however, are perfectly stable.
  • Spectrum. Classically the frequency of the emitted light is the orbital frequency. As the electron spirals in, this frequency changes continuously and a continuous spectrum comes out. The hydrogen spectrum, however, is just a few sharp lines. In 1885 Balmer had found that the wavelengths of these lines fit a simple formula: \(1/\lambda=R\,(1/n_1^2-1/n_2^2)\), with \(n_1,n_2\) integers.

In 1913 Bohr proposed three postulates that deliberately break with classical physics. In the order on the board:

  1. Discrete stable orbits. An atom can exist only in stationary states of certain energies, and it does not radiate in these states. The word “stable” is boxed on the board: the stability problem is solved by an assumption.
  2. Transitions. Energy is absorbed or emitted only by jumping between two stable orbits. If \(E_1>E_2\), the frequency of the emitted light is \(h\nu=E_1-E_2\).
  3. Quantization of angular momentum (for circular orbits). This is the topic of the next step.

The most radical part of the second postulate is that ν is no longer the orbital frequency of the electron. The frequency of the light is set by the energy difference between two levels, not by the motion in the orbit. This is the real break with classical physics.

High school example

Think of a ramp and a staircase. On a ramp you can stop at any height; on a staircase only on the steps. Someone walking down the stairs gives up exactly one step's worth of energy each time, no more and no less. Every line in the hydrogen spectrum is such a step difference. Since the steps are not evenly spaced, neither are the lines.

Angular momentum: nħ

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceBohr's third postulate is written as \(\tfrac12m_ev^2=\tfrac12nhf\); putting \(f=v/2\pi r\) for a circular orbit gives \(m_evr=n\hbar\): the angular momentum is an integer multiple of \(\hbar\).

The board has the postulate in its original form: the kinetic energy is related to the orbital frequency like this:

\[ \frac12m_ev^2=\frac12\,n\,h\,f,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots \]

f is the frequency at which the electron orbits the nucleus. One turn of a circular orbit is \(2\pi r\) and takes time T, so \(v=2\pi r/T\) and \(f=1/T=v/2\pi r\). Substitute:

\[ \frac12m_ev^2=\frac12\,n\,h\,\frac{v}{2\pi r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_ev=\frac{nh}{2\pi r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_evr=n\,\frac{h}{2\pi}\equiv n\hbar \]

At the first arrow the ½ and one v cancel on the two sides. \(m_evr\) is the angular momentum L on a circular orbit. The result: angular momentum cannot take a continuous value and is limited to integer multiples of \(\hbar=h/2\pi=1.0546\times10^{-34}\) J·s.

The unit calculation at the top right of the board shows why this is not surprising:

\[ [h]=\text{J·s}=\text{N·m·s}=\text{kg}\,\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2}\,\text{s}=\text{kg}\,\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}},\qquad [m_evr]=\text{kg}\cdot\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\cdot\text{m}=\text{kg}\,\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}} \]

Planck's constant carries the unit of angular momentum. You can think of \(\hbar\) as the natural unit of angular momentum; in quantum mechanics angular momenta will always come out as multiples of \(\hbar\). At the bottom of the board there is also a note “ν, the Greek letter nu”: the \(\nu\) in \(E=h\nu\) is frequency, with unit 1/s, while v is speed.

Non-circular orbits: the Sommerfeld–Wilson rule (in the book, not on the board)

This is the reason for the note “for circular orbits” on the board. On elliptical orbits \(m_evr\) is not a constant. Sommerfeld and Wilson generalized the rule to every degree of freedom: \(\oint p_k\,dx_k=n_kh\), with the integral over a closed orbit. On a circular orbit, since \(\oint p\,ds=m_ev\cdot2\pi r\), it reduces to \(m_evr=n\hbar\). The rule was not much use beyond hydrogen-like atoms; the real solution came with quantum mechanics in 1925.

High school example

You swing a 2 kg stone at 3 m/s on the end of a 0.5 m string. The angular momentum is \(L=mvr=2\cdot3\cdot0.5=3\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}\). Written in units of \(\hbar\), that is \(n\approx3\times10^{34}\). The next allowed value is \(n+1\), and the difference is too small to measure; that is why you do not feel quantization in everyday life.

Force balance and the bound state

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe Coulomb attraction provides the centripetal force; it follows that the potential energy is −2 times the kinetic energy, and the total energy \(E=-\tfrac12m_ev^2\) is negative: the electron is bound to the nucleus.

The road to the energy levels goes through Newton's second law in the radial direction. The only force on the electron is the Coulomb attraction of the nucleus, with magnitude \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Ze\cdot e}{r^2}\) and directed toward the center. In circular motion the acceleration also points toward the center, with magnitude \(v^2/r\). On the board the radial direction is first taken positive outward, with a minus sign on the left; in the boxed line the sign is gone. This is not a mistake: since force and acceleration point the same way, equating their magnitudes is enough.

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Ze^2}{r^2}=m_e\,\frac{v^2}{r}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,Ze^2=m_ev^2\,r \]

The total mechanical energy calculation on the blackboard uses this. The potential energy is \(V=-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\), and from the force balance \(\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}=m_ev^2\):

\[ E_e=K+V=\frac12m_ev^2-\underbrace{\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}}_{m_ev^2}=-\frac12m_ev^2 \]

A fixed ratio appears between the three quantities: \(V=-2K\) and \(E=-K=V/2\). The total energy is negative. This is what the note “bound state” on the board means: zero energy is the state where the electron is at rest infinitely far from the nucleus. An electron with negative energy cannot get there on its own; to remove it you must supply at least \(|E|\) of energy from outside. This energy is called the ionization energy.

High school example

A satellite on a circular orbit around the Earth follows the same calculation: \(\frac{GMm}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}\). The total energy of the satellite is \(-\tfrac12mv^2\), negative, and to free it from the Earth you have to supply the escape energy. The force law is \(1/r^2\) in both cases; \(Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\) just takes the place of \(GMm\).

Explain in your own words, then open and compare: why does the total energy come out as minus the kinetic energy?

The force balance says \(Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r=m_ev^2\), so the magnitude of the potential energy is twice the kinetic energy. Since its sign is negative, \(K+V=K-2K=-K\).

Particle and wave

In the photoelectric effect light behaved as packets of energy. In this part we see that the packets also carry momentum (Einstein 1916, Compton 1923) and, conversely, that particles have a wavelength too (de Broglie 1923, Davisson–Germer 1927). The duality runs both ways.

Step 9Board 7

The momentum of a photon

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe relativistic energy-momentum relation is \(E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4\); for the massless photon \(E=pc\), and combining this with \(E=h\nu\) and \(c=\lambda\nu\) gives \(p=h/\lambda\).

In 1916 Einstein showed that light quanta must carry a direction and a momentum as well as energy. A packet of energy that has momentum, travels in a definite direction and can collide: a particle-like nature. These packets were later named photons.

Relativity tells us how much momentum there is. For a particle of mass m, the total energy and the momentum are related by:

\[ E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4 \]

On the board the sign is written as minus; it should be plus. It is easy to check: put \(p=0\) for a particle at rest. With plus you get \(E=mc^2\), Einstein's rest energy. With minus you get \(E^2=-m^2c^4\), an imaginary energy. The board also has “relativistic kinetic energy” written under E. This E is not kinetic but the total relativistic energy, which includes the rest energy. The kinetic energy is \(K=E-mc^2\).

For the photon the mass is zero. The book's reasoning goes like this: from the relation, the speed of the particle is \(v=dE/dp=pc^2/E\). If the photon travels at c for every momentum, then \(pc^2/E=c\), so we need \(E=pc\), and this is only possible with \(m=0\). The rest is the chain on the board:

\[ E=pc,\quad E=h\nu,\quad c=\lambda\nu\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac{h\nu}{c}=\frac{h}{\lambda} \]

The unit check is on the right edge of the board: \([h]/[\lambda]=(\text{kg·m}^2/\text{s})/\text{m}=\text{kg·m/s}\), the unit of momentum. The momentum of a visible photon is on the order of \(10^{-27}\) kg·m/s. You cannot feel one, but when trillions of them keep hitting a surface they create a measurable pressure.

Compton scattering

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceAn X-ray photon scatters off an electron at rest like a billiard ball; with energy and momentum conserved together, the wavelength of the scattered photon gets longer by \(\lambda'-\lambda=\lambda_C(1-\cos\theta)\).

In 1923 Compton sent X-rays at a thin metal or carbon foil and measured the wavelength of the light scattered at a given angle θ. He found two components: one at the same wavelength as the incoming light, the other longer, with a shift that grows with the angle. In the carbon measurement in the book, the incoming line is 0.07078 nm and the shifted line 0.07314 nm.

The classical wave picture cannot explain the shift. The incoming wave makes the electron oscillate at its own frequency, and the electron re-radiates at the same frequency, so the wavelength does not change. Compton thought of the incoming light as photons of energy \(h\nu\) and momentum \(h/\lambda\), and solved each scattering as an elastic collision between a single photon and a single electron at rest. The red note on the board says exactly this: energy conservation plus momentum conservation.

\[ \underbrace{\frac{hc}{\lambda}+m_ec^2}_{\text{before}}=\underbrace{\frac{hc}{\lambda'}+\sqrt{p_e^2c^2+m_e^2c^4}}_{\text{after}},\qquad \vec p=\vec p\,'+\vec p_e \]

There are three unknowns: the scattered wavelength \(\lambda'\), the electron momentum \(p_e\) and the recoil angle. There are also three equations: energy and the x and y components of momentum. The plan: from the momentum equation, write \(p_e^2\) in terms of \(p\), \(p'\) and \(\cos\theta\) (this drops the recoil angle), then rearrange the energy equation and square it to eliminate \(p_e\). The single relation that remains:

\[ \lambda'-\lambda=\frac{h}{m_ec}\,(1-\cos\theta),\qquad \lambda_C=\frac{h}{m_ec}=2.426\ \text{pm} \]

\(\lambda_C\) is the Compton wavelength of the electron (\(2\pi\) times the \(\hbar/m_ec\) of Step 7). The two limiting cases of the result make a good check. At \(\theta=0\) there is no shift: the photon was not deflected and no collision took place. At \(\theta=\pi\) the shift is largest, \(2\lambda_C\approx4.85\) pm: the photon bounced back and transferred the most momentum to the electron.

Where does the unshifted component come from? Some photons scatter not from a single electron but from the whole atom, through tightly bound electrons. The mass of the atom replaces \(m_e\) in the formula, thousands of times larger, and the shift becomes too small to measure. Why is the effect not noticed with visible light? The shift is at most 5 pm, one hundred-thousandth of a 500 nm wavelength. For X-rays, the wavelength is of the same order as the shift.

The de Broglie wave

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn 1923 de Broglie turned the relation around: every particle of momentum p comes with a wavelength \(\lambda=h/p\). Electron diffraction confirmed this in 1927.

Light was thought to be a wave and turned out to have a particle side. De Broglie proposed completing the symmetry: matter is thought of as particles, so it should have a wave side too. This is the “dual picture of wave-particle for matter” on the board. The wavelength is the same relation as for the photon:

\[ \lambda=\frac{h}{p} \]

The most direct proof of a wave is interference and diffraction. If a beam of electrons is sent at a regular array of atoms, a crystal, strong reflection appears when the path difference between waves reflected from neighboring atomic planes is an integer multiple of the wavelength. The condition Bragg found for X-rays in 1912:

\[ n\lambda=2a\sin\theta \]

a is the distance between planes. In 1927 two experiments showed that electrons obey this condition: Davisson and Germer with electrons of about 54 eV reflected from a nickel crystal, and G. P. Thomson and A. Reid with electrons passing through thin metal foils. These are the two pairs of names on the board.

For a non-relativistic electron \(p=\sqrt{2m_eK}\), so:

\[ \lambda=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_eK}}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda\,[\text{nm}]\approx\frac{1.226}{\sqrt{K\,[\text{eV}]}} \]

For 54 eV this is about 0.17 nm, the same order as the atomic spacing in the crystal. This is exactly what diffraction requires. Electron microscopes work on the same idea: the wavelength of high energy electrons is thousands of times shorter than that of light, so they can resolve much smaller details. The book also describes the double slit experiment with electrons sent one at a time (Tonomura): each electron leaves a single dot on the screen, but when thousands accumulate, interference fringes appear.

We do not see wave behavior in everyday objects because h is so small. A 145 g baseball moving at 40 m/s has a wavelength on the order of \(10^{-34}\) m, nineteen orders of magnitude shorter than the size of a proton. No slit or crystal is that narrow.

Step 12Boards 8–9

Quantization from a boundary condition

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceWhen a wave is confined to a limited region, only the wavelengths that satisfy the boundary condition survive; on a circle the condition is \(2\pi r=n\lambda\), and with \(\lambda=h/m_ev\) this is exactly \(m_evr=n\hbar\).

The title of section 1.1: the Bohr atom model (old quantum condition) as a boundary condition. The rule on the board is general: if you confine a particle-wave to a finite region with boundaries, quantization comes from the boundary condition. We will see this all over quantum mechanics: the particle in a box, the particle in a well, the atom.

Let us apply it to the Bohr orbit. The de Broglie wavelength of the electron is \(\lambda_{dB}=h/p=h/m_ev\). The wave travels along the circle and after one turn comes back to where it started. There the value of the wave must be single valued: it cannot take two different values at the same point. Measuring arc length with s:

\[ \psi(s)=\psi(s+2\pi r)\qquad\text{tahtada: }\ \psi(0)=\psi(2\pi r)=\psi(n\,2\pi r) \]

This only works if a whole number of wavelengths fits around the circumference; the wave closes on itself and forms a standing wave. This is the orange drawing on the board:

\[ 2\pi r=n\,\lambda_{dB}=n\,\frac{h}{m_ev}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m_evr=n\,\frac{h}{2\pi}=n\hbar \]

Bohr's third postulate has gone from an assumption to a result. The rest is as in Step 7: combine it with the force balance and \(E_n\propto1/n^2\) follows (“from here \(E_n\propto1/n^2\) follows etc.”).

What happens if it is not a whole number? On its second turn the wave lands on top of the first slightly shifted, on the third a bit more. After hundreds of turns the crests and troughs fall everywhere and cancel each other, and nothing is left. How accurate is this picture? (not on the board)

The standing wave picture gives the correct energies and shows very well where quantization comes from, but it is not the last word. When we solve hydrogen with the Schrödinger equation, the angular momentum of the ground state will come out as zero rather than \(\hbar\); the electron is not a point circling on an orbit but a spherical probability cloud around the nucleus. The energies, however, will stay the same as Bohr's. The book gives the standing wave calculation as an exercise at the end of Chapter 1 (Problem 12).

The electron in an atom has no string, but the orbit is a closed path: its ends are not fixed but joined to each other. The condition becomes \(L=n\lambda\).

Step 13Board 10

Quantities become operators

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn standard quantum mechanics classical quantities become operators; in position space \(\hat x\) multiplies the wave function by x, and \(\hat p\) takes its derivative with \(-i\hbar\,d/dx\).

The title of 1.2 is first quantization, with “standard (non-relativistic) quantum mechanics” in parentheses. The rest of the term is in this language. The rule is short: position, momentum and every quantity built from them, which are numbers in classical mechanics, become operators in quantum mechanics.

\[ x\;\to\;\hat x,\qquad p\;\to\;\hat p \]

An operator is an operation that takes a function and gives another function. What it acts on is also clear: the quantum state of the system, the wave function \(\psi(x)\). In position space, that is, when we write the wave function as a function of x, the two act like this:

\[ \hat x\,\psi(x)=x\,\psi(x),\qquad \hat p\,\psi(x)=-i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} \]

\(\hat x\) means “multiply by x”, \(\hat p\) means “take the derivative and multiply by \(-i\hbar\)”. i is the imaginary unit, \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). The lecturer left the reason for this form of the momentum for later (“we will discuss the specific form”); it was derived from translations in week 6.

Next to the line \(\hat x\psi=x\psi\) the board says “eigenvalue equation”, and under the x on the right “position eigenvalue”. This wording needs care. In an eigenvalue equation the number on the right side is a constant: \(\hat A\phi=a\,\phi\). Here x is not a constant but a variable; the line defines how \(\hat x\) acts on any function. The true eigenfunctions of \(\hat x\) are Dirac deltas concentrated at a single point:

\[ \hat x\,\delta(x-x_0)=x\,\delta(x-x_0)=x_0\,\delta(x-x_0) \]

The last equality holds because the delta is nonzero only at \(x=x_0\). The eigenvalue \(x_0\) is the position where the particle is found with certainty. Delta functions are the topic of week 6.

The eigenfunctions of \(\hat p\) are plane waves, and there is a nice connection here:

\[ \hat p\,e^{ikx}=-i\hbar\,(ik)\,e^{ikx}=\hbar k\,e^{ikx},\qquad p=\hbar k=\frac{h}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}=\frac{h}{\lambda} \]

A wave of wavelength λ has momentum \(h/\lambda\): the de Broglie relation is built into the operator.

Board note

\(\hat x\psi(x)=x\psi(x)\) is not an eigenvalue equation but the definition of \(\hat x\) in position space; the x on the right is a variable, not a constant. The eigenvalue equation is \(\hat x\,\delta(x-x_0)=x_0\,\delta(x-x_0)\) with eigenvalue \(x_0\).

High school example

Think of an operator as a machine: you drop in a function and another function comes out. Drop \(x^3\) into the “take the derivative” machine and \(3x^2\) comes out, with a different shape. Drop in \(e^{2x}\) and \(2e^{2x}\) comes out: the same function, just multiplied by 2. A function that the machine only multiplies by a number, without changing its shape, is called an eigenfunction, and that number is its eigenvalue.

The commutator: order matters

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe order in which you apply \(\hat x\) and \(\hat p\) changes the result: \(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x=i\hbar\). This is the line that separates quantum mechanics from classical mechanics.

The commutator of two operators is the difference between applying them in the two orders:

\[ [\hat A,\hat B]\equiv\hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A \]

For numbers this is always zero: \(3\cdot5=5\cdot3\). For operators it is not. Try \(\hat x\) and \(\hat p\) on a concrete function, \(f=x^2\):

\[ \hat x\hat p\,x^2=x\cdot\big(-i\hbar\cdot2x\big)=-2i\hbar\,x^2,\qquad \hat p\hat x\,x^2=-i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}\big(x^3\big)=-3i\hbar\,x^2 \]
\[ (\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x)\,x^2=-2i\hbar\,x^2+3i\hbar\,x^2=i\hbar\,x^2 \]

The difference is \(i\hbar\) times the function itself. Whatever function you choose you get the same thing, and the reason is the product rule of the derivative, \((xf)'=f+xf'\). The difference between “multiply by x, then differentiate” and “differentiate, then multiply by x” is always \(f\) itself. That is why it is written as an operator:

\[ [\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar \]

The derivation with a general \(\psi(x)\) is on the week 7 page, in Step 13. Why is it so important? If two quantities do not commute you cannot know both with definite values at the same time; the uncertainty principle \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\) will come from this commutator. In the limit \(\hbar\to0\) the commutator goes to zero and we return to classical quantities that behave like numbers.

High school example

Putting on socks and then shoes is not the same as shoes and then socks: order matters. Putting on the right glove and then the left is the same as the reverse: order does not matter. The commutator is the numerical answer to the question “does order matter?”. The commutator of x and p is not zero; the two are like socks and shoes.

The Hamiltonian operator

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceWhen the classical energy function \(H=p^2/2m+V\) becomes an operator it turns into \(\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x)\); this operator represents energy measurement.

Let us apply the rule to the most important quantity, energy. In classical mechanics the Hamiltonian is the total energy written in terms of position and momentum (\(p=mv\)):

\[ H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V=\frac12mv^2+V \]

Going to quantum mechanics, \(p\to\hat p\), \(x\to\hat x\). \(\hat p^2\) means applying \(\hat p\) twice:

\[ \hat p^2\psi=\Big(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\Big)\Big(-i\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dx}\Big)=(-i)^2\hbar^2\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=-\hbar^2\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} \]
\[ \hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+V(\hat x)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x),\qquad \hat H\psi(x)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}+V(x)\,\psi(x) \]

In both lines on the board \(1/2m\) has been dropped, and on the right h is written instead of \(\hbar\). A unit check catches this: the unit of \(\hbar^2\,d^2/dx^2\) is \((\text{kg·m}^2/\text{s})^2/\text{m}^2=\text{kg}\cdot\text{J}\); to be an energy it has to be divided by a mass.

The note “related to energy measurement” on the board means that the possible results of an energy measurement are the eigenvalues of \(\hat H\). The equation \(\hat H\psi=E\psi\) is the time-independent Schrödinger equation, and how the wave function changes in time is also determined by \(\hat H\), \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi=\hat H\psi\). Both are the topic of week 2. For a free particle (\(V=0\)), try a plane wave:

\[ \hat H\,e^{ikx}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(ik)^2e^{ikx}=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,e^{ikx}=\frac{p^2}{2m}\,e^{ikx} \]

The eigenvalue is exactly the classical kinetic energy; the operator gives the correct energy. If \(1/2m\) were missing the result would be \(\hbar^2k^2\), which is not even an energy.

Board note

In the lines \(\hat H=-\hbar^2\,\partial^2/\partial x^2+V(\hat x)\) and \(\hat H\psi=-h^2\,d^2\psi/dx^2+V\psi\), \(1/2m\) is missing; on the right it should be \(\hbar\) instead of h. The correct form is \(\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\).

High school example

In high school the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12mv^2\). Written with momentum it is \(p^2/2m\): for a 2 kg car with momentum 6 kg·m/s the speed is 3 m/s and the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot9=9\) J; \(36/4=9\) J, the same. In quantum mechanics we have to write it with momentum, because the quantity that becomes an operator is momentum, not velocity.

Second quantization

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn first quantization x and p were operators and \(\psi\) was an ordinary function; in second quantization \(\psi\) itself becomes an operator: \(\hat\psi(x)=\sum_nu_n(x)\,\hat a_n\).

The last topic of the lecture is 1.3, second quantization or field quantization. The lecturer connected it to quantum optics: what gets quantized there is not the position of a particle but the field itself. The line on the board:

\[ \hat\psi(x)=\sum_n u_n(x)\,\hat a_n \]

It reads like this. \(u_n(x)\) are the allowed vibration patterns in a cavity or on a string, the modes, like the standing waves in Step 12. A classical field is a sum of these modes with numbers as coefficients. In second quantization the coefficients become operators. \(\hat a_n\) is the annihilation operator, which lowers the number of quanta in the nth mode by one; its conjugate \(\hat a_n^\dagger\) is the creation operator, which raises it by one. \(\hat a_n^\dagger\hat a_n\) counts the quanta in that mode.

So the week returns to where it began. Planck's assumption was that “radiation of frequency ν exchanges energy in packets of \(h\nu\)”. In second quantization this is a result: the energy of a mode of frequency \(\omega_n\) changes in steps of \(\hbar\omega_n\), each step being the creation or annihilation of one photon. A photon is a quantum excitation of one mode of the electromagnetic field. Applied to electrons, the same language becomes the basic language of quantum field theory and solid state physics.

On the board \(\hat a\) has no index n; since each mode has its own operator it should be \(\hat a_n\). At the start of week 2 there was a side question about creation and annihilation operators for bosons and fermions, and that page has a short explanation. The real calculation will come with the harmonic oscillator: \(\hat a\) and \(\hat a^\dagger\) appear there as ladder operators.

Board note

In the line \(\hat\psi(x)=\sum_nu_n(x)\,\hat a\), an index n should be added to \(\hat a\): \(\hat a_n\). Each term of the sum carries the operator of its own mode.

High school example

Picture a movie theater. Each row is a mode, and the number of people sitting in a row is the number of photons in that mode. \(\hat a_n\) means “remove one person from row n”, \(\hat a_n^\dagger\) means “seat one person in row n”. Which rows the theater has, the \(u_n(x)\), does not change; what changes is how full they are. Describing a state of light in quantum optics means saying how many people sit in which row.
