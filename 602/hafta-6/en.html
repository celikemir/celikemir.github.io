    Part A · notebook pages 1–3

    Splitting a pulse into its frequencies

    A light pulse in vacuum: which frequencies it is made of and how we calculate that.

    Step 1

    Why is a single wave not enough?

    In shortA single-frequency wave has no beginning or end and goes on forever. Real signals start and stop.

    Until now we have always worked with this wave: \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\). It has a single frequency, \(\omega\). We call it monochromatic, a single-color wave. The problem: this wave has neither a start nor an end, and it oscillates with the same amplitude from the beginning of time to the end. A laser pulse, a radar signal or a bit in a fiber starts and stops.

    Everyday example

    Think of a single whistle tone playing forever and the short “beep” of a phone. The beep has a start and an end. You cannot make a beep with a single note, because a single note never stops. For a beep you must mix many notes so that they silence each other at every moment outside the beep.

    We call this mixture a wave packet. The integral we wrote at the end of week 5 was exactly this mixture. This week we calculated it to the end for a concrete example, the Gaussian pulse.

    Check · step 1

    Which is truly single-frequency?

    1. It has a start and an end. Every signal that starts and stops contains more than one frequency.

    2. Single frequency = infinite length. Anything with a beginning is a packet.

    3. The color of a laser looks single, but as a pulse it has a range of frequencies. The shorter the pulse, the wider the range, as you will see in step 9.

    4. A clap is very short, so it is a very wide mixture of frequencies. The exact opposite of a single frequency.

    Keep in mind

    Either infinite and single-frequency, or finite and multi-frequency. You cannot have both.

    Step 2

    A packet is a sum of many waves

    In shortIf you add waves of different frequencies in the right proportions, they reinforce each other in one place and cancel everywhere else. The place where they reinforce is the packet itself.

    $$E(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\;A(\omega)\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$$

    Read this like a recipe: take \(A(\omega)\) of the wave at each frequency \(\omega\) and add them all. \(A(\omega)\) is the recipe itself, how much of each frequency is in the packet. Where we launch the pulse, at \(x=0\), this sum must equal the signal we send, \(u(t)\):

    $$u(t)=E(0,t)=\int d\omega\;A(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}$$
    High school link

    Interference. If the crests of two waves line up they reinforce (constructive interference); if a crest meets a trough they cancel (destructive interference). A packet is many waves interfering constructively in only one region and destructively everywhere else.

    When you add a finite number of waves, the packet repeats itself at regular intervals. As you pack the waves more densely, the repeats move away. An infinite number of waves, an integral, pushes the repeats to infinity and leaves a single packet. That is why we write an integral instead of a sum.

    Check · step 2

    What does \(A(\omega)\) in the formula describe?

    1. That is the job of the position x. A depends only on ω and is independent of x and t.

    2. This is the recipe: each frequency's share in the packet, its amplitude.

    3. Speed comes from the relation between k and ω (in vacuum k = ω/c). A only gives the amount.

    4. Decay will come from the imaginary part of k in Part B. A is an amount, not a decay.

    Keep in mind

    \(u(t)\) and \(A(\omega)\) are two descriptions of the same packet. One says “how it looks in time”, the other “which frequencies it is made of”.

    Step 3

    Recovering the recipe: the radio tuning trick

    In shortWe know \(u(t)\) and are looking for \(A(\omega)\). If you multiply \(u(t)\) by \(e^{i\omega' t}\) and add up over all time, only the part at frequency \(\omega'\) survives.

    The lecturer used this trick: multiply both sides by \(e^{i\omega' t}\) and integrate over t.

    $$\int u(t)\,e^{i\omega' t}\,dt=\int d\omega\,A(\omega)\int e^{-i(\omega-\omega')t}\,dt$$

    The inner integral on the right does this. If \(\omega\ne\omega'\), \(e^{-i(\omega-\omega')t}\) keeps rotating, the plus and minus parts cancel and the total is zero. When \(\omega=\omega'\) the rotation stops, the inside is always 1 and the integral grows without bound. Zero everywhere, infinite at one point: the Dirac delta, \(2\pi\,\delta(\omega-\omega')\). The delta is like a pair of tweezers, pulling only the value \(\omega=\omega'\) out of the integral:

    $$\int d\omega\,A(\omega)\,2\pi\,\delta(\omega-\omega')=2\pi A(\omega')$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad A(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt$$

    The lecturer called this the inverse Fourier transform (inverse FT). The name varies by convention; what matters is the formula. Combining the two gives the full recipe:

    $$E(x,t)=\int d\omega\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int u(t')\,e^{i\omega t'}\,dt'\right]e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$$

    The note in the notebook: this expression is for \(x\ge0\). We send the pulse from \(x=0\) in the +x direction.

    Everyday example

    A radio antenna picks up all stations at once. When you tune the radio to 98.4 it does roughly this: it multiplies the incoming signal by its own 98.4 oscillation and takes the average. The station at that frequency stays in the average, and the others die away in the ups and downs.

    Difference from the notebook

    The notebook says \(\int d\omega\,A(\omega)\,2\pi\,\delta(\omega-\omega')=A(\omega')\), with the 2π dropped on the right side. The correct form is \(2\pi A(\omega')\). The \(1/2\pi\) one line below comes exactly from this 2π, so the final formula is correct.

    Check · step 3

    Why do the frequencies with \(\omega\ne\omega'\) drop out of the integral?

    1. Their amplitudes are not zero; A(ω) exists there too. They drop out because the product oscillates.

    2. This is also why a radio does not hear the stations it does not want.

    3. The magnitude of \(e^{i\omega t}\) is 1, but it is a number rotating on the unit circle. This rotation is exactly what does the job.

    4. The delta is infinite only at ω = ω′ and zero everywhere else. It zeros out the others.

    Keep in mind

    This trick is the foundation of all Fourier analysis: multiply by the frequency you want, take the average, find that frequency's share.

    Step 4

    Example: the Gaussian wave packet

    In shortWe multiply a cosine by a bell curve. The cosine gives the oscillation, and the bell curve opens it in the middle and turns it down at the edges.

    $$u(t)=\cos(\omega_c t)\;e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}$$
    • \(\cos(\omega_c t)\): the carrier wave. \(\omega_c\) is called the carrier frequency. For a laser it is the color, for a radio the frequency of the station.
    • \(e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}\): the Gaussian envelope. Like a volume knob: 1 at \(t=0\), falling to zero on both sides.
    • \(\tau\): the temporal width. It says how wide the bell is.

    Why is the unit of τ seconds? The inside of an exponential must be unitless. For \(t^2/\tau^2\) to be unitless, τ also carries a unit of time. The notebook also says “dimension: time”.

    High school link

    The Gaussian curve is the bell curve you saw at school: the height distribution of a class or the grade distribution of an exam. Many in the middle, few at the edges.

    Check · step 4

    If you double τ, what happens to \(u(t)\)?

    1. The value of the new bell at t = 2τ equals the value of the old one at t = τ.

    2. The reverse. τ is in the denominator: as it grows t²/2τ² grows slowly, the bell falls slowly, so it widens.

    3. ω_c is a separate parameter. τ affects only the envelope.

    4. The peak is always at t = 0 and e⁰ = 1. τ changes the width, not the height.

    Keep in mind

    Large τ, long pulse. Small τ, short and sharp pulse.

    Step 5

    Measuring the width of a pulse: FWHM

    In shortWe measure the width of the bell at half the height of the peak. Its name is full width at half maximum, FWHM for short.

    Measuring from the base is useless, because a Gaussian never reaches exactly zero. Half height is a clear line. The calculation is high school math:

    $$e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}=\tfrac12\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2}=\ln2$$ $$\Longrightarrow\;t=\pm\tau\sqrt{2\ln2}\approx\pm1.18\,\tau$$ $$\text{FWHM}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\;\tau\approx2.35\,\tau$$
    Difference from the notebook

    On the board and in the notebook the FWHM appears as \(=2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\tau\), with a number noted next to it. Three different widths get mixed up here:

    What is measuredFormulaValue
    From the peak to half height, one side\(\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\tau\)≈ 1.18 τ
    FWHM of the field envelope, \(e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}\)\(2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\tau\)≈ 2.35 τ
    FWHM of the intensity, \(|u|^2\propto e^{-t^2/\tau^2}\)\(2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\tau\)≈ 1.67 τ

    The lecturer's \(2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\tau\approx1.7\,\tau\) is the third row, the width of the intensity, which is the square of the envelope. Since the duration of laser pulses is usually measured from the intensity, you will often meet that number too. For the field envelope we wrote, the correct one is the second row. In the exam say which one you use.

    Check · step 5

    fs

    CarefulThis is only one side, from the peak to half height. The full width is twice this.

    HintSolve the equation \(e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}=1/2\) and take the distance between the two solutions.

    Solution

    \(2\cdot\sqrt{2\cdot0.693}\cdot10\ \text{fs}=2\cdot1.177\cdot10\approx23.5\ \text{fs}\). For a sense of scale: light travels 0.3 µm in 1 fs.

    Step 6

    Calculation 1: split the cosine in two

    In shortWhen the cosine is written as two exponentials, everything inside the integral combines into a single exponential.

    To find the recipe of the Gaussian packet we put \(u(t)\) into the formula of step 3:

    $$A(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos(\omega_c t)\,e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt$$

    In the lecture the lecturer said “suggest a strategy”. The strategy is Euler's formula:

    $$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}$$

    Substituting, each term becomes a single exponential by the rule \(e^a e^b=e^{a+b}\). The \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{1}{2\pi}=\tfrac{1}{4\pi}\) in front:

    $$A(\omega)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\big(I_1+I_2\big)$$ $$I_1=\int e^{-\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2}+i(\omega+\omega_c)t}\,dt$$ $$I_2=\int e^{-\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2}+i(\omega-\omega_c)t}\,dt$$
    High school link

    The unit circle in complex numbers. \(e^{i\theta}\) is an arrow on the circle at angle θ. \(e^{-i\theta}\) is the mirror image of the same arrow about the x axis. If you add the two, the y components (sines) cancel and the x component doubles: \(2\cos\theta\). The lecturer also said “the sines cancel” in class.

    Check · step 6

    Why did we turn the cosine into exponentials?

    1. A cosine can be integrated. The problem is the product of the cosine with the Gaussian and \(e^{i\omega t}\).

    2. \(e^ae^b=e^{a+b}\), leaving the pattern \(\int e^{-ax^2+bx}dx\).

    3. The result, A(ω), comes out real. Imaginary numbers are a temporary tool in the intermediate steps.

    4. \(e^{i\theta}\) is generally a complex number, of magnitude 1 with a direction depending on θ.

    Keep in mind

    The reason for working with exponentials instead of cosines in physics is always the same: products become sums of exponents, and derivatives become multiplication.

    Step 7

    Calculation 2: completing the square

    In shortBoth integrals have the pattern \(\int e^{-ax^2+bx}\,dx\). We turn the quadratic expression in the exponent into “the square of something + a constant”.

    High school link

    You did this in high school: \(x^2+6x=(x+3)^2-9\). Take half of the coefficient of x (3), add and subtract its square (9). We do the same with letters.

    $$-ax^2+bx=-a\left(x^2-\frac{b}{a}x+\frac{b^2}{4a^2}-\frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right)$$ $$=-a\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+\frac{b^2}{4a}$$

    The notebook has the same lines. The integral then becomes:

    $$\int e^{-ax^2+bx}\,dx=e^{\frac{b^2}{4a}}\int e^{-a\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2}dx$$

    The constant \(e^{b^2/4a}\) has come out of the integral. A shifted bell remains inside.

    Check · step 7

    Complete the square for the expression \(-2x^2+8x\).

    1. a = 2, b = 8 → b/2a = 2, b²/4a = 64/8 = 8. Expanding gives −2x² + 8x − 8 + 8, which holds.

    2. Expand: −2(x² − 8x + 16) + 32 = −2x² + 16x. The coefficient of x became 16. The shift should be b/2a = 8/4 = 2.

    3. Expand: −2x² + 8x − 8 − 8 = −2x² + 8x − 16. The constant should have been zero. The sign is +b²/4a.

    4. Expand: −2x² − 8x − 8 + 8 = −2x² − 8x. The sign of x is reversed. The shift is x − b/2a.

    Keep in mind

    \(-ax^2+bx=-a\left(x-\tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2+\tfrac{b^2}{4a}\). Derive it once with your own hand and you will never forget it.

    Step 8

    Calculation 3: shifting does not change the area

    In shortSliding a bell left or right does not change the area under it. So the integral of a shifted bell is still \(\sqrt{\pi/a}\).

    $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2}dx=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ax^2+bx}dx=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\;e^{\frac{b^2}{4a}}$$

    Now let us put in our own values. In both integrals \(a=\dfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\). For \(I_1\), \(b=i(\omega+\omega_c)\); for \(I_2\), \(b=i(\omega-\omega_c)\):

    $$\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\tau$$ $$\frac{b^2}{4a}=\frac{i^2(\omega\pm\omega_c)^2}{4}\cdot2\tau^2=-\frac{(\omega\pm\omega_c)^2\tau^2}{2}$$

    The critical point is \(i^2=-1\). Thanks to this the exponent comes out negative and the result is again a bell:

    $$A(\omega)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\tau}{4\pi}\left[e^{-(\omega+\omega_c)^2\tau^2/2}+e^{-(\omega-\omega_c)^2\tau^2/2}\right]$$

    Reading it: there are two bells on the frequency axis. One at \(+\omega_c\) and one at \(-\omega_c\), both of width \(1/\tau\). They come from splitting the cosine into two exponentials. The bell at negative frequency is the mirror of the positive one; in a real signal they always come in pairs, and the physical information is in the bell at \(+\omega_c\).

    Everyday example

    Move a pile of sand sideways with a shovel, as it is. The place of the pile changed but the amount of sand is the same. An integral measures the area, the amount of sand.

    For the curious: the shift is actually imaginary

    Here the shift \(b/2a\) is an imaginary number. So we are sliding the bell not along the real axis but in the imaginary direction. Cauchy's theorem in complex analysis guarantees the result is still correct. The lecturer did not go into this detail; using the pattern is enough.

    Difference from the notebook

    In the notebook the sign of the exponential in the line \(I_1\) looks like “+”. Because of \(i^2=-1\) it should be minus. In the line \(A(\omega)\) on the next page, both exponentials are already written with a minus.

    Check · step 8

    How does \(A(\omega)\) look on the frequency axis?

    1. The two exponentials give two bells, and the Gaussian envelope gives them their Gaussian shape.

    2. A bell at ω = 0 would mean no carrier. The frequency of the cosine moves the bells to ±ω_c.

    3. A flat spectrum belongs to an infinitely short pulse (a delta). Our pulse has a width τ.

    4. An infinitely thin line belongs to an infinitely long cosine. Because of the envelope, the line spreads into a bell of width 1/τ.

    Keep in mind

    The Fourier transform of the envelope is again a Gaussian, and the carrier moves this bell to \(\omega_c\). Do not memorize the formula; remember these two sentences.

    Extra · quick check

    If \(b=3i\), what is \(b^2\)?

    1. You forgot the square of i: (3i)² = 9·i².

    2. (3i)² = 9·i² = −9. This is exactly why the exponent of A(ω) comes out negative.

    3. Squaring is not multiplying by 2.

    4. When squared no i remains; i² = −1 is a real number.

    Step 9

    Short pulse, wide spectrum

    In shortThe width in time is τ and the width in frequency 1/τ. When one shrinks the other grows, and their product is constant.

    The note in the notebook: “as u(t) sharpens τ shrinks, and A widens in ω space. Δω·Δt ≥ 1, the Fourier condition.” You can see this directly from the formula for A(ω): on the time side \(e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}\), on the frequency side \(e^{-(\omega-\omega_c)^2\tau^2/2}\). τ is in the denominator in one and in the numerator in the other.

    The exact number depends on how you define the width. Using τ and 1/τ here, the product for a Gaussian is exactly 1. If you measure with the standard deviation of the intensity, the lower bound comes out as 1/2, and only a Gaussian sits on that bound. What to remember: you cannot shrink both.

    Everyday example

    A drum hit is a very short sound containing almost every frequency. A note held long on a flute is almost a single frequency. The “bandwidth” of the internet is the same thing: sending more bits per second means shorter pulses, and a shorter pulse means a wider frequency band.

    A link to 601: the \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\) of quantum mechanics comes from the same Fourier fact. If a wave function is narrow in position it is wide in momentum.

    Check · step 9

    If you make a pulse 4 times shorter in time, what happens to the width of its spectrum?

    1. The opposite way. The widths in time and frequency are inversely proportional.

    2. If it did not change, the product would be 4 times smaller. The product has to stay constant.

    3. If τ → τ/4, then 1/τ → 4/τ.

    4. There is no square. The width goes as 1/τ, not 1/τ².

    Keep in mind

    That is why femtosecond laser pulses carry a very wide range of colors. Short pulse, wide spectrum.

    Step 10

    The journey through vacuum

    In shortIn vacuum all frequencies travel at the same speed, c. The packet slides along without changing shape at all.

    We found the recipe; now let us send the packet on its way. In vacuum \(k=\omega/c\). Putting this in, the exponential becomes:

    $$e^{i(kx-\omega t)}=e^{-i\omega\,(t-x/c)}$$ $$E(x,t)=\int d\omega\,A(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega(t-x/c)}=u\!\left(t-\frac{x}{c}\right)$$

    So the field at point x is a copy of the signal at \(x=0\) delayed by \(x/c\). The shape is the same; it just arrives later.

    Everyday example

    Picture a convoy with all its cars moving at the same speed. The convoy advances along the road, but the distances between the cars never change.

    Unit check · asked in class

    A classmate asked “why did the electric field come out unitless?”. The answer: we took \(u(t)\) as unitless, and both the cosine and the exponential are unitless. Since \(A(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int u\,e^{i\omega t}dt\), the unit of A is seconds. Then \(E=\int A\,d\omega\): seconds × 1/seconds = unitless. If you want a real field, you put an \(E_0\) with units of volts/meter in front.

    Check · step 10

    A pulse travels 3 m in vacuum. How many nanoseconds after x = 0 does it reach x = 3 m?

    ns

    CarefulCorrect in seconds: 10⁻⁸ s. Write nanoseconds in the box (1 ns = 10⁻⁹ s).

    Hintt = x / c, c = 3×10⁸ m/s, 1 ns = 10⁻⁹ s.

    Solution

    3 / (3×10⁸) = 10⁻⁸ s = 10 ns. When it arrives its shape is exactly the same, because all frequencies travel at the same speed in vacuum.

    Extra · units

    If \(u(t)\) is unitless, what is the unit of \(A(\omega)\)?

    1. \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int u\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt\): seconds come from dt.

    2. The integral has dt, not dω. dt brings seconds.

    3. u is unitless but it is multiplied by dt.

    4. V/m comes only if you put E₀ into u, and then the unit of A is V·s/m.

    Part B · notebook pages 3–4

    The same pulse inside matter

    Dielectric medium, complex refractive index, absorption and skin depth.

    Step 11

    The formula is the same, only k changes

    In shortInside a material the solution has the same pattern. The only thing that changes is the wave number: \(k=n(\omega)\,\omega/c\).

    The notebook says “the same in dielectric media, solutions are the same”. The solution is again \(\propto e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\), and \(A(\omega)\) is again found from the pulse at \(x=0\). Only k is no longer \(\omega/c\) as in vacuum.

    First a unit check. The lecturer said “which is correct, look at the units”: \(\omega=ck\) or \(k=\omega c\)?

    $$\omega=c\,k:\qquad\left[\frac{1}{\text{s}}\right]=\left[\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}\right]\cdot\left[\frac{1}{\text{m}}\right]$$

    It holds. In a material the refractive index is \(n=\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}\). In nonmagnetic materials we take \(\mu_r=1\) and neglect the magnetic interaction:

    $$n(\omega)=\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}=\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}$$ $$n(\omega)\,\omega=c\,k\;\Longrightarrow\;k=n(\omega)\,\frac{\omega}{c}$$

    We already derived this on the way from Maxwell to the wave equation (“we did it before” in the notebook).

    High school link

    Refraction: light slows down in glass, with speed \(v=c/n\). For glass n ≈ 1.5, so light travels about 200,000 km per second in glass. It is like running on sand: your step rate is the same but your steps get shorter. The frequency stays the same and the wavelength gets shorter.

    Check · step 11

    Decide with a unit check: which is correct?

    1. (m/s)·(1/m) = 1/s, the unit of frequency.

    2. (1/s)·(m/s) = m/s². The unit of k must be 1/m.

    3. Solving ω = ck for k gives k = ω/c, not ωc.

    4. (m/s)/(1/m) = m²/s. Not a frequency.

    Keep in mind

    Do a unit check on every new formula. Most mistakes are caught there.

    Step 12

    Complex n: one part slows, the other damps

    In shortThe refractive index is complex: \(n=n_R+i\,n_I\). The real part slows the wave and the imaginary part damps it.

    $$k=\frac{\omega}{c}\big(n_R(\omega)+i\,n_I(\omega)\big)$$

    Put this into \(e^{ikx}\) and use \(i\cdot i=-1\):

    $$e^{ikx}=e^{\,i\frac{\omega}{c}n_R x}\;\cdot\;e^{-\frac{\omega}{c}n_I x}$$

    The magnitude of the first factor is always 1. It only rotates, that is, oscillates: a wave moving at speed \(c/n_R\). The second factor is a real exponential that gets smaller as x grows. This is what is marked “decay func.” in the notebook. The full solution:

    $$E(x,t)\sim\int d\omega\,A(\omega)\,e^{\,i\frac{\omega}{c}n_R x}\,e^{-\frac{\omega}{c}n_I x}\,e^{-i\omega t}$$

    In week 3 we wrote the same thing as \(n+i\kappa\): \(n_R=n\), \(n_I=\kappa\) (extinction coefficient). In the lecture it was also said as “N plus iK”, the same thing.

    Everyday example

    Walking in a swamp: you both slow down (n_R) and lose a little energy with each step (n_I). The lens of sunglasses is like this too, both slowing the light and swallowing part of it.

    Difference from the notebook

    In the solution line of the notebook the exponential is written like \(i\big(\tfrac{\omega}{c}n_R+i\,n_I\big)x\), and \(\omega/c\) seems to multiply only \(n_R\). The correct form is \(i\tfrac{\omega}{c}\big(n_R+i\,n_I\big)x\). The separated form right next to it, \(e^{i\frac{\omega}{c}n_Rx}\,e^{-n_I\frac{\omega}{c}x}\), is correct.

    Check · step 12

    Of the two factors of \(e^{ikx}\), which one damps the wave?

    1. Its magnitude is always 1, because \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\). It only rotates the phase and does not damp.

    2. A real and negative exponential, getting smaller as x grows.

    3. This is the oscillation in time, with magnitude 1. The decay here is in space, not time.

    4. It decays if n_I ≠ 0. The imaginary part turns into a real exponential through i·i = −1.

    Keep in mind

    The real part of k is the oscillation (wavelength), the imaginary part the decay.

    Step 13

    Two effects: dispersion and absorption

    In short\(n_R\) and \(n_I\) depend on frequency. Each frequency travels at a different speed and decays at a different rate. The packet both changes shape and weakens.

    The point stressed in the lecture: the solution has the same pattern as in vacuum, but \(n_R(\omega)\) and \(n_I(\omega)\) depend on frequency. Two separate effects come from this:

    • Dispersion: each frequency has a different speed \(c/n_R(\omega)\). The waves inside the packet drift apart and the packet stretches.
    • Absorption: each frequency decays at its own rate with \(e^{-\frac{\omega}{c}n_I x}\). The packet weakens.

    Passive and active media. In a normal material \(n_I>0\) and the wave decays. This is called a passive, absorptive medium. In a medium into which you pump energy from outside, such as the gain medium of a laser, \(n_I<0\) is possible. Then the exponential grows and the wave gets stronger as it advances.

    Direction. For a wave going in the −x direction the exponential is \(e^{-ikx}\), and the decay is again in the direction the wave travels. A wave going left decays to the left.

    High school link

    A prism splitting white light into colors is dispersion itself: \(n_R\) depends on the color, the frequency, and violet light bends more than red. The deep sea looks blue because absorption depends on frequency too: water swallows red faster than blue.

    Check · step 13

    A prism splitting white light into colors is an example of which effect?

    1. A prism does not swallow light; it bends the colors at different angles. That is the job of n_R.

    2. Each color sees a different n_R and bends at a different angle. In a pulse the same happens in time: different frequencies arrive at different times.

    3. Total reflection is in Part C, in fibers. The color separation in a prism comes from refraction.

    4. In vacuum all colors travel at the same speed and do not separate. The separation happens in matter.

    Keep in mind

    Dispersion distorts the shape and absorption reduces the amplitude. In a real material both happen together.

    Extra · laser

    In the gain medium of a laser, what is the sign of \(n_I\)?

    1. A positive n_I means absorption, and the wave decays. A laser needs the opposite.

    2. When n_I < 0, \(e^{-\frac{\omega}{c}n_I x}\) is a growing exponential. The wave gets stronger as it advances.

    3. If it is zero the wave neither decays nor grows. A laser exists to amplify.

    4. n_I is already the coefficient of the imaginary part of n and is itself a real number. The question asks for its sign.

    Step 14

    Skin depth: how deep does light go?

    In shortThe depth at which the amplitude falls to 1/e of its initial value, about 37%, is called the skin depth: \(\delta=\lambda_0/(2\pi n_I)\).

    First turn \(\omega/c\) into wavelength. The high school formulas \(f\lambda=c\) and \(\omega=2\pi f\):

    $$\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\,\lambda_0=c\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{\omega}{c}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-\frac{\omega}{c}n_I x}=e^{-n_I\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}x}$$

    The \(\lambda_0\) here is the wavelength in vacuum, not in the material, because \(\omega/c\) is the vacuum relation. Now set the exponent equal to −1:

    $$n_I\,\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\,\delta=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\delta=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\,n_I}$$

    Reading it: if \(n_I\) is large, δ is small and the light dies out right away. If \(n_I\) is small, δ is large and the light goes deep. δ also depends on the wavelength.

    • At depth δ37%
    • 2δ13.5%
    • 3δ5%
    • 5δ0.7%

    These percentages are for the amplitude of the field. Intensity (energy) is the square of the amplitude, so at depth δ the intensity has fallen to \(e^{-2}\approx\%13.5\). Some books define the depth by intensity and find δ/2, so watch the definition.

    High school link

    Exponential decay is like a half-life: at each δ the amplitude falls by the same factor, ×0.37, no matter which δ it is. Diving into the sea is the same: every few meters the light falls by the same proportion, and the depths go dark.

    Check · step 14

    Extra · exponential

    At depth 2δ, the amplitude is about what percent of the initial value?

    1. Halving is the definition of a half-life. The definition of δ is 1/e, that is, 37%.

    2. This is the value at δ. At 2δ it is ×0.37 once more.

    3. e⁻² ≈ 0.135, that is, 0.37 × 0.37.

    4. An exponential never reaches exactly zero; it only gets very small.

    Step 15

    How far does light go in which material?

    In shortIn metals δ is a few tens of nanometers, which is why metal is opaque and shiny. In low-loss dielectrics δ is on the order of centimeters, which is why glass is transparent.

    Check · step 15

    Why does aluminum foil pass no light at all even though it is very thin?

    1. What matters is the ratio of the thickness to δ.

    2. Slowing is the job of n_R and does not destroy light. Decay comes from n_I.

    3. Exactly the opposite: in metals n_I is large, 2–10. If n_I = 0 the light would not decay at all.

    4. A 5 mm pane of glass is transparent. Thickness alone says nothing; the ratio thickness/δ does.

    Keep in mind

    Transparency is decided by thickness ÷ δ. If this ratio is much less than 1 it is transparent, and if much greater, opaque.

    Part C · notebook pages 4–7

    Trapping light in a pipe

    Waveguides, boundary conditions and the components of Maxwell's equations.

    Step 16

    What is a waveguide?

    In shortA structure that traps and directs light or microwaves along a certain path. It does for light what a garden hose does for water.

    The definition in the notebook: photonic structures that guide, confine and process light at sizes comparable to or even smaller than the wavelength (waveguides). Examples: optical fibers in undersea internet cables, photonic circuits on a chip, metal pipes in radars and microwave ovens.

    An optical fiber has two layers: the core in the middle (\(n_{core}\)) and the cladding around it (\(n_{clad}\)). For light to stay inside:

    $$n_{core}>n_{clad}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{total internal reflection}$$

    The critical angle from high school physics: when light goes from a denser to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence is larger than \(\theta_c\), it cannot get out and reflects back completely (total internal reflection).

    $$\sin\theta_c=\frac{n_{clad}}{n_{core}}$$

    The lecturer said in class “there is always some leakage, but if you do not meet this condition you lose the light”.

    High school link

    Looking up from the bottom of a pool at a slanted angle, the water surface looks like a mirror and you cannot see outside. This is total internal reflection. The critical angle for water is about 49°.

    Check · step 16

    What is needed for light to stay in the core of a fiber?

    1. Both together. The index order makes total reflection possible, and the angle makes it happen.

    2. Total reflection happens only going from a denser to a less dense medium. In reverse, sin θ_c > 1, there is no critical angle and the light escapes.

    3. If they are equal it acts as if there were no boundary, and the light passes straight into the cladding.

    4. A metal wall is another way, the metal pipe we will see shortly. In an optical fiber the cladding is glass and what confines the light is total internal reflection.

    Keep in mind

    Fiber: the index of the core is slightly larger, light travels at angles close to the axis and is completely reflected at each bounce.

    Solution

    Step 17

    The simplest guide: a hollow metal pipe

    In shortThe lecturer started from the easiest case: air inside, metal walls. A metal wall has two rules: E parallel to the wall is zero, and B perpendicular to the wall is zero.

    First recall the general boundary conditions (we derived them in week 3). At the boundary of two materials you can split any vector into two parts, “perpendicular to the surface” and “parallel to the surface”. The lecturer stressed “the material does not matter, this holds at every boundary”.

    $$E_{1\parallel}=E_{2\parallel},\qquad B_{1\perp}=B_{2\perp}$$

    The other two components can jump if there is charge or current on the surface: perpendicular E by the surface charge density σ, parallel B by the surface current. For the perpendicular component the lecturer said “if there is charge on the surface, the field changes by this much right at the boundary”.

    Now let one side be an ideal metal. Inside the metal \(\vec E=0\) and \(\vec B=0\). If the continuous components are zero inside, they must also be zero outside, right in front of the wall:

    $$\text{at a metal wall:}\qquad E_\parallel=0,\qquad B_\perp=0$$

    The perpendicular E and parallel B are free. The surface charges and surface currents on the wall take care of them.

    High school link

    The rule you learned in electrostatics: electric field lines always enter the surface of a conductor perpendicularly. \(E_\parallel=0\) is exactly this rule. \(B_\perp=0\) says this: magnetic field lines cannot pierce a metal wall and slide parallel to it.

    An ideal metal is an approximation. In a real metal the field leaks in by δ, but as we saw in Part B, δ is very small.

    Check · step 17

    Which must be zero right in front of a perfectly conducting wall?

    1. The reverse. The surface charge can take care of perpendicular E and the surface current of parallel B. What is zero are the continuous components.

    2. The continuous components carry the zero on the metal side over to the air side.

    3. All are zero inside the metal, but on the air side of the wall there can be perpendicular E and parallel B. The surface charge and current take care of the jump.

    4. Inside the metal E = B = 0, and the continuous components carry this zero outside.

    Keep in mind

    E enters the wall perpendicularly, B slides parallel to the wall.

    Step 18

    A wave traveling along z inside the pipe

    In shortThe wave travels in the z direction. The pattern in the cross section depends on x and y, and the advance comes from the factor \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\). This time the field must also have a z component.

    $$\vec E(x,y,z,t)=\vec E_0(x,y)\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$$ $$\vec B(x,y,z,t)=\vec B_0(x,y)\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$$ $$\vec E_0(x,y)=E_{0x}\,\hat x+E_{0y}\,\hat y+E_{0z}\,\hat z$$

    \(\vec E_0(x,y)\) is the picture you see looking into the mouth of the pipe: where the field in the cross section is strong or weak and which way it points. \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\) is the wave carrying this picture along z.

    In vacuum a plane wave is entirely transverse: \(\vec k\perp\vec E\perp\vec B\), with no z component, and you are free to choose the polarization along x or y. Inside a pipe this freedom ends. The sentence in the notebook: we have to keep the z component, or else the boundary conditions cannot be satisfied.

    Why? A rough intuition: if the field were only transverse, the E in the cross section would behave like a static field inside a closed frame whose walls are all at the same potential. Inside a frame with every edge at the same height and no peaks or dips, the floor stays flat: no slope, no field. For a wave to exist, at least one of \(E_z\) or \(B_z\) must be nonzero. Both are not required; the pattern in Figure 11 has only \(B_z\).

    Everyday example

    The Mexican wave in a stadium. The pattern of how much the spectators in each row rise (the cross section) stays the same, and the wave travels along the stands.

    Check · step 18

    Does a plane wave in vacuum have a component along the direction of travel, such as \(E_z\)?

    1. In vacuum ∇·E = 0, which for a plane wave means k·E = 0: E is perpendicular to the direction of travel.

    2. In vacuum k ⊥ E ⊥ B. Only walls can force a longitudinal component.

    3. It does not depend on frequency. A plane wave in vacuum is transverse at every frequency.

    4. The material being magnetic does not change this. Boundaries, the walls, bring the longitudinal component.

    Keep in mind

    Vacuum: entirely transverse. Metal pipe: at least one of \(E_z\) or \(B_z\) is nonzero. Next week we will separate TE and TM modes by these two.

    Step 19

    Expanding Maxwell into components: 6 equations

    In shortPutting this wave into the Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell equations gives 6 component equations. Two rules are enough: the z derivative is multiplication by ik and the t derivative by −iω.

    The derivative rule is high school math: \(\frac{d}{dz}e^{ikz}=ik\,e^{ikz}\) and \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{-i\omega t}=-i\omega\,e^{-i\omega t}\). The x and y derivatives stay, because amplitudes like \(E_{0x}(x,y)\) depend on x and y and are not constant. The lecturer drew special attention to this.

    $$\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\;\to\;ik,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\;\to\;-i\omega$$

    The components of the curl:

    $$\begin{aligned}\nabla\times\vec E=\;&\Big(\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial z}\Big)\hat x\\ +\;&\Big(\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}\Big)\hat y\\ +\;&\Big(\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y}\Big)\hat z\end{aligned}$$

    Faraday: \(\nabla\times\vec E=-\partial\vec B/\partial t=i\omega\vec B\). The exponential factor is in every term and cancels. Below, for brevity, when I write \(E_x\) I mean \(E_{0x}\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{(i)}&\quad\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y}=i\omega B_z\\ \text{(ii)}&\quad\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y}-ik\,E_y=i\omega B_x\\ \text{(iii)}&\quad ik\,E_x-\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}=i\omega B_y\end{aligned}$$

    Ampère–Maxwell, with vacuum inside the pipe: \(\nabla\times\vec B=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t=\frac{1}{c^2}\partial\vec E/\partial t=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2}\vec E\). The pattern is the same: E and B swap and \(-i\omega/c^2\) takes the place of \(i\omega\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{(iv)}&\quad\frac{\partial B_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial B_x}{\partial y}=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2}E_z\\ \text{(v)}&\quad\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}-ik\,B_y=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2}E_x\\ \text{(vi)}&\quad ik\,B_x-\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2}E_y\end{aligned}$$
    Everyday example

    A mental picture for the curl: put a small paddle wheel in flowing water. If the wheel turns, there is a vortex there. Faraday says that if the magnetic field changes in time, such a vortex forms in the electric field.

    Difference from the notebook

    In the notebook the Ampère line is first written as \(\nabla\times\vec B=\partial\vec E/\partial t\), and \(1/c^2\) is added one line below. The correct form in vacuum is \(\nabla\times\vec B=\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}\).

    Check · step 19

    For \(E_x=E_{0x}(x,y)\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\), what is \(\partial E_x/\partial z\)?

    1. The amplitude does not depend on z, but the exponential factor does. The derivative brings ik from there.

    2. The z derivative acts only on the exponential and brings down ik.

    3. −iω comes from the time derivative. The z derivative brings k.

    4. You mixed it up with the x derivative. The z derivative acts on the exponential, not the amplitude.

    Keep in mind

    The z and t derivatives turn into algebra, and the x and y derivatives stay. This is the trick of the whole waveguide calculation.

    Step 20

    Finding \(E_x\) by elimination

    In shortHigh school solution of a system of equations: multiply two equations by suitable numbers and add, and one unknown disappears. Here what we eliminate is \(B_y\).

    High school link

    \(2x+3y=7\) and \(x-3y=2\). Add side by side: \(3x=9\), and y is gone. We will do the same.

    (iii) and (v) contain \(B_y\). Multiply (iii) by k and (v) by \(-\omega\):

    $$\begin{aligned}k\cdot\text{(iii)}:&\quad ik^2E_x-k\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}=i\omega k\,B_y\\ -\omega\cdot\text{(v)}:&\quad -\omega\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}+i\omega k\,B_y=\frac{i\omega^2}{c^2}E_x\end{aligned}$$

    Add side by side. \(i\omega k\,B_y\) is on both sides and cancels:

    $$ik^2E_x-k\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}-\omega\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}=\frac{i\omega^2}{c^2}E_x$$

    Collect the terms with \(E_x\) on one side and use \(1/(-i)=i\):

    $$E_x=\frac{i}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}\left(k\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}+\omega\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}\right)$$

    By the same method, eliminating another unknown from another pair of equations, the others:

    $$\begin{aligned}E_y&=\frac{i}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}\left(k\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y}-\omega\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}\right)\\ B_x&=\frac{i}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}\left(k\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x}-\frac{\omega}{c^2}\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y}\right)\\ B_y&=\frac{i}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}\left(k\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial y}+\frac{\omega}{c^2}\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}\right)\end{aligned}$$
    Difference from the notebook

    In the notebook \(B_y\) was left blank, and in class it was passed over as “the same method”. The \(B_y\) above comes out by the same method; all four expressions were checked symbolically from the six equations.

    Check · step 20

    When finding \(E_x\), which unknown did we eliminate?

    1. B_z stays in the result (as ∂B_z/∂y). It is not eliminated; it is one of the main variables.

    2. When k·(iii) and −ω·(v) are added, the iωk·B_y terms cancel.

    3. E_z also stays in the result. It and B_z are the two main variables that determine everything.

    4. k is not an unknown but a parameter of the wave. What was eliminated was a field component.

    Keep in mind

    For whichever transverse component you want, choose the two equations containing it and its “partner” in the other field, and eliminate the partner.

    Extra · complex numbers

    What is \(1/(-i)\)?

    1. 1/i = −i, but here the denominator has −i: 1/(−i) = −(1/i) = i.

    2. Multiply numerator and denominator by i: i / (−i·i) = i / 1 = i.

    3. The i's do not cancel; an i remains in the denominator.

    4. If it were −1, (−i)·(−1) would have to be 1, but it equals i.

    Step 21

    The big result: everything comes from \(E_z\) and \(B_z\)

    In shortAll four transverse components are computed from the x, y derivatives of \(E_z\) and \(B_z\). So there are really only two unknowns.

    The sentence with which the lecture closed: “If we find \(E_z\) and \(B_z\), we automatically find \(E_x\) and \(E_y\), so we really have two unknowns.” The same holds for \(B_x\) and \(B_y\). Most likely the topic of the next lecture:

    • Solutions with \(E_z=0\): TE modes (transverse electric).
    • Solutions with \(B_z=0\): TM modes (transverse magnetic).
    • If both are zero (TEM): by the formulas all the transverse components are zero too, and no wave remains. The only escape is the case \(k=\omega/c\) with a zero denominator, and that cannot happen in a hollow, single-conductor pipe. TEM is possible in two-conductor structures such as a coaxial cable.
    • The \(\omega^2/c^2-k^2\) in the denominator will later give the cutoff frequency: waves below a certain frequency cannot pass through the pipe.
    Everyday example

    Think of an Excel sheet with two input cells, \(E_z\) and \(B_z\). The other four cells are formulas, computed the moment you type the inputs. The everyday version of the cutoff frequency is the perforated metal screen in a microwave oven door: the holes are very small next to the microwave wavelength (about 12 cm), so microwaves cannot pass, but visible light does and you can see inside.

    Check · step 21

    If \(E_z=0\) and \(B_z=0\) (and \(k\ne\omega/c\)), what would \(E_x, E_y, B_x, B_y\) be according to the formulas?

    1. All are proportional to the derivatives of E_z and B_z; if the derivatives are zero they are zero too.

    2. The denominator is not zero (k ≠ ω/c) and the numerator is zero. The result is zero.

    3. They all depend on E_z and B_z; when those vanish they cannot stay unchanged.

    4. E_x is also proportional to ∂E_z/∂x and ∂B_z/∂y, so it is zero too.

    Keep in mind

    A hollow metal pipe has no entirely transverse (TEM) wave. If you want to pass light through a pipe, one of \(E_z\) or \(B_z\) is required.

    Why is it enough to know \(E_z\) and \(B_z\) in a metal pipe? Explain starting from the boundary conditions.
