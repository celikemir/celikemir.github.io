How does an electron respond to light?

Why a spring and a mass?

The Lorentz model is for insulators and semiconductors. The electron is bound to the atom. When it shifts a little the nucleus pulls it back, and for small displacements this force is linear, \(-m\omega_0^2x\). On top comes the damping \(-m\gamma\dot x\), representing collisions and radiation loss. Finally the force of the light \(qE\):

$$m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x+m\omega_0^2x=qE_0\,e^{-i\omega t}$$

This is the mass, spring and damper system you know from control theory, a driven second order system. When the frequency of the light approaches \(\omega_0\) there is a resonance, and the electron absorbs the most energy there. The “very high absorption” in the notes comes from here.

Where is Drude different?

In a metal a conduction electron is not bound to any atom and roams like a gas. There is no spring, so \(\omega_0=0\). Drude is not a separate model but the \(\omega_0=0\) case of Lorentz:

$$m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x=qE_0\,e^{-i\omega t}$$

Here \(\gamma\) is how often the electron hits the lattice or impurities, and \(1/\gamma\) is the average time between two collisions.

Why do we try the solution as \(x(t)=\tilde x\,e^{-i\omega t}\)?

If you drive a linear system at a single frequency, after the transient dies out the output oscillates at the same frequency. Only its amplitude and phase change. A complex exponential turns the derivative into a multiplication:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\;\to\;-i\omega,\qquad \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\;\to\;(-i\omega)^2=-\omega^2$$

The differential equation turns into an algebraic equation. \(\tilde x\) is a complex number. Its modulus is the amplitude and its angle the phase lag relative to the light. The physical displacement is the real part. For Drude, taking \(q=-e\):

$$\tilde x=\frac{eE_0/m}{\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}$$

CorrectionOne line of the notes says \((-i\omega t)^2\). There should be no \(t\) there; it is \((-i\omega)^2\).

The force is opposite to the field but \(\tilde x\) came out positive. Does the electron go in the same direction as the field?

Yes, this is the key point of the lecture. Take \(\gamma=0\): \(\tilde x=eE_0/m\omega^2>0\). The force is opposite to the field, and the displacement is in phase with the field. The reason: a mass driven above resonance moves 180° out of phase with the force. If you push a mass back and forth quickly with your hand, at the moment you push hardest to the right the mass is at its leftmost. In sinusoidal motion the acceleration is \(=-\omega^2\times\)times the position. The acceleration is in the direction of the force, and the position opposite to both.

The result: the electron goes in the direction of the field, but its charge is negative. The resulting dipole \(p=-ex\) points opposite to the field. The material produces a polarization that partly cancels the incoming field. This is exactly why ε will soon come out negative.

From polarization to ε(ω)

What is polarization, and why do we multiply by \(n\)?

The dipole moment of one electron is \(p=-e\tilde x\). P is the total dipole moment per unit volume. There are \(n\) electrons per unit volume:

$$P=np=-ne\tilde x=-\frac{ne^2E_0/m}{\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}$$

CorrectionThis minus was dropped both on the board and in the notes. The minus was added to the χ line later and the P line stayed without it. It is not an unimportant sign; this is exactly the minus that pulls ε below 1.

From the definition \(P=\varepsilon_0\chi_eE\):

$$\chi_e=\frac{P}{\varepsilon_0E_0}=-\frac{ne^2}{m\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}$$

Where did ωp come from, is it a made-up shorthand?

The unit analysis done in red pen connects here. χ is dimensionless and \(1/(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)\) has units of 1/frequency². So \(ne^2/m\varepsilon_0\) must have units of frequency². We call it \(\omega_p^2\):

$$\omega_p^2=\frac{ne^2}{m\varepsilon_0},\qquad \varepsilon(\omega)=1+\chi_e=1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}$$

Where \(1+\chi\) comes from: \(D=\varepsilon_0E+P=\varepsilon_0(1+\chi)E\). The contribution of vacuum is 1 and that of the material χ.

But it is not just a shorthand. If you shift the electron gas a little, positive ions are exposed behind it and the resulting field pulls the gas back. Individual electrons have no spring but the collective does, and its natural oscillation frequency is \(\omega_p\). Putting in \(n\approx8.5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\) for copper gives the free electron estimate \(\omega_p\approx1.6\times10^{16}\) rad/s, that is, \(\hbar\omega_p\approx11\) eV, in the ultraviolet.

What do scaled units gain us?

Writing \(\bar\omega=\omega/\omega_p\) and \(\bar\gamma=\gamma/\omega_p\):

$$\varepsilon(\bar\omega)=1-\frac{1}{\bar\omega^2+i\bar\gamma\bar\omega}$$

Everything specific to the material is buried in \(\omega_p\), leaving a single parameter \(\bar\gamma\). All Drude metals trace the same curve. As mentioned in the lecture, these are also called normalized units.

3

ω < ωp: why is a metal a mirror?

Why was the damping thrown away? Isn't that a trick?

Putting in \(\gamma=0\) gives \(\varepsilon=1-\omega_p^2/\omega^2\), purely real. The justification is right: the denominator has \(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega\), and if \(\omega\gg\gamma\) the second term is unimportant. In copper \(\gamma\approx4\times10^{13}\) s⁻¹, and for visible light \(\omega\approx3.5\times10^{15}\) rad/s, a difference of nearly a hundred. At optical frequencies the lossless approximation is reasonable; at microwave frequencies it is not. This will come back in the skin depth.

k came out imaginary. What does that mean?

When \(\omega<\omega_p\), ε is negative:

$$k=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\varepsilon}=\frac1c\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_p^2}=i\alpha,\qquad \alpha=\frac1c\sqrt{\omega_p^2-\omega^2}$$ $$E=E_0\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}=E_0\,e^{-\alpha x}\,e^{-i\omega t}$$

The part depending on x has no oscillation, only exponential decay. The field shakes in time but does not advance in space; it dies away from the surface inward. This is what the lecturer called an “evanescent wave”. The other sign of the root, \(-i\alpha\), gives \(e^{+\alpha x}\) inside, and the field grows to infinity with depth. In a semi-infinite material that is not physical and is discarded.

If it decays inside, where does the energy go? Isn't it absorbed?

This is exactly the question discussed in Turkish at the back of the class. The answer is no. Decaying in space and losing energy are different things. Loss comes only from Im ε, that is, from γ, and here γ = 0. In the evanescent region \(H=kE/\omega\mu_0\) and k is imaginary, so there is a 90° phase difference between H and E. The time average of the Poynting vector is zero. The field pushes a little energy in during each half period and takes it back in the next. The only place for energy that can neither get in nor vanish to go is back:

$$r=\frac{1-n}{1+n},\qquad n=i\kappa\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;|r|=\frac{|1-i\kappa|}{|1+i\kappa|}=1$$

R is exactly 1. This is why metals are mirrors. In a real metal γ is not zero and a few percent goes to heat, so a real mirror does not reflect 100%.

What does the skin depth measure?

The depth at which the amplitude falls to 1/e of its initial value. Putting in \(x=1/\alpha\) gives \(E_0/e\):

$$\delta=\frac1\alpha=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\omega_p^2-\omega^2}}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;\frac{c}{\omega_p}\quad(\omega\ll\omega_p)$$

The lecturer's remark “the power of the exponential must be dimensionless” belongs here: αx is dimensionless, so α is 1/length and δ a length. In copper \(c/\omega_p\approx3\times10^8/1.6\times10^{16}\approx19\) nm.

But wasn't the skin depth of copper on the order of millimeters at 50 Hz?

Both are right, in different regimes. The electricians' formula belongs to the regime \(\omega\ll\gamma\). There the damping term in the denominator dominates and ε is nearly purely imaginary, the exact opposite of the lossless case. Putting in the conductivity from Drude, this formula comes from the same ε:

$$\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu_0\sigma\omega}},\qquad \sigma=\frac{\varepsilon_0\omega_p^2}{\gamma}$$
RegimeConditionδ in copper
Loss dominatedω ≪ γ≈ 9 mm at 50 Hz, ≈ 2 µm at 1 GHz
Lossless, opticalγ ≪ ω < ωp≈ c/ωp ≈ 19 nm, independent of frequency

This is where the lecturer said “especially for lossy materials”. The definition does not change; δ is always \(1/\mathrm{Im}\,k\). In the lossy case k has both a real and an imaginary part.

4

ω > ωp: the metal turns transparent

\(0<\varepsilon<1\), k is real and the wave propagates. This is the regime the lecturer called “transparent”. Alkali metals really are like this in the ultraviolet. Writing the dispersion relation in this form makes your job much easier:

$$k^2c^2=\omega^2-\omega_p^2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2$$ $$v_p=\frac{\omega}{k}=\frac{c}{n}>c\qquad(n<1)$$

The phase velocity is greater than c. Doesn't that break relativity?

No. The phase velocity is the speed of the crests of an infinitely long, single-frequency wave. It carries neither energy nor information. Information needs a pulse, and a pulse is a packet of frequencies. The envelope of the packet travels at the group velocity, \(v_g=d\omega/dk\). In the lossless case energy is also carried at the group velocity.

Homework · group velocity

The lecturer left this as homework, and its answer is not here. Taking the derivative of the form \(\omega^2=\omega_p^2+c^2k^2\) is one line. Then look at three things: is \(v_g\) less than c? What does the product \(v_p\,v_g\) come out as? What happens to \(v_g\) in the limit \(\omega\to\omega_p\), and what does that mean physically?

Why did Cherenkov come up?

If a charged particle moves faster than the phase velocity of light in a medium it emits Cherenkov radiation, like the light version of a sound barrier. In the \(\omega>\omega_p\) region the phase velocity is already above c, and no particle can exceed it. There is no Cherenkov at those frequencies. This radiation can occur only at frequencies with \(n>1\).

5

From a single frequency to a pulse: Fourier

Why did the lecture suddenly move to Fourier?

Until now everything was for a single frequency. A real laser pulse is a packet of frequencies, and a short pulse means a wide spectrum. What saves us is linearity: the equation is linear in x, so split the drive into frequency components, solve each separately and add. It is the same logic as the transfer function in signal processing. χ(ω) is the transfer function of the material.

$$E_p(t)=\sum_\omega E_\omega\,e^{-i\omega t}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}E_p(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega$$

What are the units of the coefficients?

In a discrete series the exponential is dimensionless, so \(E_\omega\) is V/m, the same as the field itself. In a continuous integral the unit of dω is 1/s, so \(E_p(\omega)\) must be V·s/m. In the recording it sounds like “electric field multiplied by frequency”. Either it was misheard or it was a slip of the tongue; the correct form is divided by frequency.

The meaning: in the discrete case each frequency has its own amplitude. In the continuous case the amplitude of a single frequency is zero, and what is meaningful is the amplitude per unit frequency interval, the spectral density. It is the same as the difference between probability and probability density. The answer to the question asked in class, “why did we write it discretely”, is the lecturer's too: both carry the same information, and the discrete form makes the logic of equating coefficients easier to see.

What comes out when we put it into the equation?

The time derivatives go inside the sum and each turns into multiplication by \(-i\omega\):

$$\int d\omega\,\Big[m\,x(\omega)\big(\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega\big)-qE_p(\omega)\Big]e^{-i\omega t}=0$$

CorrectionOn the board the parenthesis contains \(-i\omega^2\); it should be \((-i\omega)^2=-\omega^2\). The charge is also missing on the right side of the equation of motion; it should be \(qE_p(t)\).

Since the two integrals are equal, can I equate the terms frequency by frequency?

Multiply both sides by \(e^{i\omega' t}\) and integrate over t:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\,e^{i\omega' t}\,dt=2\pi\,\delta(\omega-\omega')$$

As the lecturer explained, when ω ≠ ω′ the integrand contains sines and cosines, which average to zero over an infinite range. When ω = ω′ the inside becomes 1 and the integral goes to infinity. Infinite at one point and zero everywhere else: the Dirac delta. The delta is like tweezers, pulling a single frequency out of the sum:

$$x(\omega)=\frac{q}{m}\,\frac{E_p(\omega)}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}$$

The same as the single-frequency Lorentz solution, with \(E_p(\omega)\) in place of \(E_0\). The single-frequency solution holds as it is for each component.

NoteThe board says “multiply by \(e^{-i\omega' t}\)”. To pull out a component in the expansion with \(e^{-i\omega t}\) you actually need \(e^{+i\omega' t}\). Since the delta is symmetric the result does not change.

The meaning on the time side is nice too. A product in frequency is a convolution in time: \(P(t)=\varepsilon_0\int\chi(t-t')\,E(t')\,dt'\). The material has a memory; the present polarization depends on the past field.

Why did the lecturer say “forget about zero frequency”?

The context is not clear in the recording, but in Drude there is a real reason for it. When \(\omega_0=0\), \(x(\omega)\propto1/(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)\), and as ω → 0 the denominator goes to zero. If you apply a constant field to a free electron there is no spring to pull it back; it drifts on and on and the displacement grows without bound. What stays finite is the velocity, the current. That is why at DC we describe a metal by conductivity rather than displacement. In Lorentz \(\omega_0\neq0\), so this problem does not arise.

How does a pulse travel in space?

Why does the general solution in free space have two terms?

$$\vec E(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\Big[A(\omega)\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}+B(\omega)\,e^{-i(kx+\omega t)}\Big],\qquad k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}$$

To keep the phase constant in \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\) we need \(x=(\omega/k)\,t\). As time advances x grows and the wave goes in the +x direction. The other term goes in the −x direction by the same logic. A(ω) and B(ω) are weights. As the lecturer said, the structure is the same in a medium; only the dispersion relation k(ω) takes the place of k.

How do we write a Gaussian pulse?

The lecturer asked “how is a Gaussian written”, and the answer is \(e^{-ax^2}\). But the drawing on the board is an oscillating signal with a Gaussian envelope, that is, envelope times carrier:

$$u(t)=E_0\,e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}\,e^{-i\omega_c t}$$ $$u(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int u(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt=\frac{E_0\,\tau}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\;e^{-(\omega-\omega_c)^2\tau^2/2}$$

τ is the pulse duration and \(\omega_c\) the carrier frequency, the color of the laser. Its Fourier transform is again a Gaussian, around \(\omega_c\) on the frequency axis with width \(1/\tau\). In the literature the field is written as u, as the lecturer said, to work with a scalar quantity.

What is the relation of this to A(ω)?

If the pulse is sent only in the +x direction, B = 0. At x = 0, \(E(0,t)=\int A(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega=u(t)\), so A(ω) = u(ω), the Fourier transform of the input pulse. The recipe: split the pulse at x = 0 into frequencies, let each component pick up a phase \(e^{ik(\omega)x}\) over the distance x, and add them all back.

In free space \(k=\omega/c\), the phase is only a delay of \(x/c\), and the pulse slides along keeping its shape. In a material \(k(\omega)=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\varepsilon(\omega)}\), and for Drude \(\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_p^2}/c\). k is no longer linear in ω, the components travel at different speeds and the pulse broadens. The ε computed in the first half comes into play here; the two halves of the lecture meet here.

The lecture ended right here. Next is most likely the Taylor expansion of k(ω) around \(\omega_c\): the first derivative is \(1/v_g\), the speed of the envelope, and the second derivative is the group velocity dispersion that broadens the pulse.

Test yourself

Say the answer yourself first, then open it.

In Drude the electron goes in the direction of the field, but the polarization is opposite to the field. How is this?

A mass driven above resonance is 180° out of phase with the force; since the force is −eE, the displacement is along E. The dipole is p = −ex, and since the charge is negative the dipole is opposite to E. The polarization partly cancels the incoming field and ε falls below 1.

When ω < ωp the field decays inside the metal, but R = 1. Why is the energy not absorbed?

Absorption comes from Im ε, that is, from γ. In the lossless case E and H are 90° out of phase in the evanescent field and the average Poynting flux is zero. Energy goes in and comes back out, with no net flow, and everything is reflected.

The skin depth of copper is ≈ 9 mm at 50 Hz and ≈ 19 nm in the visible. Why are there two different formulas?

Different regimes. At ω ≪ γ damping dominates, ε is nearly purely imaginary and δ = √(2/μ₀σω) shrinks with frequency. At γ ≪ ω < ωp the lossless approximation holds and δ ≈ c/ωp, independent of frequency. Both are δ = 1/Im k.

If the phase velocity comes out greater than c, does it contradict relativity?

No. The phase velocity is the crest speed of an infinite single-frequency wave and carries no information or energy. A signal is a packet, and its envelope travels at the group velocity. The details are in the homework.

Why is the unit of the continuous Fourier coefficient Ep(ω) not the same as the field?

The integral multiplies by dω, and the unit of dω is 1/s. Ep(ω) is V·s/m, an amplitude per unit frequency, a spectral density.

Two Gaussian pulses pass through the same glass block, one with half the duration of the other. Which is distorted more by dispersion?

The short one. The spectral width is 1/τ, and the short pulse covers a frequency range twice as wide. The more k(ω) departs from linear over this range, the more the components differ in speed. On top of that, since the short pulse is already short, the same absolute broadening is larger relative to it.
