A

Mathematical tools

Vectors, fields, derivative operators, integrals and complex waves

Vectors

Vectors and the two products

In three dimensions a vector is written with unit vectors: \(\vec A = A_x\hat x + A_y\hat y + A_z\hat z\), with magnitude \(|\vec A| = \sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2+A_z^2}\). Two products will come up constantly in physics.

Dot product

\[ \vec A\cdot\vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta \]

The result is a number. It measures the share of one vector along the other: zero for perpendicular vectors, largest for parallel ones. In physics work \(W = \vec F\cdot\vec d\) and flux \(\vec E\cdot d\vec a\) are calculated this way.

Cross product

\[ \vec A\times\vec B = \begin{vmatrix}\hat x&\hat y&\hat z\\ A_x&A_y&A_z\\ B_x&B_y&B_z\end{vmatrix}, \qquad |\vec A\times\vec B| = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta \]

The result is a vector, perpendicular to both. Its direction comes from the right-hand rule: as the fingers of your right hand curl from \(\vec A\) to \(\vec B\), the thumb points along the result. Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two vectors. It is zero for parallel vectors, and the order matters: \(\vec B\times\vec A = -\vec A\times\vec B\). In physics the magnetic force \(q\,\vec v\times\vec B\) and the curl come from this product.

Fields · Gradient

Fields and the gradient

A field is a function that assigns a value to every point of space. A scalar field assigns a number to each point: the temperature in a room \(T(x,y,z)\) or the electric potential \(V\). A vector field assigns a vector to each point: wind velocity, the electric field \(\vec E\), the magnetic field \(\vec B\). Electromagnetism is the language of vector fields.

The partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x\), is the change of \(f\) only in the \(x\) direction, with the other coordinates held fixed. Collecting the three partial derivatives into a vector gives the gradient:

\[ \nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\hat x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\hat y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\hat z \]

The same object as the gradient you know from optimization. Its direction is where \(f\) increases fastest, its magnitude the rate of increase in that direction, and it is perpendicular everywhere to the level surfaces (contour lines). The change along any direction \(\hat u\) is found with \(\nabla f\cdot\hat u\).

The \(\nabla\) (nabla) here is a vector operator: \(\nabla = \hat x\,\partial_x + \hat y\,\partial_y + \hat z\,\partial_z\). There are three ways to use it like a vector, and all of Maxwell's equations are written with these three:

OperationApplied toResultWhat it measures
Gradient \(\nabla f\)scalarvectordirection and rate of fastest increase
Divergence \(\nabla\cdot\vec F\)vectorscalarnet outflow from a point
Curl \(\nabla\times\vec F\)vectorvectorcirculation around a point

Divergence · Curl

Divergence and curl

Think of a vector field as a flow of water. You can ask two questions: does water gush out of a point, and does water rotate around a point? Divergence measures the first, curl the second.

Divergence: sources and sinks

Put a small closed surface around the point and divide the net outward flux by the volume. The number left as the volume goes to zero is the divergence:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec F = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta V}\oint_S \vec F\cdot d\vec a = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z} \]

If positive the point is a source, if negative a sink. Example: for \(\vec F = (x, y, z)\), \(\nabla\cdot\vec F = 3\); every point is a source flowing outward.

Curl: circulation

Put a small loop around the point, add up the field along the loop (the circulation) and divide by the loop's area. The result is the component of the curl perpendicular to the plane of the loop. In components:

\[ \nabla\times\vec F = \begin{vmatrix}\hat x&\hat y&\hat z\\ \partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\ F_x&F_y&F_z\end{vmatrix} \qquad \begin{aligned} (\nabla\times\vec F)_x &= \partial_y F_z - \partial_z F_y \\ (\nabla\times\vec F)_y &= \partial_z F_x - \partial_x F_z \\ (\nabla\times\vec F)_z &= \partial_x F_y - \partial_y F_x \end{aligned} \]

Imagine putting a small paddle wheel in the flow. If the curl is nonzero the wheel turns, with an angular velocity equal to half the curl. Example: for \(\vec F = (-y, x, 0)\), \(\nabla\times\vec F = 2\hat z\).

The explorer hides three lessons. The Shear field has perfectly straight lines, yet the wheel turns, because the upper layer flows faster than the lower. The Current wire field has circular lines, yet the wheel does not turn at any point outside the wire; still, a loop enclosing the wire has circulation 2π. All the circulation comes from the singular point at the wire, and this is exactly what Ampère's law will say. In the Charged wire field the divergence is zero everywhere outside the charge, yet a loop enclosing the charge has net flux: Gauss's law.

Line and surface integrals

Flux, circulation and the two big theorems

A line integral adds up the component of the field tangent to a curve along that curve: \(\int_C \vec F\cdot d\vec l\). In a force field this is the work done. If the curve is closed, \(\oint_C\) is written and the result is called the circulation.

A surface integral adds up the field passing through a surface: \(\int_S \vec F\cdot d\vec a\). Here \(d\vec a = \hat n\,da\), is a vector perpendicular to the surface whose magnitude is the area of the small surface element. The result is the flux: a measure of how many field lines pierce the surface. On a closed surface \(\hat n\) points outward. On an open surface the direction is tied to the sense of the boundary curve by the right-hand rule.

The \(\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a)\) you know from single-variable calculus is the one-dimensional version of this idea: the sum of the derivatives inside equals the values at the boundary. We go back and forth between the differential and integral forms of Maxwell's equations with these two theorems; the “integral forms” column in Part 4 comes from here.

Laplacian · Identities

The Laplacian and three identities

The divergence of the gradient is the Laplacian:

\[ \nabla^2 f = \nabla\cdot(\nabla f) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} \]

It measures how far the value at a point departs from the average of the values around it. At a peak \(\nabla^2 f \lt 0\), in a valley \(\nabla^2 f \gt 0\). A function with \(\nabla^2 f = 0\) cannot have a peak or a valley inside; every point is the average of its neighbors. In the “gradient of a hill” field of the explorer the divergence is negative on top of the hill and positive on the slopes: that divergence is \(\nabla^2 f\) itself.

Three identities used constantly in the series:

IdentityMeaningWhere it is needed
\(\nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0\)gradient fields do not rotatein electrostatics \(\nabla\times\vec E = 0\), so \(\vec E = -\nabla V\)
\(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec F) = 0\)curl fields have no sourcescharge conservation and Maxwell's correction
\(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec F) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec F) - \nabla^2\vec F\)BAC-CAB rule, vector triple productderiving the wave equation, Part 4

One last result, the Helmholtz theorem: a vector field that vanishes at infinity is fully determined once its divergence and curl are known. This is why Maxwell's equations consist of exactly two divergence and two curl equations; they are everything needed to determine \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\).

Complex exponentials · Waves

The complex exponential and the wave equation

Euler's formula

\[ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \]

\(e^{i\theta}\) is a point on the unit circle in the complex plane, at angle \(\theta\). As \(\theta\) changes the point moves around the circle; its real part is \(\cos\theta\), its imaginary part \(\sin\theta\). Multiplying a number by \(e^{i\theta}\) means rotating it by \(\theta\).

Why do we write waves as complex?

Because taking a derivative turns into multiplication:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)} = ik\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)} = -i\omega\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)} \]

Differential equations come down to algebra: \(\partial/\partial x \to ik\), \(\partial/\partial t \to -i\omega\). The physical field is a real number; we do the calculation in complex form and take the real part at the end: \(E(x,t) = \mathrm{Re}\{E_0\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\} = E_0\cos(kx-\omega t)\). This is safe as long as the equations are linear. The plane wave calculation in Part 4 uses exactly this step.

The wave equation

A stretched string, sound or light: all satisfy the same equation.

\[ \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} \]

Its solution is surprisingly simple: for any shape \(f\), \(u(x,t) = f(x - vt)\) is a solution. Substitute it: the left side is \(f''\), the right side \(v^2 f''/v^2 = f''\). So the shape slides to the right at speed \(v\) without distortion; \(g(x+vt)\) slides to the left in the same way.

Substituting the harmonic wave \(u = A\cos(kx-\omega t)\) gives \(-k^2 = -\omega^2/v^2\), that is, \(\omega = vk\). This is the simplest form of the dispersion relation of Part 1.

Superposition and Fourier

Since the equation is linear, a sum of solutions is also a solution. Fourier's result: every reasonable shape can be written as a sum of harmonic waves at different values of \(k\):

\[ u(x,t) = \int A(k)\,e^{i(kx-\omega(k)t)}\,dk \]

The wave packet and group velocity of Part 1 are built on this formula: if \(\omega(k)\) is not linear, the components travel at different speeds and the packet changes shape.

Electromagnetism

From charge to the four Maxwell equations and light

Charge · Coulomb’s law · Electric field

Charge, Coulomb's law and the electric field

Electric charge comes in two kinds, plus and minus; like charges repel and opposites attract. Charge is quantized: every charge is a whole multiple of the elementary charge, \(e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\) C. And it is conserved: the total charge does not change in any process.

The force between two point charges is given by Coulomb's law:

\[ \vec F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r \]

\(\hat r\) is the unit vector pointing from one charge to the other, \(1/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 8.99\times10^9\) N·m²/C². \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) C²/(N·m²) is the electric permittivity of vacuum; the \(\varepsilon_r\) of Part 3 is compared with it. With more than one charge the forces add as vectors: superposition.

The electric field

Splitting the force into two steps is a much more powerful idea: a charge creates a field in the space around it, and another charge feels that field. The electric field is the force per unit charge on a small test charge:

\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q}, \qquad \text{for a point charge}\quad \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{r^2}\,\hat r \]

Its unit is N/C, equivalently V/m. Field lines start on positive charges, end on negative charges and do not cross; the density of the lines shows the strength of the field.

Scale

At the distance of the electron in hydrogen (0.053 nm) the field of the proton is about 5×10¹¹ V/m. The field of sunlight is about 900 V/m. That is a factor of 6×10⁸; ordinary light can only nudge an atom gently. This is why the response of matter to light is almost always linear. As the field approaches the atomic value, that is, in strong lasers, the nonlinear terms of Part 3 come into play.

Flux · Gauss’s law

Electric flux and Gauss's law

Electric flux is a measure of the field lines passing through a surface: \(\Phi_E = \int_S \vec E\cdot d\vec a\). Imagine a sphere of radius \(R\) centered on a point charge. The field is perpendicular to the sphere everywhere, with magnitude \(Q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2\); the area of the sphere is \(4\pi R^2\). Multiplying:

\[ \oint_S \vec E\cdot d\vec a = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}\cdot 4\pi R^2 = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \]

\(R\) canceled: the field falls as \(1/r^2\) while the surface grows as \(r^2\). The result depends neither on the size of the sphere nor on its shape. The field lines of charges outside a closed surface enter it at one place and leave at another; their net contribution is zero. Gauss's law:

\[ \oint_S \vec E\cdot d\vec a = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Apply the divergence theorem to the left side and write the charge as an integral of the charge density: \(\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec E\,dV = \int_V \rho/\varepsilon_0\,dV\). Since this is true for every volume, the integrands must be equal:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \]

This is Maxwell's first equation: charges are the sources of the electric field. In Part 3 you will see it become \(\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f\) in matter.

Two useful results

In symmetric cases Gauss's law gives the field in one line. An infinite plane with surface charge density \(\sigma\) has a field on both sides of \(E = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0\). The fields of two parallel plates with opposite charge add between them and cancel outside: inside \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\), and zero outside. The capacitor figure of Part 3 puts a dielectric inside this arrangement.

Potential · Conductors · Dipoles

Potential, conductors and dipoles

In the field of static charges, if you carry a charge around a closed path the total work done is zero: \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0\), and with Stokes \(\nabla\times\vec E = 0\). A field with zero curl can be written as the gradient of a scalar (the first identity):

\[ \vec E = -\nabla V \]

\(V\) is the electric potential: potential energy per unit charge, in volts (J/C). The minus sign says that the field points in the direction of decreasing potential. Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines.

A bridge to Part 2

An electron passing through a potential difference of 1 V gains \(e\times 1\ \mathrm V = 1.602\times10^{-19}\) J of energy. This unit of energy is called the electronvolt. The “10 eV electron” of Part 2 means an electron accelerated through 10 V.

Putting \(\vec E = -\nabla V\) into Gauss's law gives the Poisson equation; in a charge-free region it reduces to the Laplace equation. Both are in the table in Part 4:

\[ \nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \rho = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla^2 V = 0 \]

Conductors

In a metal the free electrons move as long as there is a field; they stop once the field inside is zero. In the static state, inside a conductor \(\vec E = 0\), the whole conductor is at one potential, excess charge is on the surface and the field outside is perpendicular to the surface. This is the “E = 0 in a metal” of Part 3.

The dipole

\(+q\) and \(-q\) a distance \(\vec d\) apart form a dipole. The dipole moment \(\vec p = q\vec d\), points from the negative charge to the positive. Since the net charge is zero its far field dies out faster than that of a point charge, as \(1/r^3\). In a uniform field a torque acts on the dipole, \(\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E\), trying to turn it parallel to the field. A neutral atom in a field itself becomes a dipole: the electron cloud is pulled one way and the nucleus the other. This is the starting point of the polarization of Part 3.

Current · Magnetic field · Lorentz force

Current and the magnetic field

Current is the charge passing per unit time: \(I = dQ/dt\), in amperes (C/s). The flow of charge in space is described by the current density \(\vec J\) (A/m²); for carriers of density \(n\), charge \(q\) and velocity \(\vec v\), \(\vec J = nq\vec v\). The current through a surface is \(I = \int_S \vec J\cdot d\vec a\).

The continuity equation

Since charge is conserved, current flowing out of a closed surface must reduce the charge inside: \(\oint_S \vec J\cdot d\vec a = -dQ_{\text{enc}}/dt\). With the divergence theorem the local form is:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec J + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = 0 \]

The Lorentz force

Moving charges feel a magnetic force in addition to the electric field. The total force on a charge:

\[ \vec F = q\big(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\big) \]

The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work: it changes the direction of the particle's velocity but not its magnitude, turning it on a circle or helix. The unit of \(\vec B\) is the tesla, T = N/(A·m). The Earth's field is about 50 µT, and that of an MRI machine 1.5–3 T.

The source of the magnetic field: current

Moving charges, that is, currents, produce the magnetic field. The field of a long straight wire is circles around the wire, decreasing with distance:

\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}, \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\,m/A} \]

In general the field is calculated with the Biot–Savart law, \(d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}\): the magnetic counterpart of Coulomb's law. \(\mu_0\) is the magnetic permeability of vacuum; the \(\mu_r\) of Part 3 is compared with it.

There is no magnetic charge

Electric field lines start and end on charges. Magnetic field lines start nowhere; they always form closed loops. If you cut a magnet in two you get two magnets, and you cannot get a north pole alone. The mathematical statement is Maxwell's second equation:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0 \]

Ampère’s law

Ampère's law

Add up the field of the wire along a circle: \(B\) is constant on the circle and tangent to it, and the length of the circle is \(2\pi r\). The result is \(B\cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I\), independent of the radius. The general form for steady currents:

\[ \oint_C \vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} \]

With Stokes' theorem the differential form is \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J\). Recall the “current wire” field of the explorer: the curl was zero outside the wire, but the circulation around a loop enclosing the wire was not. All the circulation comes from where the current flows.

Reading Gauss and Ampère side by side reveals a symmetry: charges are the divergence source of \(\vec E\), and currents are the curl source of \(\vec B\).

Faraday’s law · Lenz’s rule

Faraday induction and Lenz's rule

The magnetic flux through a surface is \(\Phi_B = \int_S \vec B\cdot d\vec a\), in webers. In 1831 Faraday found that if the magnetic flux through a loop changes, an electromotive force (EMF), that is, a voltage around the loop, appears:

\[ \mathcal E = \oint_C \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \]

The flux can change in three ways: the field itself changes, the loop's area changes or the loop rotates. Generators work with the third, transformers and wireless charging with the first. The signal in an MRI machine is picked up this way too: the rotating magnetization induces a voltage in the receiver coil.

The minus sign is Lenz's rule: the magnetic field of the induced current opposes the change in flux. If it were otherwise, a small change would amplify itself and energy would appear from nothing.

With the loop fixed, apply Stokes' theorem: \(\int_S (\nabla\times\vec E)\cdot d\vec a = -\int_S \partial\vec B/\partial t\cdot d\vec a\), for every surface. The differential form is Maxwell's third equation:

\[ \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} \]

Its meaning is profound: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field circulating in space, even with no wire or charge there. Such a field can no longer be written as \(-\nabla V\); the electrostatic rule \(\nabla\times\vec E = 0\) was valid only in the static case.

Displacement current · Maxwell’s equations

Maxwell's correction and the four equations

There is an inconsistency in Ampère's law. Put a loop around the wire of a charging capacitor. If you choose a flat disk pierced by the wire as the surface spanned by the loop, the current \(I\) passes through it. You can also span the same loop with a bag-shaped surface passing between the plates; no current passes through that. The same loop gives two different answers.

The problem also shows in differential form. Take the divergence of \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J\): the left side is always zero (the second identity), so \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0\) must hold. But the continuity equation says \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t\), and while charge accumulates on the plates this is not zero.

Maxwell's solution was to add a term:

\[ \nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} \]

Check: take the divergence of both sides. The left side is still zero, and the right side now comes out zero as well:

\[ \mu_0\Big(\nabla\cdot\vec J + \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial(\nabla\cdot\vec E)}{\partial t}\Big) = \mu_0\Big(\nabla\cdot\vec J + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\Big) = 0 \]

Fully consistent with the continuity equation. In a capacitor the field between the plates is \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0A)\), so \(\varepsilon_0\,(dE/dt)\,A = dQ/dt = I\). The “displacement current” through the bag surface exactly equals the current in the wire.

Light

Where does light come from?

In a region far from charge and current \(\rho = 0\), \(\vec J = 0\). Read the remaining two curl equations like a chain: a changing \(\vec B\) creates a circulating \(\vec E\), and a changing \(\vec E\) creates a circulating \(\vec B\). A disturbance, once started, feeds itself and spreads with no need for a source. In 1865 Maxwell calculated the speed of this wave:

\[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = 3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} \]

The same as the measured speed of light. The conclusion: light is an electromagnetic wave. In 1887 Hertz confirmed this by producing radio waves in the laboratory. The whole spectrum from radio to gamma rays is a solution of the same equations; only the frequency differs. The step-by-step derivation is in Part 4.

A wave carries energy

Energy is stored in the fields; the energy per unit volume is \(u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2 + \tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2\). The flow of energy is given by the Poynting vector:

\[ \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E\times\vec B \qquad [\mathrm{W/m^2}] \]

In a plane wave \(\vec S\) points along the propagation direction; its time average gives the intensity, \(I = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0E_0^2\). Sunlight at the ground is about 1000 W/m², which means \(E_0 \approx 870\) V/m. The 1 mm² beam of a 1 mW laser pointer is also about 1000 W/m².

Fields in matter

A brief look at matter

Up to here everything was in vacuum. In matter the field polarizes and magnetizes the atoms, which produces their own bound charges and bound currents. Instead of following each atom one by one, we fold this response into two auxiliary fields:

\[ \vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P, \qquad \vec H = \frac{\vec B}{\mu_0} - \vec M \]

Maxwell's equations are then written only with the free charges and currents we control: \(\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f\), \(\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f + \partial\vec D/\partial t\). In a linear material \(\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E\), \(\vec B = \mu\vec H\), and the speed of light falls to \(v = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu} = c/n\). All of this is the subject of Part 3.

Self-check

Test yourself

For \(\vec F = (x^2, y, 0)\), what is the divergence at the point \((1, 2)\)?

\(\partial_x(x^2) + \partial_y(y) = 2x + 1 = 3\).

What is the curl of the shear field \(\vec F = (y, 0, 0)\), and which way does the wheel turn?

\((\nabla\times\vec F)_z = \partial_x F_y - \partial_y F_x = 0 - 1 = -1\). The negative z direction: it turns clockwise seen from above.

What is the net electric flux through a closed surface that does not enclose a charge?

Zero. The field lines of a charge outside enter the surface at one place and leave at another.

What does \(\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0\) say physically?

There is no magnetic charge (monopole); magnetic field lines neither start nor end anywhere and form closed loops.

What is the energy, in joules, of an electron accelerated through 10 V?

10 eV = \(10\times1.602\times10^{-19} = 1.602\times10^{-18}\) J. This is the starting value of the example in Part 2.

What is B at 1 cm from a wire carrying 10 A?
\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \frac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\cdot 10}{2\pi\cdot 0.01} = 2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm T \]

So 200 µT, about four times the Earth's field.

Why must the term \(\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t\) be added to Ampère's law?

Since the divergence of a curl is zero, the old form required \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0\); wherever charge accumulates (a charging capacitor) this contradicts the continuity equation. The added term restores consistency and makes light possible.

What is the time derivative of \(e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\)?

\(-i\omega\,e^{i(kx-\omega t)}\). The time derivative turns into multiplication by \(-i\omega\).
