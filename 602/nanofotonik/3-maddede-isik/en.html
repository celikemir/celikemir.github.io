Refractive index

The complex refractive index

The refractive index says how fast light travels in a medium and depends on frequency: \(n(\omega)\). To account for absorption too, it is written as a complex number:

\[ \tilde n = n + i\kappa \]
\(n\)
Real part. \(n = c/v\); \(v\) is the phase velocity of light in the medium.
\(\kappa\)
Extinction coefficient. It gives the absorption and attenuation.
Notation

The notebook uses the letter \(k\) for the extinction coefficient. To avoid confusion with the wave vector \(k\), \(\kappa\) (kappa) is used here.

To see what the two parts do, write a plane wave in the medium. If the wavenumber in vacuum is \(k_0 = 2\pi/\lambda_0 = \omega/c\), in the medium it is \(\tilde n k_0\):

\[ E(z,t) = E_0\, e^{i(\tilde n k_0 z - \omega t)} = E_0\,\underbrace{e^{-\kappa k_0 z}}_{\text{decay}}\;\underbrace{e^{i(n k_0 z-\omega t)}}_{\text{oscillation}} \]

The real part shortens the wavelength to \(\lambda_0/n\), and the imaginary part lowers the amplitude exponentially. Since the intensity we measure is the square of the amplitude:

\[ I(z) = I_0\,e^{-\alpha z}, \qquad \alpha = 2\kappa k_0 = \frac{4\pi\kappa}{\lambda_0} \]

Permittivity · Permeability

Electric permittivity and magnetic permeability

The refractive index comes from two responses of the material:

\(\varepsilon\)
Electric permittivity. How easily the material is polarized by an applied electric field. \(\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E\), in F/m.
\(\mu\)
Magnetic permeability. How strongly the material is magnetized by an external magnetic field. \(\vec B = \mu\vec H\), in H/m.

The vacuum values are \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m and \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7} \approx 1.257\times10^{-6}\) H/m. We define the material relative to these:

\[ \varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \mu_r = \frac{\mu}{\mu_0}, \qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}} \]

\(\varepsilon_r\) is also known as the dielectric constant; in vacuum \(\varepsilon_r = 1\). Since the speed in the medium is \(v = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}\):

\[ \tilde n = \frac{c}{v} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r\,\mu_r} \]

In nonmagnetic materials \(\mu_r \approx 1\). At optical frequencies almost all natural materials are like this; the magnetic response of atoms cannot follow an oscillation at 10¹⁴ Hz. This is why the formula actually used in nanophotonics is:

\[ \tilde n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tilde n^2 = \varepsilon_r \]
One step further

Metamaterials try to achieve \(\mu_r \neq 1\) at optical frequencies too, using artificial resonators smaller than the light. The idea of a negative refractive index comes from here.

Complex permittivity

The link between n, κ and ε₁, ε₂

In an absorbing material \(\varepsilon_r\) is also complex: \(\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon_1 + i\varepsilon_2\). Expanding the equality \(\tilde n^2 = \varepsilon_r\):

\[ (n+i\kappa)^2 = n^2 + 2in\kappa + i^2\kappa^2 = (n^2-\kappa^2) + i\,(2n\kappa) \]
\[ \varepsilon_1 = n^2-\kappa^2, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = 2n\kappa \]
Correction to the notebook

Check the expansion in the notebook. Since \(i^2 = -1\), \(\kappa^2\) comes with a minus sign and the imaginary part keeps a factor of \(n\): \(\varepsilon_1 = n^2-\kappa^2\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 2n\kappa\).

In the reverse direction, for \(\varepsilon_2 \geq 0\):

\[ n = \sqrt{\frac{|\varepsilon_r|+\varepsilon_1}{2}}, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\frac{|\varepsilon_r|-\varepsilon_1}{2}}, \qquad |\varepsilon_r| = \sqrt{\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2} \]

The reading rule: if \(\varepsilon_2 \neq 0\), there is absorption. \(\varepsilon_1\) can also be negative, as for metals in the visible. Then \(\kappa > n\), the light dies out within a few tens of nanometers and most of it is reflected.

The static \(\varepsilon_r\) of water is about 80, while in the visible \(n^2 \approx 1.78\). The rotational alignment of water molecules cannot follow a field at 10¹⁴ Hz; at that speed only the electrons respond.

Continuum approximation

Why does matter behave like a continuous medium?

The wavelength of green light is about 530 nm, while an atom is on the order of 0.1 nm (Bohr radius 0.053 nm). Thousands of atoms line up along a single wavelength. The wave sees not individual atoms but an average over many atoms. So we can add up the atomic dipoles, define a macroscopic polarization density \(\vec P\) and treat the medium as uniform in terms of polarization.

Seen from afar, the field of a charge distribution is written as a multipole expansion. Each term dies out with distance faster than the previous one:

TermSourceField
Monopolenet charge, point particle\(\propto 1/r^2\)
Dipoleseparated + and − charge\(\propto 1/r^3\)
Quadrupoletwo opposite dipoles\(\propto 1/r^4\)

Since an atom is neutral, its monopole term is zero. When a field is applied, the first nonzero and slowest-decaying term is the dipole. This is why we describe the optical response of matter with dipoles.

The velocity note in the notebook connects here too: in the Bohr model of hydrogen the electron orbits at \(2.18\times10^6\) m/s. This is about 0.7% of c, exactly \(\alpha c\) (\(\alpha \approx 1/137\), the fine-structure constant). Non-relativistic physics is enough for atomic electrons.

Dipole moment · Polarization density

From the dipole to the polarization density

When \(\vec E\) is applied to a neutral atom, the nucleus feels a force along the field and the electron cloud in the opposite direction (\(\vec F = q\vec E\)). The charge centers separate by \(\vec d\) and the atom becomes a dipole:

\[ \vec p = q\,\vec d \]

\(\vec p\) is the dipole moment, a microscopic quantity belonging to a single atom. This response comes mostly from the valence electrons, which are loosely bound in the outer shell. The nucleus and inner-shell (core) electrons behave like a tightly bound ion.

The total dipole moment per unit volume is the polarization density, a macroscopic quantity:

\[ \vec P = \frac{N\vec p}{V} = \frac{Nq\vec d}{V} \qquad [\mathrm{C/m^2}] \]

Unit analysis: \(\dfrac{[\text{charge}]\cdot[\text{length}]}{[\text{length}]^3} = \dfrac{[\text{charge}]}{[\text{length}]^2}\).

Linear response

Linear materials: χ, D and E

If the field is not too strong, \(\vec P\) is proportional to the field. Polarization is an induced effect created by the applied field:

\[ \vec P = \chi_e\,\varepsilon_0\,\vec E \]

\(\chi_e\) is the electric susceptibility, dimensionless. Check: the unit of \(\varepsilon_0\vec E\) is

\[ \frac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm V}{\mathrm m} = \frac{\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm J}{\mathrm{C\,m}} = \frac{\mathrm C}{\mathrm{m^2}} \]

just like \(\vec P\). The displacement vector \(\vec D\) is the sum of two contributions:

\[ \vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \chi_e\varepsilon_0\vec E + \varepsilon_0\vec E = (1+\chi_e)\,\varepsilon_0\vec E = \varepsilon\vec E \]
\[ \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1+\chi_e), \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1+\chi_e \]

Why does polarization reduce the field inside?

Write the equality as \(\varepsilon_0\vec E = \vec D - \vec P\). \(\vec D\) comes from the free charges; it corresponds to the applied field you control from outside. \(\vec E\) is the total field inside the matter. The bound charges that polarization accumulates on the surfaces produce a field opposing the applied one; the total field inside falls by a factor of \(\varepsilon_r\).

A metal is the extreme case: free electrons move until the field vanishes, and in the static state \(\vec E = 0\) inside. In a semiconductor or insulator there are no or very few free electrons; bound charges can only shift slightly. The field decreases but does not vanish.

One step further

At optical frequencies the field inside a metal is not exactly zero; it leaks into a layer of a few tens of nm called the skin depth. You can see it by choosing silver in the explorer and zooming in. Plasmonics happens in this thin layer.

Isotropic · Anisotropic

Isotropic and anisotropic materials

In an isotropic material the response is the same in every direction: \(\chi_e\) and \(\varepsilon_r\) are scalars, and \(\vec P\) and \(\vec E\) are parallel. In an anisotropic material, for example crystals such as calcite or LiNbO₃, the response depends on direction and \(\chi\) becomes a tensor:

\[ \begin{pmatrix}P_x\\P_y\\P_z\end{pmatrix} = \varepsilon_0 \begin{pmatrix}\chi_{xx}&\chi_{xy}&\chi_{xz}\\ \chi_{yx}&\chi_{yy}&\chi_{yz}\\ \chi_{zx}&\chi_{zy}&\chi_{zz}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}E_x\\E_y\\E_z\end{pmatrix} \]

In this case \(\vec P\) is generally not parallel to \(\vec E\). With the crystal axes chosen, the tensor becomes diagonal. If the diagonal elements differ, the speed of light depends on its polarization; this is called birefringence. Wave plates and polarizers work with this effect.

Nonlinear optics

Nonlinear materials

In strong fields such as lasers, \(\vec P\) also contains higher powers of the field:

\[ P = \varepsilon_0\left(\chi^{(1)}E + \chi^{(2)}E^2 + \chi^{(3)}E^3 + \cdots\right) \]
\(\chi^{(1)}\)
Linear term. Refraction and absorption come from here.
\(\chi^{(2)}\)
Second order. Second harmonic generation (SHG, frequency doubling): 1064 nm → 532 nm green laser. It is nonzero only in crystals without a center of symmetry.
\(\chi^{(3)}\)
Third order. Third harmonic generation (THG), four-wave mixing (FWM) and the Kerr effect, that is, the change of refractive index with intensity. It exists in every material.

Where the new frequencies come from is seen from trigonometry. Putting in \(E = E_0\cos\omega t\):

\[ \cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12\big(1+\cos 2\omega t\big), \qquad \cos^3\omega t = \tfrac14\big(3\cos\omega t+\cos 3\omega t\big) \]

The square term produces \(2\omega\) and a constant (DC, optical rectification) component. The cube term produces \(3\omega\) and changes the response at \(\omega\).

Free and bound charge

Free and bound charges

In matter Gauss's law is written with free charges:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f \]

\(\rho_f\) is the free charge density: the charges you put on electrodes and can control. \(\rho_b\) is the bound charge density: it arises from polarization and cannot be controlled directly. The relation between them:

\[ \rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P, \qquad \sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n, \qquad \varepsilon_0\,\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_f + \rho_b \]

This is the benefit of using \(\vec D\): it removes from the equation the bound charges you do not know. The surface charges in the capacitor figure are the ones calculated with \(\sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n\).

Magnetization

Magnetic response: dia- and paramagnetism

The electron has two angular momenta, and both produce a magnetic moment \(\vec m\):

\(\vec L\)
Orbital angular momentum. When an external field is applied, the orbital motion adjusts so as to oppose the change, by Lenz's law. The induced moment is opposite to the field, \(\vec m \parallel -\vec H\). This is diamagnetism: present in every material, weak, \(\chi_m \lt 0\).
\(\vec S\)
Spin angular momentum. Atoms with unpaired electrons have a permanent moment that tries to line up with the field, \(\vec m \parallel \vec H\). This is paramagnetism, \(\chi_m \gt 0\).

Diamagnetism is much weaker than paramagnetism. In atoms with unpaired electrons paramagnetism dominates.

MaterialχmType
Water−9×10⁻⁶diamagnetic
Copper−1×10⁻⁵diamagnetic
Aluminum+2×10⁻⁵paramagnetic
Iron≈ 10³–10⁵ferromagnetic, nonlinear

The magnetization density is the total magnetic moment per unit volume. In a linear material it is proportional to the field:

\[ \vec M = \frac{N\vec m}{V}, \qquad \vec M = \chi_m\vec H \]

\(\chi_m\) is the magnetic susceptibility, dimensionless. The total field:

\[ \vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M) = \mu_0(\chi_m\vec H + \vec H) = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)\vec H = \mu\vec H \]
\[ \mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m), \qquad \mu_r = 1+\chi_m \]

\(\vec M\) is related to bound currents: the volume current \(\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M\) and the surface current \(\vec K_b = \vec M\times\hat n\). These cannot be controlled. \(\vec H\) is related to free currents, that is, the controllable currents you pass through wires.

Side by side

Comparing the electric and magnetic sides

ElectricMagnetic
Microscopic moment\(\vec p = q\vec d\)\(\vec m\)
Density\(\vec P = N\vec p/V\)\(\vec M = N\vec m/V\)
Linear response\(\vec P = \chi_e\varepsilon_0\vec E\)\(\vec M = \chi_m\vec H\)
Auxiliary field\(\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P\)\(\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M\)
Material constant\(\varepsilon_r = 1+\chi_e\)\(\mu_r = 1+\chi_m\)
Controlled source\(\rho_f\), free charge\(\vec J_f\), free current
Uncontrolled\(\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P\)\(\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M\)

Note the asymmetry: in electricity the fundamental field is \(\vec E\), and the auxiliary field \(\vec D\). In magnetism the fundamental field is \(\vec B\), and the auxiliary field \(\vec H\). This is why \(\chi_e\) is defined with \(\vec E\) and \(\chi_m\) with \(\vec H\): both are written with the field closest to the side “you control”.

Self-check

Test yourself

At 633 nm n = 3.88 and κ = 0.02 for silicon. What are ε₁ and ε₂?

\(\varepsilon_1 = 3.88^2 - 0.02^2 \approx 15.05\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 2(3.88)(0.02) \approx 0.155\).

The static εr of water is 80, so why is n only 1.33 in the visible?

The static value also includes the orientational polarization of molecules. At optical frequencies the molecules are too slow to rotate; only the electrons respond. Optical \(\varepsilon_r = n^2 \approx 1.78\).

For silver at 633 nm κ = 4.28. What is the depth at which the intensity falls to 1/e?

\(1/\alpha = \lambda_0/(4\pi\kappa) = 633/(4\pi\cdot 4.28) \approx 11.8\) nm.

What is the unit of polarization density, and how does it come out?

C/m². \(\vec P = Nq\vec d/V\): charge times length divided by volume.

Why is χ⁽²⁾ zero in centrosymmetric materials?

With central symmetry, reversing the field must exactly reverse the polarization: \(P(-E) = -P(E)\). Even-power terms break this condition, so their coefficients must be zero.

From which angular momentum, and because of which law, does diamagnetism arise?

From the orbital angular momentum. Lenz's law: the induced moment opposes the applied field, \(\chi_m \lt 0\).
