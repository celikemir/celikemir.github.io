Matter waves

The wavelength of particles

In 1924 de Broglie proposed that a wave accompanies every particle. Three years later Davisson and Germer showed this in an experiment: 54 eV electrons sent onto a nickel crystal formed a diffraction pattern, just like X-rays. The note in the notebook: quantum particles, for example electrons, show wave properties.

In the non-relativistic regime the momentum is proportional to the wave vector:

\[ \vec p = \hbar\vec k, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} \]

Substituting \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) gives \(p = h/\lambda\). Inverting it gives the de Broglie wavelength:

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} \]
For the exam

In the notebook the value 6.625×10⁻³⁴ J·s belongs to \(h\) (the circled h). \(\hbar\) is this divided by \(2\pi\), 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J·s. \(\lambda = h/p\) but \(p = \hbar k\); mixing up the two shifts the result by a factor of 2π.

Free electron

The free electron: energy and wavelength

The energy of a free electron is entirely kinetic:

\[ E = \frac{p^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \]

Combining \(p = \sqrt{2mE}\) and \(\lambda = h/p\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mE}} \]

Putting in the constants for the electron gives a practical shortcut:

\[ \lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}] \approx \frac{1.226}{\sqrt{E\,[\mathrm{eV}]}} \]

This is also a dispersion relation

Writing \(E = \hbar\omega\) gives \(\omega(k) = \hbar k^2/2m\) for a free electron. Unlike the photon's line \(\omega = ck\), this is a parabola: the electron wave is dispersive even in vacuum. The definitions of Part 1 give here:

\[ v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{p}{m} = v, \qquad v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{\hbar k}{2m} = \frac{v}{2} \]

The familiar classical velocity of the particle is the group velocity; the phase velocity is half of it. This is why it is consistent to think of the electron as a wave packet: the packet moves just like the particle itself.

Worked example

Example: a 10 eV electron

The example in the notebook: find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron with energy \(E = 10\) eV.

  1. Convert the energy to joules.

    \[ E = 10 \times 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J} = 1.602\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm J \]

  2. Find the momentum.

    \[ p = \sqrt{2m_eE} = \sqrt{2\times 9.11\times10^{-31}\times 1.602\times10^{-18}} = 1.708\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s} \]

  3. Find the wavelength.

    \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.708\times10^{-24}} = 3.88\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m \approx 0.39\ \mathrm{nm} \]

  4. Check with the shortcut: \(1.226/\sqrt{10} = 0.388\) nm. It matches.

This electron travels at about \(1.9\times10^6\) m/s, 0.6% of c. The non-relativistic formula is comfortably valid.

Why it matters: confinement in matter

The example gives the wavelength of the incoming light as 1000 nm. By comparison the wavelength of the electron is about 2600 times smaller. 0.39 nm is of the same order as interatomic distances. So the electron wave feels structures at the atomic scale: it diffracts from a crystal, and when confined in structures of a few nm (quantum wells, quantum dots) its energy levels become discrete. The “confinement in matter” note in the notebook describes this. For light, similar effects begin only in structures of hundreds of nm; that is the scale of nanophotonics.

Comparing at the same energy is even more striking. A 1000 nm photon has an energy of 1.24 eV. An electron with the same 1.24 eV has a wavelength of 1.10 nm, about 900 times shorter. In the calculator below the “same energy” row shows this ratio for every value.

Photon

The photon: the particle side of light

The dispersion relation of light in vacuum is \(\omega = ck\). Multiplying both sides by \(\hbar\) gives the relation between energy and momentum:

\[ \hbar\omega = \hbar c k \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = pc \]

This is the dispersion law of the photon. Since the photon has zero rest mass, all its energy comes from its momentum. In terms of frequency, using \(c = \lambda\nu\):

\[ E = \hbar\omega = \frac{h}{2\pi}\,2\pi\nu = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

For the momentum too, \(p = E/c = h/\lambda\): the same de Broglie relation as for the electron. What separates the two particles is their dispersion relations.

Unit shortcut

The 1240 rule

\(hc = 1.986\times10^{-25}\) J·m. Dividing this by 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J and converting meters to nanometers:

\[ hc = 1239.84\ \mathrm{eV\cdot nm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E\,[\mathrm{eV}] = \frac{1240}{\lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]} \]

The example in the notebook: for \(\lambda = 400\) nm, \(E = 1240/400 = 3.10\) eV. The notebook has 3.097 eV; the difference comes from rounding the constant.

From band gap to cutoff wavelength

A semiconductor cannot absorb photons with energy below the band gap \(E_g\). Read backward, the rule gives the cutoff wavelength: \(\lambda_c = 1240/E_g\).

Material\(E_g\)\(\lambda_c\)
GaN3.4 eV365 nm
GaAs1.42 eV873 nm
Si1.12 eV1107 nm
In₀.₅₃Ga₀.₄₇As0.75 eV1653 nm

A 1550 nm photon carries 0.80 eV and cannot cross the 1.12 eV band gap of silicon: silicon is transparent at this wavelength. This is why silicon photonic waveguides work at 1550 nm, and why telecom detectors are made of InGaAs rather than silicon.

Self-check

Test yourself

What is the de Broglie wavelength of a 100 eV electron?

With the shortcut \(1.226/\sqrt{100} = 0.123\) nm. The long way: \(p = \sqrt{2 m_e E} = 5.40\times10^{-24}\) kg·m/s, \(\lambda = h/p = 1.23\times10^{-10}\) m.

What is the energy of a 1550 nm photon in eV?

\(E = 1240/1550 = 0.80\) eV.

What is the reason that \(v_p = v_g\) for a photon?

The dispersion relation is linear: \(\omega = ck\). Since the curve is a line, \(\omega/k = d\omega/dk = c\).

How are the group and phase velocities of a free electron wave related to the velocity of the particle?

Since \(\omega = \hbar k^2/2m\), \(v_g = \hbar k/m = v\) and \(v_p = \hbar k/2m = v/2\). The particle velocity is the group velocity.

If you write \(p = h k\) instead of \(p = \hbar k\) in a problem, by how much does the result shift?

A factor of \(2\pi\). \(\lambda = h/p\) is consistent together with \(p = \hbar k\); since \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\), \(\hbar k = h/\lambda\).

Does silicon absorb 1550 nm light?

No. The photon's energy is 0.80 eV and the band gap of silicon is 1.12 eV. The photon cannot lift an electron to the conduction band; silicon is transparent at this wavelength.
