From Maxwell to the Wave Equation

We write the four equations in matter, in special cases and in integral form, and derive the wave equation in free space step by step. At the end the speed of light comes out of μ₀ and ε₀ from the plane wave solution.

Vector calculus

Nabla and the four operations

The language of Maxwell's equations is a single operator: nabla.

\[ \nabla = \hat x\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat y\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat z\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \]

The derivation will also need a vector identity, the BAC-CAB rule:

\[ \vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = \vec b\,(\vec a\cdot\vec c) - \vec c\,(\vec a\cdot\vec b) \]

Maxwell’s equations

Maxwell's equations in matter

Four equations, two divergences and two curls. With the medium selector you can see how the equations simplify in special cases; the terms that change are colored.

Correction to the notebook

For an unpolarized medium the notebook has “μ₀H + M = B”. The correct form is \(\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M)\): \(\vec M\) and \(\vec H\) have the same unit (A/m), and \(\mu_0\) multiplies both. When \(\vec M = 0\) the result is the same, \(\vec B = \mu_0\vec H\).

In free space the four equations are nearly symmetric between E and B. There is no source, yet there is a nonzero solution: changing B creates circulation in E (3), and changing E in B (4). This pair feeding each other is light itself.

Divergence and Stokes theorems

From differential to integral form

The integral forms are found with two theorems. The divergence theorem converts equations 1 and 2, and Stokes' theorem equations 3 and 4.

Example, Faraday's law. Integrate equation 3 over an open surface and apply Stokes' theorem to the left side:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_S (\nabla\times\vec E)\cdot d\vec a &= -\int_S \frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec a \\ \Rightarrow\quad \oint_C \vec E\cdot d\vec l &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_S \vec B\cdot d\vec a = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \end{aligned} \]

\(\Phi_B\) is the magnetic flux through the surface. This is the reason for the “open surfaces” note in the notebook: in Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell the surface is open, in the Gauss laws it is closed.

Displacement current

Why is there a ∂D/∂t term?

Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law. The divergence of a curl is always zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec H) &= \nabla\cdot\vec J_f + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\nabla\cdot\vec D\big) \\ &= \nabla\cdot\vec J_f + \frac{\partial\rho_f}{\partial t} \end{aligned} \]

The result is charge conservation, that is, the continuity equation. Without the \(\partial\vec D/\partial t\) term the left side would be zero while the right side remained \(\nabla\cdot\vec J_f\); in a charging capacitor this is not zero. Without this term added by Maxwell there would be no electromagnetic waves either: in the derivation below it is exactly the term that links changing E to B.

Derivation

The wave equation in free space

  1. Start with Faraday's law.

    \[ \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} \]

  2. Take the curl of both sides. The order of space and time derivatives can be swapped.

    \[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = -\nabla\times\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\nabla\times\vec B\big) \]

  3. Apply the BAC-CAB rule to the left side: \(\vec a = \nabla\), \(\vec b = \nabla\), \(\vec c = \vec E\).

    \[ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E) - (\nabla\cdot\nabla)\vec E = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E) - \nabla^2\vec E \]

    The order matters with operators: \(\nabla\) always acts on what is to its right. So the \(\vec c(\vec a\cdot\vec b)\) term is written not as \(\vec E(\nabla\cdot\nabla)\) but as \((\nabla\cdot\nabla)\vec E\).

  4. In free space equation 1 is \(\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0\); the first term drops.

    \[ \nabla(\underbrace{\nabla\cdot\vec E}_{=\,0}) - \nabla^2\vec E = -\nabla^2\vec E \]

  5. Put equation 4 into the right side, \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t\).

    \[ -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}\Big) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} \]

  6. Equate the two sides; the wave equation for E:

    \[ \nabla^2\vec E = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} \]

For B the same path starts from equation 4 and uses equations 2 and 3:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) &= \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\nabla\times\vec E\big) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec B}{\partial t^2} \\ \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) &= \nabla(\underbrace{\nabla\cdot\vec B}_{=\,0}) - \nabla^2\vec B = -\nabla^2\vec B \end{aligned} \]
\[ \nabla^2\vec B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\vec B}{\partial t^2} \]
Correction to the notebook

At the bottom of the notebook, when the wave equation for B is rewritten, the right side was copied as \(\partial^2\vec E/\partial t^2\). The correct form is \(\partial^2\vec B/\partial t^2\); the first version above it is right.

Comparing with the general wave equation, the propagation speed can be read off:

\[ \nabla^2 u = \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = c \]

In a linear, homogeneous, source-free material the same derivation gives \(\mu\varepsilon\) in place of \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0\): \(v = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r} = c/n\). The relation \(n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r}\) of Part 3 comes from here.

Careful

Writing \(\nabla^2\vec E\) component by component as \((\nabla^2E_x, \nabla^2E_y, \nabla^2E_z)\) is correct only in Cartesian coordinates. In cylindrical or spherical coordinates the unit vectors also change with position and extra terms appear.

Boundary and initial conditions

How many conditions does a solution need?

The wave equation is second order in each variable. Each second-order derivative requires two conditions:

VariableConditionA number
xboundary conditions2
yboundary conditions2
zboundary conditions2
tinitial condition2
Total8

The two conditions in time are the initial field and its time derivative:

\[ \vec E(\vec r, t=0) = \vec E_0(\vec r), \qquad \frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=0} = \vec V_0(\vec r) \]

Giving the second is the same as giving the initial magnetic field; in free space \(\partial\vec E/\partial t = c^2\,\nabla\times\vec B\). This is why the arrow in the notebook goes to \(\vec B(\vec r, t=0)\).

The Sommerfeld radiation condition

In open problems extending to infinity you cannot write a condition on a distant boundary. Instead the Sommerfeld radiation condition is used: at infinity there are only outgoing waves, and no waves come in from infinity. In three dimensions, with \(e^{-i\omega t}\) time dependence:

\[ \lim_{r\to\infty} r\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} - iku\right) = 0 \]

The components are not independent

Each of the \(E_x, E_y, E_z, B_x, B_y, B_z\) components satisfies a scalar wave equation on its own, but they are not independent of each other. The divergence equations, for example

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} = 0 \]

and the curl equations link them. For a plane wave \(\nabla \to i\vec k\) and \(\partial/\partial t \to -i\omega\), and these links reduce to a very simple form:

\[ \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec k\cdot\vec E_0 = 0 \] \[ \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} \;\Rightarrow\; i\vec k\times\vec E_0 = i\omega\vec B_0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec B_0 = \frac{1}{c}\,\hat k\times\vec E_0 \]

The field is perpendicular to the propagation direction: the wave is transverse. Knowing E fully determines B.

Plane wave

The plane wave solution

The simplest solution of the wave equation is the plane wave:

\[ \vec E(\vec r,t) = \vec E_0\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)}, \qquad \vec B(\vec r,t) = \vec B_0\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)} \]

For simplicity choose the propagation direction as \(\vec k = k\hat z\), the polarization as \(\hat x\), and substitute into the equation:

\[ \vec E(z,t) = E_0\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\,\hat x \] \[ \nabla^2\vec E = E_0\,(ik)^2 e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\,\hat x = -k^2\vec E \] \[ \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,(-i\omega)^2\vec E = -\omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0\vec E \] \[ \Rightarrow\; E_0\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\,\hat x\,\big(-k^2 + \omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0\big) = 0 \]

If the field is nonzero the parenthesis must be zero:

\[ k^2 = \omega^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} = c \]

This is the vacuum dispersion relation of Part 1, \(\omega = ck\); here it came out of Maxwell's equations. Numerically:

\[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.257\times10^{-6}\,\frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm A^2}\times 8.854\times10^{-12}\,\frac{\mathrm C^2}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}}}} \approx 2.998\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} \]

Units: \(\frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm A^2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm C^2}{\mathrm{N\,m^2}} = \frac{\mathrm C^2}{\mathrm A^2\,\mathrm m^2} = \frac{\mathrm s^2}{\mathrm m^2}\), because \(\mathrm A = \mathrm C/\mathrm s\); this is the red note on the board. The inverse of the square root gives m/s. With four-digit constants it comes out as 2.998×10⁸; the exact value of 299 792 458 m/s in the notebook comes from the exact values of the constants.

The real field is the real part of the complex expression. B also follows from Faraday's law:

\[ \vec E = E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B = \frac{E_0}{c}\cos(kz-\omega t)\,\hat y \]

E, B and \(\vec k\) are mutually perpendicular; E and B oscillate in phase; \(|\vec B| = |\vec E|/c\).

Self-check

Test yourself

What would happen if \(\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0\) were not used in the derivation?

The \(\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E)\) term would remain and the equation would not be a pure wave equation. With charge present this term brings the sources into the equation.

Is the surface used in the integral form of Faraday's law open or closed?

Open. Its boundary is the curve C, and it turns into a line integral by Stokes' theorem. A closed surface is used in the Gauss laws, with the divergence theorem.

What is the wave speed in a medium with εr = 2.25 and μr = 1?

\(n = \sqrt{2.25} = 1.5\), \(v = c/1.5 = 2\times10^8\) m/s.

If \(E_0 = 300\) V/m in a plane wave, what is \(B_0\)?

\(B_0 = E_0/c = 300/(3\times10^8) = 10^{-6}\) T = 1 µT.

How many initial conditions does the wave equation need, and why?

Two, because it is second order in time. \(\vec E(\vec r,0)\) and \(\partial\vec E/\partial t\,(\vec r,0)\); the second is equivalent to giving the initial B.

Why are the six components of E and B not independent?

The divergence and curl equations link them. In a plane wave \(\vec k\cdot\vec E_0 = 0\) and \(\vec B_0 = \hat k\times\vec E_0/c\): the two transverse components of E determine the whole field.
