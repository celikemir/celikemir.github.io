Which materials? bound charges

recording 0:00

The model describes bound charges (bound charges), that is, valence electrons bound to atomic nuclei (valence electrons bound to atomic nuclei). So it is applied to insulators (insulators) and semiconductors (semiconductors).

In conductors (conductors) the conduction electrons roam freely, and for them the Drude model (Drude model) is used. This is what appears in the transcript as "Durant" or "Durand mode".

not in the lecture The Drude model is really the Lorentz model with the spring removed, taking \(\omega_0 = 0\). There is no force pulling the free electron to the nucleus; only the damping and the external field remain.

2

The classical analogy damped driven mass-spring oscillator

recording 0:02

The lecturer compares the model to a classical mass-spring-damper system (mass-spring-damper system). The definition on the board: bound electrons are treated as a harmonic oscillator (harmonic oscillator), as if tied to the nucleus by a spring, and the system is driven by the electromagnetic field (driven by the electromagnetic field).

Mechanical partEnglishIts counterpart in the model
Mass \(m\)massElectron
Spring, \(F = -kx\)spring, restoring forceCoulomb attraction between the nucleus and the valence electron
Damperdamper, damping termRadiation and scattering losses, \(\gamma\)
External force \(F(t)\)driving forceThe electric field of the incoming EM wave, \(qE_0e^{-i\omega t}\)

Without damping the system would oscillate forever after one push. The oscillation has to die out after the wave leaves the material, and \(\gamma\) makes that happen. The lecturer defined it as “the term that keeps the oscillations from lasting forever” (avoids the oscillations to last infinite time).

3

The restoring force restoring force

recording 0:05

A spring pulls the mass back to equilibrium. In the model this job is done by the attractive Coulomb force (attractive Coulomb force) between the nucleus and the valence electron.

\[ F_R = -kx = -m\omega_0^2 x, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} \]

\(\omega_0\) is the natural frequency of the electron (natural frequency), also called the resonant frequency (resonant frequency). \(x\) is not the absolute position of the electron but its displacement from equilibrium (displacement from its equilibrium position).

How ω₀ connects to the quantum side

Since the energy of the electron is quantized (quantized energy levels), it can absorb only energies corresponding to differences between levels. So \(\omega_0\) cannot take every value:

\[ \hbar\omega_0 = E_f - E_i \]

Since an atom has many levels, there are many natural frequencies such as \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\). For now the lecture proceeds with a single \(\omega_0\); the sum comes in Section 10.

4

Damping damping

recording 0:09 – 0:51

The longest part of the lecture. The lecturer lists two mechanisms of energy loss, numbered in this order on the board too.

Mechanism 1: radiation radiation

Every accelerating charge radiates energy. Electrons in an antenna emitting radio waves are the same phenomenon. The radiated power is given by the Larmor formula (Larmor formula):

\[ \mathcal{P}_{\text{rad}} = \frac{e^2 a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3} \]

A radiating particle feels a recoil force in return for the energy it loses. This is called the Abraham–Lorentz force or radiation reaction force (Abraham–Lorentz recoil force, radiation reaction). It depends on the second derivative of the velocity, the third derivative of the position:

\[ F_{\text{rad}} = +\frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}\,\ddot v \]

In the harmonic approximation (harmonic approximation) \(x \propto e^{-i\omega t}\), so each derivative only multiplies by \(-i\omega\). Two derivatives bring \((-i\omega)^2 = -\omega^2\):

\[ \ddot v = -\omega^2 v \;\Rightarrow\; F_{\text{rad}} = -\frac{e^2\omega^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}\,v \equiv -m\gamma v \]

This is the answer to a student's question in the lecture, “why does the third derivative come out proportional to the velocity”. In a harmonic solution taking a derivative just means multiplying by a constant, so the third derivative becomes proportional to the velocity.

Mistake on the board · sign

The board says \(F_{\text{rad}} = -\dfrac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}\ddot v\), with \(\ddot v \propto -\omega^2 v\) just above it. Combining the two gives a force \(+(\dots)\,v\), pushing the electron along its velocity. That would be anti-damping rather than damping. The correct form has a plus sign, as above.

Mechanism 2: scattering scattering

Valence electrons interact with other electrons and with phonons (phonons). A phonon is a collective excitation of the vibrations of the crystal lattice (collective excitation of lattice vibrations). In these interactions the energy of the electron passes to the lattice and turns into heat (dissipated as heat).

Mistake in the lecture · which dominates

In the lecture and on the board radiation appears as the “first and most important” mechanism. This is not true for solids. With the lecturer's own value \(\omega_0 = 3\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\):

\[ \gamma_{\text{rad}} = \frac{e^2\omega_0^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 m c^3} \approx 6\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

The \(\gamma\) the lecturer gave for silicon is \(10^{13}\)–\(10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). There are 5–6 orders of magnitude between them. In a solid the damping is set almost entirely by scattering. Radiation damping stands out in isolated atoms of a dilute gas and gives the natural linewidth (natural linewidth). Also, \(\gamma_{\text{rad}}\) depends on \(\omega^2\), and taking a constant \(\gamma\) is valid only near resonance.

Why −bv? The work argument

In classical mechanics damping forces are generally taken to depend on velocity: \(F = -bv\), in this model \(b = m\gamma\). The lecturer showed why this form is right through the work done (work done):

\[ W = \int F\,dx = \int F\,v\,dt = -b\int v^2\,dt \;<\; 0 \]

\(b\) is positive and \(v^2\) is positive, so the integral is positive. The minus sign in front guarantees that the work is always negative: the electron loses energy (energy dissipation) and never gains it. In response to a student's question: air drag in free fall is modeled with the same form (linear drag).

The unit and meaning of γ

Since \(m\gamma v\) is a force, \(\mathrm{kg}\cdot[\gamma]\cdot\mathrm{m/s} = \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}\), so:

\[ [\gamma] = \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\tau} \]

\(\gamma\) is the damping rate (damping rate), with unit hertz. Its physical meaning is the collision frequency (collision frequency): how many times per unit time an electron interacts with other electrons or phonons. \(\tau\) is the mean free time between two collisions (mean free time, relaxation time). If \(\gamma\) is large the system loses its energy fast, and if small, slowly.

If only the damping term is kept, the equation is solved in one line:

\[ m\dot v = -m\gamma v \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dv}{v} = -\gamma\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; v(t) = v_0\,e^{-\gamma t} \]

The velocity decays exponentially (exponential decay). The larger \(\gamma\) is, the steeper the fall.

\(\gamma\) depends on the medium (medium-dependent). In a dense medium collisions are frequent and \(\gamma\) is large. In a dilute gas collisions are rare, \(\gamma\) is small and the spectral lines are narrow.

In the transcript · gases

The transcript has the sentence “damping is very high in gases”. The truth is the opposite. A student's intuition that “γ increases as the medium gets denser” is right.

5

The equation of motion equation of motion

recording 0:52

Newton's second law \(F = ma\), with three forces on the right side: the restoring force, damping and the \(F = qE\) coming from the electric field of the incoming wave.

\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -m\omega_0^2 x - m\gamma\frac{dx}{dt} + qE_0\,e^{-i\omega t} \]

Do not mix up the two frequencies: \(\omega\) is the frequency of the incoming EM wave, the driving frequency (driving frequency). \(\omega_0\) is the electron's own natural frequency (natural frequency).

Where did the spatial part of the field go? dipole approximation

The full form of a plane wave is \(E_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\), but the equation has only the time part. The lecturer explained this with scales: the electron oscillates in a region of about 0.1 nm, while the wavelength of visible light is about 500 nm.

\[ kx \sim \frac{2\pi}{500\ \text{nm}}\times 0.1\ \text{nm} \approx 1.3\times10^{-3} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{ikx} \approx 1 \]

In the region the electron sees, the field can be taken as constant in space (spatially uniform). This is called the dipole or long-wavelength approximation (long-wavelength approximation). The transcript says “0.1 mm”, but the board correctly says “x ∝ 0.1 nm”.

6

The solution trial solution

recording 0:59

A trial solution in harmonic form (trial solution, harmonic form) is taken. \(\tilde x\) is a time-independent complex amplitude (complex amplitude):

\[ x(t) = \tilde x\,e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad \dot x = -i\omega\,\tilde x\,e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad \ddot x = -\omega^2\,\tilde x\,e^{-i\omega t} \]

Put into the equation, the \(e^{-i\omega t}\) in every term cancels:

\[ \tilde x\left(-m\omega^2 + m\omega_0^2 - i\omega m\gamma\right) = qE_0 \]
\[ \boxed{\;\tilde x = \frac{qE_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\omega\gamma}\;} \]

Unit check (dimensional analysis): the denominator is \(\mathrm{s^{-2}}\) and the numerator \(qE_0/m\) is an acceleration, \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\). The ratio is in meters, so \(\tilde x\) really is a length. As noted on the board, this result is for a single valence electron (only one valence electron).

What the complex amplitude says not in the lecture

Writing \(\tilde x = |\tilde x|\,e^{i\varphi}\), the real motion is \(x(t) = |\tilde x|\cos(\omega t - \varphi)\). So the electron follows the field with a lag of \(\varphi\) (phase lag):

\[ |\tilde x| = \frac{qE_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2} \]

The imaginary part of the complex number carries this lag. As you will see in Section 9, this is exactly why absorption comes from the imaginary part.

From one electron to the material polarization density

recording 1:08

A dipole forms between the displaced electron and the nucleus. The dipole moment (dipole moment) is the charge times the distance between the two charges:

\[ \mathbf p = q\,\mathbf x \]

If there are \(N\) such dipoles in a volume \(V\), the total dipole moment per unit volume is the polarization density (polarization density):

\[ P = \frac{N}{V}\,q\tilde x = \frac{N}{V}\,\frac{q^2E_0/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\omega\gamma} \]

\(N/V\) is the bound electron density (bound electron density). Since \(q^2\) appears, the negative charge of the electron does not affect the result.

The electric susceptibility electric susceptibility

The defining relation between polarization and field is \(P = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE\). Equating the two expressions cancels \(E_0\):

\[ \frac{N}{V}\,\frac{q^2E_0/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\omega\gamma} = \varepsilon_0\chi_eE_0 \;\Rightarrow\; \chi_e = \frac{Nq^2}{Vm\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\omega\gamma} \]

To tidy up the constants, a \(\omega_0^2\) is multiplied and divided on the board:

\[ \chi_e = f\,\frac{\omega_0^2}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\omega\gamma}, \qquad f = \frac{Nq^2/(Vm\varepsilon_0)}{\omega_0^2} \]

\(f\) is the oscillator strength (oscillator strength). The question on the board is “what is the unit of f?”, and the answer is unitless (unitless, dimensionless):

\[ \left[\frac{Nq^2}{Vm\varepsilon_0}\right] = \frac{\mathrm{m^{-3}}\,\mathrm{C^2}}{\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}} = \frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm{kg\cdot m}} = \mathrm{s^{-2}} \]

not in the lecture This squared frequency in the numerator has a name: the plasma frequency (plasma frequency), \(\omega_p^2 = Nq^2/(Vm\varepsilon_0)\). So \(f = (\omega_p/\omega_0)^2\). This expression will come up again in the Drude model.

Inconsistency on the board · typical value of f

The board says “f: typical values 0.1–0.8 (silicon, glass, semiconductor)”. This range does not agree with the definition on the board. In silicon the atomic density is \(5\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), with 4 valence electrons per atom, so \(N/V = 2\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\). This gives \(\omega_p \approx 2.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (\(\hbar\omega_p \approx 16.6\) eV). With the lecturer's value \(\omega_0 = 3\times10^{15}\), \(f \approx 70\). It is easy to check: \(\varepsilon_r(0) = 1 + f\), and the static value of silicon is 11.7, so with a single oscillator \(f \approx 10.7\).

The 0.1–0.8 range belongs to the quantum mechanical oscillator strength, which is defined differently:

\[ \chi_e = \frac{Ne^2}{V\varepsilon_0 m}\sum_j \frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_j\omega}, \qquad \sum_j f_j = 1 \]

Here \(f_j\) is the “electron share” of a transition (the Thomas–Reiche–Kuhn sum rule, TRK sum rule) and \(\omega_p^2\) stays outside. The board mixes up the two definitions. If the exam asks for a numerical \(f\), do not be surprised if the result comes out greater than 1.

The dielectric function relative permittivity

recording 1:31

From the definition of the electric displacement field (electric displacement field):

\[ \mathbf D = \varepsilon_0\mathbf E + \mathbf P = \varepsilon_0\mathbf E + \varepsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E = \varepsilon\mathbf E, \qquad \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1+\chi_e), \qquad \varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e \]

Substituting \(\chi_e\) gives a frequency-dependent relative permittivity. This is called the Lorentz dielectric function (Lorentz dielectric function):

\[ \boxed{\;\varepsilon_r(\omega) = 1 + f\,\frac{\omega_0^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega}\;} \]

The terms one by one: \(f\) is the oscillator strength, depending on the material and the natural frequency. \(\omega_0\) is the natural frequency, corresponding to the energy difference of a transition from the valence band to the conduction band. \(\omega\) is the frequency of the wave sent in. \(\gamma\) is the damping rate.

Scaled parameters scaled parameters

All three parameters are frequencies, so they are all divided by \(\omega_0\):

\[ \bar\omega = \frac{\omega}{\omega_0}, \quad \bar\gamma = \frac{\gamma}{\omega_0} \;\Rightarrow\; \varepsilon_r(\bar\omega) = 1 + \frac{f}{1-\bar\omega^2 - i\bar\gamma\bar\omega} \]

The model thus comes down to two numbers: \(f\) and \(\bar\gamma\). The lecturer's warning: the scaled \(\bar\gamma\) and the real \(\gamma\) are different things, do not mix them up.

not in the lecture Writing the real and imaginary parts separately is useful in the exam, \(\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon_1 + i\varepsilon_2\):

\[ \varepsilon_1 = 1 + \frac{f\,(1-\bar\omega^2)}{(1-\bar\omega^2)^2 + \bar\gamma^2\bar\omega^2}, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = \frac{f\,\bar\gamma\bar\omega}{(1-\bar\omega^2)^2 + \bar\gamma^2\bar\omega^2} \]

The numbers for silicon

The values on the board: \(\gamma \sim 10^{13}\)–\(10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\omega_0 \sim 3\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The result is \(\bar\gamma \ll 1\), so the resonance is very sharp.

Mistake on the board · calculation

\(10^{13}/(3\times10^{15}) \approx 3\times10^{-3}\) and \(10^{14}/(3\times10^{15}) \approx 3\times10^{-2}\). The board says \(\bar\gamma \sim 10^{-1}\)–\(10^{-2}\). The conclusion does not change, but do not memorize the number.

Three regimes

ω ≪ ω₀, low frequency. The electron follows the field easily and the phase lag is near zero (in phase). \(\varepsilon_r \approx 1 + f\), real and nearly constant, \(\varepsilon_2 \approx 0\). The material is transparent (transparent) and only slows the light. Glass being transparent to visible light is this regime. The increase of \(\varepsilon_1\) with frequency in this region is called normal dispersion (normal dispersion).

ω ≈ ω₀, resonance. This was the question the lecturer asked in class. The real part of the denominator vanishes and only \(-i\gamma\omega\) remains. The only thing limiting the amplitude is \(\gamma\), and the phase lag is exactly 90°. The electron oscillates with the largest amplitude (maximum amplitude), energy is transferred most efficiently, and the imaginary part dominates:

\[ \varepsilon_r(\omega_0) = 1 + \frac{f}{-i\bar\gamma} = 1 + i\,\frac{f}{\bar\gamma} \]

So at resonance \(\varepsilon_1\) is exactly 1, \(\varepsilon_2 = f/\bar\gamma\). Since \(\bar\gamma\) is small, the peak is high and narrow, with a width of about \(\bar\gamma\). As the collision rate grows the line widens and lowers. The fall of \(\varepsilon_1\) with frequency near resonance is called anomalous dispersion (anomalous dispersion).

ω ≫ ω₀, high frequency. The electron cannot keep up and oscillates 180° out of phase with the field (out of phase). \(\chi_e\) is negative, and \(\varepsilon_r\) approaches 1 from below.

Between \(\omega_0\) and about \(\omega_0\sqrt{1+f}\), \(\varepsilon_1 < 0\). In this band the wave cannot enter the material and the reflection is high (high reflectivity). This is the shaded area in the graph.

Why absorption is in the imaginary part

A 90° lag means that the velocity of the electron is in phase with the field. When force and velocity point the same way, the field does positive work at every moment, and this energy is dissipated through \(\gamma\). The average power over a period comes only from the component of \(x\) shifted by 90° relative to the field, and that component is \(\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_e = \varepsilon_2\). The calculation is in the last question of Appendix C.

not in the lecture The step to measured quantities is made with the complex refractive index (complex refractive index, extinction coefficient, absorption coefficient):

\[ \tilde n = n + i\kappa = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}, \qquad \varepsilon_1 = n^2 - \kappa^2, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = 2n\kappa, \qquad \alpha = \frac{2\omega\kappa}{c} \]

Real materials band structure

recording 1:39, 1:47

The function so far corresponds to a single natural frequency. In a real material electrons can move to many states. The lecturer showed this with a simple band diagram: the vertical axis is energy, the horizontal axis is the wave vector \(k\) (wave vector), with symmetry points such as \(\Gamma\) and \(M\) at the bottom (high-symmetry points).

The valence bands are filled (filled valence bands), so no transition can happen within a valence band. Above are the conduction bands (conduction bands), and between them the forbidden region, the band gap (band gap, energy gap). Insulators have no electrons in the conduction band to carry charge, which is why they insulate.

Each transition from the valence to the conduction band has its own natural frequency (interband transition), so in a real material we cannot speak of a single frequency. The dielectric function is the sum over all transitions, each with its own oscillator strength:

\[ \varepsilon_r(\omega) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{n} f_j\,\frac{\omega_j^2}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma_j\omega} \]
Missing on the board · γ

The sum on the board has a single \(\gamma\). Each transition should have its own damping \(\gamma_j\).

not in the lecture Why are the arrows vertical? The wave vector of a photon is very small compared to the size of the Brillouin zone, so \(k\) hardly changes in a transition (vertical (direct) transitions).

The practical result: transparency

If the photon energy is below \(E_g\), all the terms stay in the first regime, \(\varepsilon_2 \approx 0\), and the material is transparent. Above it the transitions open up and absorption begins. For silicon:

\[ \lambda_g = \frac{hc}{E_g} \approx \frac{1240\ \text{nm·eV}}{1.12\ \text{eV}} \approx 1.1\ \mu\text{m} \]

This is why silicon is transparent above 1.1 µm and is used as an IR window and lens material. What separates an insulator from a semiconductor is the size of \(E_g\); the model is the same for both.

From the wave equation to Helmholtz Helmholtz equation

The notebook shows that the lecture opened with this derivation before the recording started. In a source-free medium (\(\rho = 0\), \(J = 0\), \(P = 0\), \(M = 0\)) the wave equation (wave equation):

\[ \nabla^2\mathbf E - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2} = 0 \]

The fields are written in harmonic form, \(\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t) = \mathrm{Re}\{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)e^{-i\omega t}\}\). From the Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell equations:

\[ \nabla\times\mathbf E = i\omega\mathbf B, \quad \nabla\times\mathbf B = -i\omega\mu_0\varepsilon_0\mathbf E \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla^2\mathbf E + k^2\mathbf E = 0, \quad k = \frac{\omega}{c} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \]

\(k\) is the wave number (wave number), \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\). The same equation holds for \(\mathbf B\). The note in the notebook: the equation is time independent, works in frequency space and is solved as an eigenvalue problem with the finite element method (finite element method, FEM).

Why the magnetic field is not in the equation

It came up at the end of the lecture: a magnetic field does no work on a charge. Since the force is perpendicular to the velocity:

\[ \mathbf F = q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v = 0 \]

The electric field transfers the energy. not in the lecture Also, in a plane wave \(|\mathbf B| = |\mathbf E|/c\), so the ratio of the magnetic force to the electric force is of order \(v/c\), which is very small for a bound electron. These are the two reasons the equation of motion has only \(qE\).

The tunneling question quantum tunneling

While the band diagram was being drawn, a student asked whether the electron “tunnels” across the band gap. The lecturer's answer: that is a separate phenomenon. In tunneling an electron passes to the other side of a potential barrier (potential barrier), and the wave function comes into play. For example, if two pieces are brought within a few nanometers, an electron can pass from one to the other. Crossing the band gap, on the other hand, happens by absorbing a photon.

S1

Show that \(f = Nq^2/(Vm\varepsilon_0\omega_0^2)\) is unitless. The \(Nq^2/(Vm\varepsilon_0)\) in the numerator is the square of which frequency?

warm-up
Answer
\[ \frac{\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2}}{\mathrm{kg}\cdot\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}} = \frac{\mathrm N}{\mathrm{kg\cdot m}} = \mathrm{s^{-2}} \]

This is \(\omega_p^2\), the square of the plasma frequency. Divided by \(\omega_0^2\) it is unitless: \(f = (\omega_p/\omega_0)^2\).

S2

Why can we drop the factor \(e^{ikz}\) in the equation of motion? Justify with numbers.

warm-up
Answer

The electron's region of oscillation is \(x \sim 0.1\) nm, and in visible light \(\lambda \sim 500\) nm. \(kx = 2\pi x/\lambda \approx 1.3\times10^{-3}\), so \(e^{ikx} \approx 1 + 0.0013i \approx 1\). The field is constant in space in the region the electron sees: the dipole approximation.

S3

For \(\omega \ll \omega_0\), \(\omega = \omega_0\) and \(\omega \gg \omega_0\), what is the phase difference between the displacement of the electron and the driving force \(qE\)?

medium
Answer

\(\tan\varphi = \gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\). At low frequency the denominator is positive and large, \(\varphi \to 0\). At resonance the denominator is zero, \(\varphi = 90°\). At high frequency the denominator is negative and large, \(\varphi \to 180°\).

S4

At exact resonance, find the real and imaginary parts of \(\varepsilon_r\) in terms of \(f\) and \(\bar\gamma\).

medium
Answer
\[ \bar\omega = 1:\quad \varepsilon_r = 1 + \frac{f}{-i\bar\gamma} = 1 + i\,\frac{f}{\bar\gamma} \]

\(\varepsilon_1 = 1\), \(\varepsilon_2 = f/\bar\gamma\). In the lecture example (\(f = 1\), \(\bar\gamma = 0.1\)) \(\varepsilon_2 = 10\).

S5

In the limit \(\gamma \to 0\), at what frequency is \(\varepsilon_1\) zero? What happens to the wave between this frequency and \(\omega_0\)?

hard
Answer
\[ 1 + \frac{f}{1-\bar\omega^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \bar\omega^2 = 1 + f \;\Rightarrow\; \omega = \omega_0\sqrt{1+f} \]

In the band between them \(\varepsilon_r\) is negative and real, and \(\tilde n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\) is purely imaginary. The wave cannot enter the material, decays exponentially inside and is almost completely reflected (\(R \to 1\)).

S6

Calculate the radiation damping for \(\omega_0 = 3\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and compare with the lecturer's \(\gamma\) for silicon. Which mechanism dominates?

hard
Answer
\[ \tau_0 = \frac{e^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 m c^3} \approx 6.3\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm s, \qquad \gamma_{\text{rad}} = \tau_0\,\omega_0^2 \approx 5.6\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

In silicon \(\gamma \sim 10^{13}\)–\(10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the radiation contribution is in the ratio \(10^{-5}\)–\(10^{-6}\). What dominates is phonon and electron scattering.

S7

The static relative permittivity of silicon is 11.7. What is \(f\) in the single-oscillator model? If you take \(N/V = 2\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), what is \(\omega_0\) in eV?

hard
Answer
\[ f = \varepsilon_r(0) - 1 \approx 10.7, \qquad \omega_p \approx 2.5\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{\omega_p}{\sqrt f} \approx 7.7\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

\(\hbar\omega_0 \approx 5.1\) eV. The average band gap of silicon (the Penn gap, Penn gap) is about 4.8 eV, so the single-oscillator model gives a rough but reasonable result. The \(\omega_0 = 3\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (≈ 2 eV) on the board is low for this material.

S8

Show that the average power the field delivers to a unit volume in steady state is proportional to \(\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_e\).

hard
Answer

The period average of the product of two harmonic quantities is \(\langle \mathrm{Re}(Ae^{-i\omega t})\,\mathrm{Re}(Be^{-i\omega t})\rangle = \tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(AB^*)\). The amplitude of the force is \(qE_0\) and that of the velocity \(-i\omega\tilde x\):

\[ \langle F v\rangle = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[qE_0\,(-i\omega\tilde x)^*\right] = \tfrac12\,\omega\,qE_0\,\mathrm{Im}\,\tilde x \]

Since \(q\tilde x = (V/N)\,\varepsilon_0\chi_eE_0\), multiplying by \(N/V\) gives, per unit volume:

\[ \langle \mathcal P\rangle_V = \tfrac12\,\omega\,\varepsilon_0\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_e\,E_0^2 = \tfrac12\,\omega\,\varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_2\,E_0^2 \]

The real part delivers zero power on average. Only \(\varepsilon_2\) determines the absorption.
