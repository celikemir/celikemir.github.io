Two speeds in a guide, two kinds of cladding

Last week we found the TE modes and the cutoff frequency of a rectangular metal pipe. This week we calculated how fast the wave above cutoff travels: the crests move faster than c, the signal slower than c, and the product of the two speeds is exactly \(c^2\). Then we looked at which modes really exist (no TE₀₀, TM₀₁ or TM₁₀, and no TEM in an empty pipe). In the last part of the lecture we compared metal cladding with dielectric cladding: one squeezes light into nanometers but kills it within micrometers, the other carries it for kilometers but cannot go below about half a wavelength.

Step 1Board

The summary on the board: TE modes and cutoff

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe lecture gathered the end of last week on a single board: the TE mode \(B_z=B_0\cos(m\pi x/a)\cos(n\pi y/b)\) in a rectangular pipe, the dispersion relation of the guide \(c^2k^2=\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\), and no propagating wave below cutoff.

The top right corner is the starting point. Right at a metal wall, the component of the electric field parallel to the wall and the component of the magnetic field perpendicular to the wall are zero: on the board \(E_t=0\) (t for tangential; the \(E_\parallel\) of week 7) and \(B_\perp=0\). The wave travels along z, \(\vec k=k\hat z\), and all the fields carry the factor \(e^{ikz}\). In TE, \(E_z=0\), and the unknown to solve for is \(B_z\). Its wall condition is on the derivative rather than the value: at x = 0, \(\partial B_z/\partial x=0\). We saw why it is a derivative in Step 10 of week 7: the condition \(B_\perp=0\) goes through the formulas for the transverse fields to the derivative of \(B_z\).

The red \(X(x)Y(y)\) in the middle is separation of variables. The Helmholtz equation splits into two \(f''=-\kappa^2f\) equations, and the separation constants build the relation at the very top of the board:

\[ k_x^2+k_y^2+k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\Big(\frac{m\pi}{a}\Big)^2-\Big(\frac{n\pi}{b}\Big)^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&k_x^2+k_y^2+k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\\&k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\Big(\frac{m\pi}{a}\Big)^2-\Big(\frac{n\pi}{b}\Big)^2\end{aligned} \]

The wall conditions gave \(k_x=m\pi/a\), \(k_y=n\pi/b\); m and n are integers (“m, n are integers”). The cosines come from here too. Each pair (m, n) is a mode.

Giving a name to the two terms under the wavy line brings out the cutoff frequency:

\[ \frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{c^2}=\Big(\frac{m\pi}{a}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{n\pi}{b}\Big)^2,\qquad k^2=\frac{1}{c^2}\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big) \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{c^2}&=\Big(\frac{m\pi}{a}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{n\pi}{b}\Big)^2\\k^2&=\frac{1}{c^2}\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big)\end{aligned} \]

The red lines at the bottom state the result: if \(\omega\lt\omega_{mn}\), then \(k^2\lt0\), k is purely imaginary, and \(e^{-\alpha z}\) takes the place of \(e^{ikz}\). “Only \(\omega\gt\omega_{mn}\) can propagate.” The arrow and mark next to TE also recall that E is perpendicular to z, that is, \(E_z=0\).

This week we asked three questions on top of this table. How fast does the wave above cutoff travel (Part B)? Which (m, n) pairs are really modes (Part C)? How does a real, lossy metal spoil this picture and what is used instead (Part D)?

High school example

The diagonal of a box. The diagonal of a box with sides 1, 2 and 2 is \(\sqrt{1+4+4}=3\). The length of the wave vector is such a diagonal, fixed at \(\omega/c\). The more you “spend” on oscillating from wall to wall in the cross section (\(k_x\), \(k_y\)), the less remains for the z direction. As the frequency drops the diagonal shortens, and at some point nothing is left for z; that point is the cutoff frequency.

Step 2Board

TE₁₀: the lowest cutoff

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentencePutting m = 1, n = 0 gives \(\omega_{10}=c\pi/a\); if you know the wide side of the cross section you can compute the cutoff frequency right away, and for a > b this is the lowest cutoff frequency of the guide.

The first minute of the lecture: “put 1 here and 0 here.” In the definition of \(\omega_{mn}\), m = 1, n = 0:

\[ \frac{\omega_{10}^2}{c^2}=\Big(\frac{\pi}{a}\Big)^2+0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \omega_{10}=\frac{c\pi}{a} \]

The lecturer made a note while taking the square root: a is a positive number, so there is no need to take the negative root. Frequency here is a magnitude; \(-c\pi/a\) carries no new information.

In hertz \(f_{10}=\omega_{10}/2\pi=c/2a\). Read the other way, the cutoff wavelength is \(\lambda_c=c/f_{10}=2a\): the free-space wavelength must be shorter than twice the wide side, so at least half a wave must fit in the cross section. In the lecturer's words, if you know the dimensions of a rectangular guide you can easily compute the cutoff frequency; \(\omega_{mn}\) is just a number, a property of the geometry of the guide.

Why the lowest? \(\omega_{mn}^2\propto m^2/a^2+n^2/b^2\). For a > b, the smallest nonzero value comes from m = 1, n = 0. The cutoff of \(\mathrm{TE}_{01}\) is \(c\pi/b\), which is larger; m = n = 0 is not a mode at all (Step 8). That is why \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\) is called the dominant mode. In the standard WR-90 guide a = 22.86 mm and \(f_{10}\approx6.56\) GHz.

After the break a classmate asked: “Which mode will we use in our work?” The lecturer's answer: the lowest modes, because their cutoff frequencies are low. In practice a guide is run in the frequency range where only \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\) propagates; this is the single-mode operation in the last step of week 7.

High school example

A jump rope. Two people turn a rope. The easiest motion is a single arc between the two hands: the rope bulges in the middle with its ends in the hands. This is \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\): a single half wave along the wide side, the lowest frequency. For a two-arc turn with a still middle (\(\mathrm{TE}_{20}\)) you must turn the rope twice as fast. If you shorten the rope you must turn it faster even for the same single arc; when the pipe narrows, the cutoff rises.

Two speeds in a guide

Two speeds come from the dispersion relation: the speed of the crests is greater than c and the speed of the packet less than c. Then we discuss why this is not a contradiction, and the picture of the wave zigzagging between the walls.

Step 3Notebook 1

Phase velocity exceeds c

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe phase velocity is the speed of the crests of a single-frequency wave, \(v_p=\omega/k\); in a guide \(v_p=c/\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}\), and it is greater than c for every propagating mode.

The lecturer's definition: phase velocity is the speed of a monochromatic, single-frequency wave. In \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\), following a crest means keeping the phase constant:

\[ kz-\omega t=\text{constant}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z=\frac{\omega}{k}\,t+\text{constant}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_p=\frac{\omega}{k} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&kz-\omega t=\text{constant}\\&\Longrightarrow\ z=\frac{\omega}{k}\,t+\text{constant}\\&\Longrightarrow\ v_p=\frac{\omega}{k}\end{aligned} \]

In the guide we take k from the dispersion relation. The lecturer pulled \(\omega^2\) out of the parenthesis:

\[ k^2=\frac{1}{c^2}\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big)=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\Big(1-\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{\omega^2}\Big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{\omega^2}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}k^2&=\frac{1}{c^2}\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big)=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\Big(1-\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{\omega^2}\Big)\\k&=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{\omega^2}}\end{aligned} \]

Dividing ω by this:

\[ v_p=\frac{\omega}{k}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}} \]

Why is it greater than c? The argument was built step by step. Below cutoff the wave does not propagate, so we care only about \(\omega\gt\omega_{mn}\). Then \(\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2\) is positive and less than 1; \(1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2\) is between 0 and 1, and so is its square root. Dividing c by a number less than 1 gives a result greater than c. The note in the notebook: “this is larger than c”.

Both extremes were discussed in class. As ω approaches cutoff from above, the denominator goes to zero and \(v_p\to\infty\). As ω gets very large, \(\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2\to0\) and \(v_p\to c\): at high frequency the wave barely feels the walls and travels as in free space.

One more thing: \(v_p\) depends on frequency. In free space every frequency travels at c; in a guide the crests of each frequency travel at a different speed. This is called dispersion, and here it comes from the geometry rather than a material: the pipe is empty yet still dispersive.

High school example

The “Mexican wave” in a stadium. Everyone just stands up and sits down in place, but the wave races around the stands. If neighbors rise with less delay the wave runs even faster, faster than the world's fastest runner if you like. Yet nobody goes anywhere and nothing is carried; all that runs is a pattern. Phase velocity is the speed of such a pattern.

Step 4Notebook 1

Group velocity is below c

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe group velocity is \(v_g=d\omega/dk\); it is easier to take the derivative the other way around, through \(dk/d\omega\), and the result is \(v_g=c\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}\), always less than c.

The group velocity is the speed of the envelope of a wave packet made of many frequencies; its definition is \(v_g=d\omega/dk\). We have \(k(\omega)\) rather than \(\omega(k)\), so the lecturer took the derivative the other way around:

\[ v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{1}{dk/d\omega} \]

\(k=\frac1c(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2)^{1/2}\). The chain rule: the derivative of the outer power times the derivative of the inner part, \(2\omega\).

\[ \frac{dk}{d\omega}=\frac1c\cdot\frac12\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big)^{-1/2}\cdot2\omega=\frac1c\,\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2}}=\frac1c\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}} \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{dk}{d\omega}&=\frac1c\cdot\frac12\big(\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\big)^{-1/2}\cdot2\omega\\&=\frac1c\,\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2}}\\&=\frac1c\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}}\end{aligned} \]

In the last step we divided the numerator and denominator by ω; ω went inside the root as \(\omega^2\). The lecturer showed the 2's canceling and left the rest with “do it yourselves at home”. Take the inverse:

\[ v_g=c\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_{mn}^2}{\omega^2}}\lt c \]

The root is less than 1, and multiplying c by it gives a result less than c. The lecturer: as it should be; because of relativity no signal or energy transfer can exceed the speed of light. The extremes are the inverse of \(v_p\): near cutoff \(v_g\to0\), the wave nearly stops (we will see why in Step 7); at high frequency \(v_g\to c\).

Predict first: at the frequency where \(v_g\) is half of c, how many c is \(v_p\)? Then find the point \(v_g=0.5c\) with the slider.

Red is \(v_p/c\), blue \(v_g/c\), and the dashed line c. Just above cutoff \(v_p\) goes off the graph.

Board note

The intermediate line in the notebook says \(\frac1c\,\frac{\omega}{\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2}\); the denominator should be inside the square root: \(\frac1c\,\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2}}\). The result in the circle is correct; only the intermediate line slipped.

A shortcut: the derivative of the dispersion relation (not in the lecture)

Take the derivative of both sides of \(\omega^2=\omega_{mn}^2+c^2k^2\): \(2\omega\,d\omega=2c^2k\,dk\). Without opening any square root:

\[ v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{c^2k}{\omega} \]

Substituting k gives the same result. This form reduces the next step to one line.

Runners sometimes state their pace as “I run a kilometer in 5 minutes”. This is dt/dx; the speed is its inverse, 1/5 km/min = 12 km/h. The lecturer did the same: \(dk/d\omega\) was easy to find, and \(d\omega/dk\) is its inverse.

Step 5Notebook 1

The product is exactly \(c^2\)

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceMultiplying the two formulas cancels the roots: \(v_pv_g=c^2\). However much one exceeds c, the other falls below it by the same factor; in free space both are exactly c.

The lecturer said “just multiply”:

\[ v_p\,v_g=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}}\cdot c\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}=c^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}v_p\,v_g&=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}}\cdot c\sqrt{1-\omega_{mn}^2/\omega^2}\\&=c^2\end{aligned} \]

The roots cancel and a result independent of frequency, mode and size remains. One is greater than c and the other less; c is their geometric mean, \(c=\sqrt{v_pv_g}\).

Then the lecturer asked: in free space? Without walls \(\omega_{mn}=0\) and \(v_p=v_g=c\). The crests and the packet travel at the same speed, with no dispersion.

With the shortcut of Step 4 this is one line: \(v_p=\omega/k\), \(v_g=c^2k/\omega\), and multiplying gives \(c^2\). All we used is the form of the dispersion relation, \(\omega^2=\omega_{mn}^2+c^2k^2\). This form is the same for TE and TM, and for a metal pipe of circular cross section; only the value of \(\omega_{mn}\) changes. So \(v_pv_g=c^2\) holds in every hollow metal guide. If the pipe is filled with a single homogeneous material, the speed of light in the material, c/n, takes the place of c.

Light in a guide is like a massive particle (not in the lecture, my addition)

Multiply the dispersion relation by \(\hbar^2\), and write \(E=\hbar\omega\) and \(p=\hbar k\):

\[ (\hbar\omega)^2=(\hbar\omega_{mn})^2+(\hbar k)^2c^2\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad E^2=(mc^2)^2+(pc)^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&(\hbar\omega)^2=(\hbar\omega_{mn})^2+(\hbar k)^2c^2\\&\longleftrightarrow\ E^2=(mc^2)^2+(pc)^2\end{aligned} \]

This is exactly the relativistic energy-momentum relation of a massive particle, with effective mass \(m=\hbar\omega_{mn}/c^2\). The speed of a massive particle is \(v=pc^2/E=c^2k/\omega\): this is exactly \(v_g\). The phase velocity of its de Broglie wave is \(E/p=c^2/v\), greater than c. This is the same structure as the phase velocity, group velocity and wave-particle duality of week 1: the crests of the matter wave are faster than c, the particle itself slower than c, and their product is \(c^2\). The cutoff frequency plays the role of mass here. For the \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\) of WR-90, \(m\approx4.8\times10^{-41}\) kg.

Step 6spoken

The phase races, the signal cannot

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceA single-frequency wave is a pattern with no beginning or end that carries nothing; a signal can be sent only by mixing frequencies, with a packet, and the envelope of the packet travels at \(v_g\), below c. The crests enter the envelope from behind and vanish at the front.

The lecturer's question: look at the individual phases; whatever the frequency, their phase velocity is greater than c. But their group, the wave packet, travels slower than c. How can this be?

The answers from the class pointed the right way: “the phase is not a physical thing, it is like an imaginary reference” and “the signal travels with the envelope”. Let us make this precise.

A single-frequency wave \(e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\) is infinitely long and the same everywhere. All the crests are identical; you cannot mark one and send news across, because nothing tells one crest from another. To put information in you must change the wave: turn it on and off, raise and lower the amplitude. A changing wave is no longer a single frequency but, by Fourier, a band of frequencies. The speed of that change, the envelope, is \(d\omega/dk\).

You can see what happens inside the packet in the plot below. A crest is marked with a red dot. Since \(v_p\gt v_g\), the crest is faster than the envelope: it enters from the back of the envelope and moves forward, and on reaching the front edge its amplitude dies away and it disappears, while new ones come in from behind. The dashed gray line is a light signal that set off at the same moment: the crest passes it, the envelope cannot.

Predict first: as time advances, does the marked crest stay inside the envelope? Then drag the time slider to the right or press “Play”.

Units c = 1 and \(\omega_{mn}=1\). The blue dot is the peak of the envelope (\(v_g\)), the red dot the marked phase crest (\(v_p\)), and the dashed gray line light (c). The spreading of the packet in time is neglected in this plot.

Energy also travels with the envelope: in a lossless guide the ratio of the carried power to the energy per unit length comes out as exactly \(v_g\) (not in the lecture; it can be shown with the power flow and energy density in the guide). In short, what exceeds c is only a pattern; energy and information stay below c.

High school example

Scissors. As the two blades close, the point where they cross slides toward the tips, and if the angle between the blades is small this point moves much faster than the blades themselves. Imagine scissors kilometers long: the crossing point could even run faster than light. But nothing is carried at that point; at each moment a different piece of metal is crossing. A phase crest is such a crossing point.

Step 7spoken

The zigzag path: a mode is two plane waves

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceThe \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\) mode is the sum of two plane waves bouncing between the walls at an angle θ; each travels at c, but the advance along z is \(v_g=c\cos\theta\) and the slide of the crests along z is \(v_p=c/\cos\theta\).

In the middle of the discussion of the speeds the lecturer gave a picture: these waves also have components in the x and y directions. They do not propagate in those directions, but they have components, so they do not follow a straight path but a zigzag one. Let us verify this picture by calculation.

In \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\), \(B_z\propto\cos(\pi x/a)\,e^{ikz}\) and the cosine is the average of two exponentials. The decomposition on the left says this: the mode is two plane waves with wave vectors \((\pm\pi/a,\,0,\,k)\), one slanted up and one down. The length of each wave vector is:

\[ \sqrt{(\pi/a)^2+k^2}=\sqrt{\omega_{10}^2/c^2+k^2}=\frac{\omega}{c} \]

This is the dispersion relation itself. So each plane wave travels at exactly c, like light in free space; all they do in the guide is bounce from wall to wall. The angle they make with the z axis:

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{k}{\omega/c}=\frac{ck}{\omega}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_{10}^2}{\omega^2}} \]

The two speeds come out of this by themselves. Something moving at c on a slant advances along z at \(c\cos\theta\): this is \(v_g\). The crest lines stand slanted, and the point where a crest meets the wall slides along z at \(c/\cos\theta\): this is \(v_p\). Their product is \(c^2\), as in Step 5.

At cutoff \(\cos\theta=0\), θ = 90°: the ray bounces perpendicular from wall to wall and does not advance at all. \(v_g=0\), \(v_p=\infty\). Below cutoff there is no such angle and the wave decays. At very high frequency θ → 0; the ray is nearly parallel to the axis and both speeds approach c. This is the picture of the sentence in Step 4, “near cutoff the wave nearly stops”.

Predict first: as you bring the frequency closer to cutoff, what happens to the angle of the ray? Then drag the slider to the left.

The vertical axis is the cross section (width a), the horizontal axis z along the guide. The dark ray is the plane wave slanted up and the light ray the one slanted down. The starting value 1.525 is about 10 GHz in WR-90.

A billiard ball moves along the long side of the table, bouncing off the rails. The ball always moves at the same speed, but the time to reach the far end depends on the angle: if it hits the rails almost perpendicularly it hardly goes forward, and if almost parallel its whole speed goes forward. The ball's speed is c and its advance along the long side is \(v_g=c\cos\theta\).

Step 8Notebook 1

TE₀₀: a constant field, no wave

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceFor m = n = 0, \(B_z=B_0\) is constant in the cross section; all the transverse fields come from the derivative of \(B_z\), so they vanish, and in TE \(E_z\) is zero too. What remains is a constant magnetic field, which is not an electromagnetic wave.

The first topic after the break (“2nd lecture” in the notebook). The lecturer put m = n = 0 into the formula for \(B_z\): \(\cos0=1\), \(\cos0=1\), leaving \(B_z=B_0\).

Then we were asked to look at the other components. The formulas from Step 3 of week 7:

\[ E_x=\frac{i\big(k\,\partial_xE_z+\omega\,\partial_yB_z\big)}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2},\qquad E_y=\frac{i\big(k\,\partial_yE_z-\omega\,\partial_xB_z\big)}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2},\ \dots \]
\[ \begin{aligned}E_x&=\frac{i\big(k\,\partial_xE_z+\omega\,\partial_yB_z\big)}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2}\\E_y&=\frac{i\big(k\,\partial_yE_z-\omega\,\partial_xB_z\big)}{\omega^2/c^2-k^2},\ \dots\end{aligned} \]

All four are defined by the derivatives of \(E_z\) and \(B_z\), not by the fields themselves. The derivative of a constant is zero, and in TE \(E_z=0\) too. The result: \(E_x=E_y=B_x=B_y=0\) and \(E_z=0\). All we have is a constant \(B_z\).

The lecturer: this is not an electromagnetic wave, only a constant B; perhaps it corresponds to DC. An electromagnetic wave needs E and B that give rise to each other and vary in time and space; here there is no E. In week 7 we reached the same result with \(\nabla\cdot\vec B=0\): \(ikB_z=0\), so either k = 0 (no propagating wave) or \(B_z=0\). Both routes lead to the same place: a hollow metal pipe has no 00 mode, for TE or for TM.

The word “DC” is a good clue. An empty metal pipe cannot carry direct current: it is a single conductor, with no separate paths for going and returning. The mode with the lowest cutoff is \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\), and its cutoff is above zero. A mode reaching down to zero frequency needs two separate conductors: the coaxial cable and TEM in Step 10.

TM modes: the derivation to do at home

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn TM, \(B_z=0\) and the unknown is \(E_z\); since \(E_z\) itself vanishes at the wall, a sine comes out instead of a cosine, \(E_z=E_0\sin(m\pi x/a)\sin(n\pi y/b)\). m and n are at least 1, and the cutoff frequency formula is the same as for TE.

The lecturer did not solve the TM case in class: apply the same procedure, separation of variables; the only difference is the boundary conditions, and applying them gives a different kind of solution for the electric field, with sines rather than cosines for the z components. “Do it at home. Seriously.” Here I do it from start to finish; you should be able to write the skeleton at the end with the book closed.

1. What is zero, what to solve for. In TM, \(B_z=0\); the unknown is \(E_z\). The Helmholtz equation from week 7:

\[ \Big[\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\gamma^2\Big]E_z=0,\qquad \gamma^2\equiv\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-k^2 \]

2. Separate. Put in \(E_z=X(x)\,Y(y)\) and divide by XY:

\[ \frac{X''}{X}+\frac{Y''}{Y}=-\gamma^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X''=-k_x^2X,\quad Y''=-k_y^2Y,\quad k_x^2+k_y^2=\gamma^2 \]
\[ \begin{aligned}&\frac{X''}{X}+\frac{Y''}{Y}=-\gamma^2\\&X''=-k_x^2X,\quad Y''=-k_y^2Y\\&k_x^2+k_y^2=\gamma^2\end{aligned} \]

If the sum of two terms, one depending only on x and the other only on y, is constant, each is separately constant.

3. Boundary condition. \(E_z\) is along z and tangent to all four walls. At a metal wall \(E_\parallel=0\), so directly:

\[ E_z=0\qquad x=0,\ x=a,\ y=0,\ y=b\ \text{(the four walls)} \]

The value itself in place of the derivative condition of TE. The difference is exactly here.

4. Solve. \(X=A\sin k_xx+B\cos k_xx\). \(X(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0\). \(X(a)=0\Rightarrow\sin k_xa=0\Rightarrow k_x=m\pi/a\). The same for y: \(k_y=n\pi/b\).

\[ E_z=E_0\sin\Big(\frac{m\pi x}{a}\Big)\sin\Big(\frac{n\pi y}{b}\Big)\,e^{i(kz-\omega t)} \]

5. Which (m, n)? If m = 0, \(\sin0=0\) and \(E_z\) is zero everywhere; the same for n = 0. \(B_z\) is already zero; with both zero only TEM would remain, and an empty pipe has no TEM (Step 10). In the lecturer's words: in TM, m or n cannot be zero, because then \(E_z\) would be zero; \(B_z\) is also zero, and the two cannot both be zero while satisfying the boundary conditions. The result: there is no \(\mathrm{TM}_{01}\) or \(\mathrm{TM}_{10}\), and the smallest TM mode is \(\mathrm{TM}_{11}\).

6. Cutoff. \(\gamma^2=(m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2=\omega_{mn}^2/c^2\). The same formula as TE, the same dispersion relation \(c^2k^2=\omega^2-\omega_{mn}^2\); all the speeds of Part B hold for TM too. For every pair with m, n ≥ 1, \(\mathrm{TE}_{mn}\) and \(\mathrm{TM}_{mn}\) have the same cutoff; the two are degenerate.

7. Transverse fields. Put \(B_z=0\) into the formulas of week 7; the denominator is \(\gamma^2=\omega_{mn}^2/c^2\):

\[ E_x=\frac{ik}{\gamma^2}\,\partial_xE_z,\quad E_y=\frac{ik}{\gamma^2}\,\partial_yE_z,\quad B_x=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2\gamma^2}\,\partial_yE_z,\quad B_y=\frac{i\omega}{c^2\gamma^2}\,\partial_xE_z \]
\[ \begin{aligned}E_x&=\frac{ik}{\gamma^2}\,\partial_xE_z,&E_y&=\frac{ik}{\gamma^2}\,\partial_yE_z\\B_x&=-\frac{i\omega}{c^2\gamma^2}\,\partial_yE_z,&B_y&=\frac{i\omega}{c^2\gamma^2}\,\partial_xE_z\end{aligned} \]

Check: at the x = 0 wall the tangential E components are \(E_y\) and \(E_z\), and the perpendicular B component is \(B_x\). All three carry \(\sin(m\pi x/a)\) and vanish at x = 0 and x = a. At the y walls \(E_x\), \(E_z\) and \(B_y\) vanish through \(\sin(n\pi y/b)\). All the conditions are satisfied; I checked the four Maxwell equations symbolically with these fields. The transverse E points along the slope (gradient) of \(E_z\) in the cross section; these are the arrows in the plot below.

MHG, metallic hollow guide. We saw why an empty pipe has no TEM in Steps 6 and 7 of week 7. In TEM, \(\omega=ck\) and the transverse field is the gradient of a potential in the cross section; that potential is constant on the single-piece wall, obeys Laplace's equation in two dimensions and must stay constant inside too, so the field is zero. The lecturer's short version this week: \(E_z\) and \(B_z\) cannot both be zero while satisfying the boundary conditions.

A coaxial cable has two conductors: the inner wire and the outer shield. A potential difference can be placed between them and a field can live in the gap; TEM is possible, with zero cutoff. This is the “DC” connection of Step 8: a coaxial cable carries signals starting from zero frequency.

The lecturer added: in theory TEM is possible in a coaxial cable, but in reality the boundaries are very lossy, so the wave cannot go very far in a coax either; hence fiber and dielectric cladding. This sentence is true for optical frequencies. At radio frequencies coaxial cable carries signals for tens or hundreds of meters: antenna cables, cable TV. Loss grows with frequency; at optical frequencies a metal wall is far from ideal (Step 11).

Hybrid modes. A dielectric cladding does not impose as strict a condition as a metal: the field also leaks into the cladding (decaying), and the continuity conditions at the interface tie \(E_z\) and \(B_z\) together. If one is zero the other is usually forced to be zero too, so generally both are present. In a cylindrical fiber the rotationally symmetric modes (\(\mathrm{TE}_{0m}\), \(\mathrm{TM}_{0m}\)) are still pure TE and TM, and all the others are hybrid. The fundamental mode of a fiber is \(\mathrm{HE}_{11}\); it has no cutoff and propagates at every frequency.

Step 11Notebook 2

A real metal is lossy

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceAn ideal metal wall reflects a wave without loss; in a real metal the imaginary part of the refractive index dominates, the field goes a few tens of nanometers into the wall and decays, and at each reflection part of the energy goes to heat. At optical frequencies this condemns the metal guide to short distances.

Until now the walls were ideal conductors. The lecturer moved on to real metal: metals interact very strongly with electromagnetic waves, form a lossy medium, and have a very short skin depth. If you write \(e^{ikz}\) inside a metal, k is complex with a dominant imaginary part; the wave decays as soon as it enters the metal. This is the drawing in the notebook: two walls and small waves decaying inside them.

More precisely, the refractive index of a metal is \(\tilde n=n_R+i\kappa\), and in visible light \(\kappa\gg n_R\). The field decays inside the metal like \(e^{-2\pi\kappa d/\lambda}\); in the visible this means a few tens of nanometers. The skin depth of week 6 and the electron gas of week 5 are the physics behind this.

There is a subtlety. The imaginary part being dominant does not by itself mean loss: if \(\tilde n\) were purely imaginary the metal would be a perfect mirror; the field would go in a little, but all the energy would come back. What turns energy into heat is that \(n_R\) is not zero, that is, electron collisions. At normal incidence the reflectance is \(R=|(\tilde n-1)/(\tilde n+1)|^2\), and for real metals it is slightly below 1:

StatePower lost in one reflection
Copper, 10 GHz≈ %0,03
Silver, visible light≈ %1
Gold, 600 nm≈ %10

The numbers are rough values for normal incidence, by my calculation. The second factor shortening the distance is the number of bounces: as the guide gets smaller, there are more hits on the wall per unit length. In a guide a micrometer wide, light hits the wall roughly a thousand times in a millimeter; if 2% is lost at each hit, what remains after a millimeter is \(0.98^{1000}\approx2\times10^{-9}\). As the lecturer said, the inside of the guide (air) in theory absorbs nothing; the problem is the walls.

Watch the frequency. The sentence “metal cladding is very lossy” is for optical frequencies. At microwave frequencies metal is nearly ideal: in a standard copper WR-90 guide the loss at 10 GHz is about 0.1 dB per meter and the skin depth is 0.66 µm. That is why radars, satellite ground stations and particle accelerators use hollow metal pipes. When the same pipe shrinks to micrometers for visible light, the reflection gets worse and the number of bounces explodes.

At a barber's or in an elevator you see the image stretching to infinity, but the distant images get steadily dimmer, shift slightly toward green and vanish after a few dozen reflections. An ordinary mirror reflects about 90% of the light; \(0.9^{30}\approx0.04\). Light in a metal guide lives between two such mirrors.

Step 12Notebook 2

The mechanism of metal cladding: the surface plasmon

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceIn a metal-clad nanoguide what carries the light is not bouncing off the walls but the photon combining with the collective oscillation of the free electrons at the metal surface, the surface plasmon; this wave travels on the order of micrometers and dies out, but it can squeeze light below 10 nm.

The lecturer named the mechanism of metal cladding: the coupling of photons with surface plasmons, the interaction between them. The details will be explained later; we will see plasma modes and the plasma resonance.

What is a plasmon? The lecturer's definition: a collective excitation of electrons. A metal has free electrons; these electrons can move together and form collective excitations, called plasmons. This is the electron gas of week 5: when the whole electron cloud shifts a little relative to the ions, a restoring force appears and the cloud oscillates. The frequency of this oscillation is the plasma frequency \(\omega_p\); below it the dielectric constant of the metal is negative and the metal reflects light.

A surface plasmon is this oscillation stuck to the interface between a metal and a dielectric. Its field decays exponentially away from the surface in both directions and it travels along the surface. The mixed wave that appears when it combines with light is called a surface plasmon polariton. The answer to a question in class explains this: the photon and plasmon interact, but after interacting we treat them as a single system and study the photon-plasmon structure together.

Distance and confinement. The lecturer first said the propagation distance was “less than 10 nm”, then corrected it: on the order of micrometers. The “< 10 nm” in the notebook is the size to which light can be squeezed. This is the real strength of metal cladding: a dielectric guide cannot put light into a region smaller than roughly λ/2 (Step 13), while plasmonic structures with the right geometry can go below 10 nm. The price comes from the same place: the more you squeeze, the larger the share of the field inside the metal and the faster it dies. Confinement and distance oppose each other.

Uses. The lecturer listed short-distance jobs: nanoscale optical devices, plasmonic chips, SPR (surface plasmon resonance) sensors and biosensors. The idea of SPR: since the field of a surface plasmon clings to the surface, even a thin layer of molecules attached to the surface changes the refractive index there and shifts the angle or wavelength of the resonance. By measuring this shift you can follow molecules binding to the surface without using labels.

High school example

If you gently push a glass of water, all the water piles up on one side, comes back and sloshes for a while. It is not individual molecules but all the water oscillating together. A plasmon is such a collective sloshing of the electron “water” in a metal. A surface plasmon is a slosh running only along the shore, at the edge of the glass.

The lecturer said the choice for long-haul optical communication is dielectric cladding. The mechanism is total internal reflection (TIR): the refractive index of the core is larger than that of the cladding, \(n_{core}\gt n_{clad}\), and light arriving at the interface at a shallower angle than the critical angle is reflected one hundred percent.

This is exactly the difference from metal cladding. In total internal reflection there is no reflection tax, no share lost at each bounce as with a metal wall. What remains is the absorption and scattering of the glass itself; in modern telecom fiber about 0.2 dB per kilometer at 1550 nm. That is why light can travel tens of kilometers between amplifiers.

The diffraction limit. The lecturer: the measure of confinement in an optical fiber is the diffraction limit; you cannot squeeze light into a size smaller than λ/2. We can see this in this week's language. A wave fitting in a region of width w must make at least half a wave in the cross section, \(k_x\gtrsim\pi/w\). On the other hand, in a medium of index n, \(k_x^2+k_z^2=n^2\omega^2/c^2\), and for \(k_z\) to stay real, \(k_x\le n\omega/c\). Combine the two:

\[ \frac{\pi}{w}\lesssim\frac{n\omega}{c}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda/n}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w\gtrsim\frac{\lambda}{2n} \]

This is the same as the cutoff condition of \(\mathrm{TE}_{10}\) in Step 2, a > λ/2: the cutoff frequency is the form the diffraction limit takes in a metal pipe. For 1550 nm light, λ/2 = 775 nm in air and about 530 nm in silica (n ≈ 1.45). Metal can beat this limit because its dielectric constant is negative: a surface wave decaying toward both sides can form at the interface, and such a wave does not need to make half a wave in the cross section.

The table of the two claddings comes down to one sentence: dielectric cladding goes far but is wide, metal cladding is narrow but stays close.

High school example

Looking up from the bottom of a pool you see the outside only within a circle overhead; outside that circle the water surface is like a silver mirror. This is total internal reflection: light coming from water to air at a shallow angle turns back. The core of a fiber uses the same mirror everywhere, and the light loses nothing at each turn.

Step 14spoken

Modes are discrete, frequency continuous

This step is locked for now. To open it, first finish the questions of this step: .

In one sentenceWhat is discrete in a guide is the list of modes, the patterns in the cross section; frequency is not discrete. Each mode behaves like a high-pass filter passing all frequencies above its cutoff; a signal is a band of frequencies, and the band can travel in every mode whose cutoff is below it.

At the end of the lecture a classmate asked: why do these modes exist? The answer is familiar: if instead of leaving a wave free you force it to travel in a certain region, like vibrating a rope fixed at both ends, it can vibrate only in certain shapes. That is the logic of modes.

Right after that came a warning: the energy of these modes is not quantized. There is only a cutoff frequency; every frequency above it propagates and those below do not. Someone in class connected this to signal processing: signals above cutoff propagate, like a filter. Right, each mode is a high-pass filter. It is the same idea as the filters we use to suppress noise; here the passing side is above cutoff.

Why is the particle in a box different? A classmate recalled the particle in a box from quantum mechanics, where the energy is quantized. The difference is that a box is closed in all three directions; the particle has no direction to move freely, and the wave number is discrete in every direction. A guide is closed in only two directions: \(k_x\) and \(k_y\) are discrete, but the z direction is open and k is continuous; ω also depends continuously on k. Quantum mechanics has an exact counterpart: an electron confined in two directions and free in one, a quantum wire. Its energy is \(E=E_{mn}+\hbar^2k^2/2m\): a discrete base (subband) plus a continuous kinetic part. The \(\omega^2=\omega_{mn}^2+c^2k^2\) in a guide has the same structure; each mode is a subband. (This comparison is mine.)

A classmate went further: if we wrote the fields with a wave function, wouldn't it be quantized at the end of the day? The answer in class: there is such a language to describe the particle form of light, photons, and you can think of the fields like a wave function; but in this course think of light as a wave, with superposition and constructive and destructive interference. Let me add this: when working with photons, what is quantized is that the energy in a given mode at a given frequency comes in packets of \(\hbar\omega\). This does not make ω discrete; frequency stays continuous.

The last question: past the cutoff, will the modes appear one by one? The answer: do not think of frequency as discrete, think of it as a band. Each frequency in the band of the signal you send can travel in every mode whose cutoff is below that frequency. The shape of the source (antenna, probe) determines how much energy goes to which mode. If several modes are excited, each travels at a different group velocity and the signal spreads; that is why single-mode operation is preferred.
